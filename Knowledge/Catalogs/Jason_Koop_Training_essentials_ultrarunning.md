## L2 Workouts & Protocols

### [TEU-L2-001] Nine-step field blister patch and repair protocol

- Goal: fix minor/moderate blisters in the field and keep moving.
- When used: blister or hot spot during race.
- Structure:
1. Clean blister and surrounding skin (alcohol pad or disinfectant if available; otherwise still proceed). Small broken blisters are less infection-prone than large ones.
2. Puncture with sterile needle, sharp scissors or scalpel (author prefers scalpel to create a window for drainage). Drain without detaching the blister roof. With a needle (sterilized safety pins from a race bib work) make three to four holes, positioned so it keeps draining while running.
3. Squeeze out the fluid.
4. Clean and dry blister and surrounding skin; optionally a very small dab of lubricant on the blister roof so the patch does not stick to it when peeled.
5. Cut tape/bandage large enough to stick to surrounding undamaged skin. Toe blister: may wrap whole toe, and usually the adjacent toes too.
6. Apply tape adhesive (e.g. tincture of benzoin) around the blister.
7. Place tape from one edge to the other, avoiding folds/creases; if a fold or crease occurs, start over.
8. Lightly press to ensure full adhesion.
9. Socks on, lace up, run.
- Notes: practice at home if blister-prone (tape sticks to itself and fingers; sweaty, dirty, hurried race conditions). Simple solution is best unless a medical professional experienced in blister management.

### [TEU-L2-003] Heat acclimation protocol selection (Figure 7.8) and sauna rules

- Goal: heat adaptation with minimal disruption to training.
- When used: before hot races (Western States 100, Transgrancanaria, The Coastal Challenge), or to jumpstart summer training.
- Structure (Fig 7.8): Racing in heat / scared about summer / trying to get fit -> yes to any proceeds; if all no: "Stay cool, get fit." Then: dry sauna -> 7 days, ~30 min post exercise; maintain 1 x 30 min session every 3rd day. Hot tub -> 7 days, 60–90 min postexercise. Wet sauna -> 7 days, 60–90 min postexercise. Extra layers of clothes -> 7 days, 60–90 min postexercise. None -> stay cool, get fit.
- Intensity anchors: core ~38.5°C.
- Things to keep in mind: acclimation will make you tired, minimize this; ideally 7–10 days in a row; maintain by adding heat exposure once every three days; decay ~2.5% per day after first 48 h without heat; two-week taper with no heat: ~35% decay in heart rate control, ~6% in core temperature control, ~30% in sweat rate improvements; reacclimation in as little as 4 days when traveling to a hot event; schedule on the most manageable training (not on peak volume); sauna preferred (efficacy, utility, accessibility), hot-water immersion bath a close second.

### [TEU-L2-004] RecoveryRun (RR)

- Goal: loosen legs, increase circulation and respiration with mild activity.
- When used: balanced with harder sessions; frequency depends on schedule, often 2–3 per week.
- Structure: continuous easy running, max 60 min; typical about 40 min.
- Intensity anchors: RPE 4 to 5 (not a leisurely walk, but substantially easier than an EnduranceRun). Must be very easy to be effective.
- Total duration: 20–60 min.

### [TEU-L2-005] EnduranceRun (ER)

- Goal: the "forever" moderate intensity; also surrounds interval sets (most running time is here).
- Structure: continuous; durations 30 min to more than 6 hours; typical 2 hours; 2–6 per week.
- Intensity anchors: RPE 5 or 6, varies naturally with hills.
- Cues: slow down when going uphill; intensity easily creeps into SteadyStateRun/lactate threshold territory, adding training stress and using energy needed later.

### [TEU-L2-006] SteadyStateRun (SSR)

- Goal: develop a stronger aerobic engine by holding an effort above normal "forever" pace but below lactate threshold.
- Structure: intervals as continuous as possible, 20–60 min each; total time at intensity 30 min–2 hours; typical 2 x 30 min separated by 4 min easy recovery. Recovery is intentionally short relative to interval length to protect the intensity, which stays well below lactate threshold. Work:rest 5 to 8:1 (Table 9.1).
- Intensity anchors: RPE 7 to 8; very close to everyday ER intensity; runners naturally fall into it on continuous climbs longer than 20 min.
- Cues: watch that intensity does not creep toward TempoRun territory on climbs. Can be done on flat, uphill or slightly rolling terrain.
- Frequency: 2–4 per week.

### [TEU-L2-008] TempoRun (TR)

- Goal: faster, stronger runner. Drives increases in size and density of mitochondria (better lactate processing/use as fuel), increases plasma volume (core temperature management), and increases mitochondrial enzyme activity (more energy faster).
- Intensity anchors: slightly below or at lactate threshold; RPE 8 or 9, gradually creeping up through each interval and the set.
- Structure: total interval time 40–90 min, in intervals of 8–15 min (Table 9.1 lists 8–20 min and 30–75 min); rest = half the interval (2:1), e.g. 6 min after 12-min intervals. Terrain flat, uphill or slightly rolling. Frequency 2–3 per week.
- Design logic: time at intensity is critical. Too much interval volume with long intervals and too little recovery makes intensity too low; too little volume with protracted intervals and copious recovery may make intensity too high.
- Rule of thumb: most trained runners can sustain TR intensity 40–90 min in an all-out time trial. Less experienced start at 40–50 min at LT pace; trained can reach an hour or longer, even as pace gets faster. Training both speeds LT pace and increases tolerance for longer durations.

### [TEU-L2-010] RunningIntervals (RI)

- Goal: improve stroke volume, maximum cardiac output and VO2max; increase blood plasma volume (heat tolerance); improve muscle capillarization (blood/oxygen delivery). Ultrarunners tend to avoid them but benefit.
- Intensity: "VO2 max" intensity (in quotes: true VO2max only for part of the workout). The driver of adaptation is accumulating more than 10 total minutes at an effort eliciting 90 percent of VO2max (research mostly by Véronique Billat, per the text "Vérinoque Billat").
- Structure: intervals 2–4 min (Table 9.1 says 1–3 min), total work 12–24 min, 1:1 work:recovery, RPE 9 to 10. Frequency 2–3 per week.
- Terrain: any or treadmill; uphill recommended (more workload, reach 90% VO2max more consistently). Never downhill (nearly impossible to reach >90% VO2max).

### [TEU-L2-012] Peak and fade RunningIntervals

- Goal: higher absolute and average rate of oxygen delivery to working muscles, hence stronger adaptation; also builds toughness (comfortable being uncomfortable, not saving anything).
- When used: advanced technique; requires two to three years running ultras and experience with interval work.
- Structure: start from your normal RunningInterval workout (e.g. six sets of 3 min hard, 3 min easy). At the start of each interval take 15 seconds to ramp to the highest intensity you think you can sustain for 60–90 seconds; hold as long as possible. Because the interval exceeds 90 s, the pace is intentionally unsustainable and you gradually slow in the last half. Repeat.
- Cues: on track/flat you are likely about 10 percent faster at the start than the end; heart rate rises through the set. Pacing will never be perfect (the point). Correct if pace is faster at start than end (may not be measurable on trails) and each interval is a literal struggle.
- Evidence: Rønnestad et al. 2019: well-trained cross-country skiers did 5 x 5 min hard, 3 min recovery, peak-and-fade vs evenly paced (TRAD); peak and fade gave higher mean and absolute VO2, i.e. more aerobic stress with identical construction. ### [TEU-L2-013] The Bull's-Eye Nutrition Strategy

- Goal: find 3–5 foods you can count on in any situation, to reach target calorie ranges and beat food fatigue.
- When used (period/phase, prerequisites): developed in training, particularly longer runs, by experimenting with different (sometimes counterintuitive) foods; target calorie range identified earlier in the book (Ch 13).
- Structure: classify every food tried on an archery-style target:
  • Bull's-eye foods: tried-and-true favorites.
  • Second ring: foods you don't eat all the time but have tried in training and know work; used if bull's-eye foods fail (tired of them, craving sweetness/saltiness/different texture). Typical fallback: aid station fare of cookies, soup, fruit, sandwiches.
  • Outer ring: foods not tried but similar to foods tried (e.g., chocolate chip cookies work but only oatmeal cookies available; regular potato chips work but only BBQ chips available). Also falls into aid station assortment.
  • Off target: foods known not to work and foods similar to them. List them so athlete and crew are reminded.
- Foods that work: easy to open and eat on the run, taste good, don't stick in teeth, make you run as well as or better than before eating. Foods that don't: hard to open, messy, crumbly, hard to hold in one hand, stick in mouth, too dry, hard to swallow, sit in the gut like a "calorie bomb", cause bloating/fullness, slow you down.
- Intensity anchors: the 3–5 bull's-eye foods together must include: AT LEAST ONE REAL FOOD (something you make/assemble or not made specifically for running: rice ball, PB&J sandwich, pretzels); AT LEAST ONE ENGINEERED FOOD (gel, chewable, sports drink); SOMETHING SWEET; SOMETHING SAVORY; SOMETHING SALTY.
- Total duration: n/a
- Progressions / variations: bull's-eye foods are rotated and substituted during any race according to target calorie range; once vetted, experiment with backup (second-ring) foods.
- Cues / notes: easy to explain to the support crew; athlete or crew can consult the target at any moment to decide.
Figure 14.1 (one runner's example): Bull's-eye: vanilla gel (engineered, sweet); Koop's bacon & egg rice ball (real food, savory and salty). Second ring: mixed nuts, banana, energy (item), Oreo cookies. Outer ring: cashews, cantaloupe, other, chocolate chip cookies. Off target: turkey sandwich, yogurt, potatoes.

### [TEU-L2-014] Training manipulation to enhance fat oxidation (Table 14.3)

- Goal: improve fat oxidation by restricting carbohydrate availability in specific sessions. Premise: low-carbohydrate sessions amplify cellular signaling to build more fat-oxidizing machinery (more mitochondria, upregulated oxidative enzymes; Marquet et al. 2016).
- When used: low-risk tools the author tolerates (see decision rule entry); needs time for two sessions in a day without added stress for two-a-day.
- Structure:
 1. Two-a-day: Step 1 running interval session (harder, to deplete glycogen); Step 2 restrict carbohydrate immediately post run; Step 3 second session of 1.5–2-hour EnduranceRun performed without fuel; Step 4 refuel with carbohydrate post run.
 2. Fasted run: Step 1 overnight fast (or, for evening runs, moderate carbohydrate through the day); Step 2 EnduranceRun of 1.5–2 hours upon waking, performed without fuel; Step 3 refuel with carbohydrate post run. Session typically limited to under two hours, no fuel.
- Intensity anchors: first session of two-a-day is a hard interval workout; second is EnduranceRun or RecoveryRun.
- Total duration: second/fasted run 1.5–2 hours.
- Cues / notes: author's 4 goals ("goldilocks"): leverage fat burning; maintain high-intensity performance; promote adaptation; avoid harm to bone health from low energy availability.

### [TEU-L2-017] Associative Strategy #1: The body scan

- Goal: formal systems check of the whole body in workouts/races; become more in tune with feedback, notice minor problems before they grow, regulate effort and adjust plan.

### [TEU-L2-018] Associative Strategy #2: Follow your breath

- Goal: regulate exertion, particularly in harder efforts; respiration rate is a near-universal proxy for heart rate and oxygen consumption during intervals.

### [TEU-L2-019] Worst-case imagery exercise (contingency planning)

- Goal: prevent a DNF when many things go wrong; especially for 200-mile events and long stage races.
- Step 1: write down the worst possible race situation: pick three or four horror stories (broken headlamp, leaving aid station without critical gear, getting lost, bonking badly), choose a point in the upcoming race where they could all happen at once; write the narrative in as much detail as possible. Author example: fall on Hope Pass, mile 44 of Leadville 100; knee split open, hands cut, bottles and poles broken, calories lost, no fluid or food for two hours, vomiting, crew not at Winfield, 2,000-foot climb ahead.
- Step 2: read the story and mentally rehearse, step by step, specifically, what you will do about each ailment (other crews/volunteers can help; medical patch-up; borrow a pack from a pacer). Be vivid (hair color, names, conversation, pack model/colorway); not just a counter-checklist. End successfully continuing down the trail (e.g., bandaged, borrowed pack, no poles), turning a possible DNF into a comeback.
- Notes: author has seen exactly such scenarios; some athletes continue, others quit.

### [TEU-L5-004] Finding your WHY (adapted from Simon Sinek, Start with Why)

Rationale: COVID-19 cancelled races; athletes whose running served a purpose beyond finisher medals and belt buckles kept training; others lost motivation. Racing is a legitimate motivator but over-emphasizing races is a danger; a strong purpose is a turbocharger. In 20+ years the author recalls only a handful who ran only to race (they did not last in ultrarunning).
1. Write down all the things you do consistently: voluntary, look forward to, don't postpone, derive satisfaction from; bonus for things nobody gives credit for. Not "getting out of bed". No meaning assigned yet. Author's list: build trails, coach athletes, mentor coaches, train/run (Fig 15.7).
2. Find threads intertwined with your actions: themes in common; takes time (author took a month; revisit weekly). Avoid truisms ("makes me feel good", "I love it"); ask why/what. Author's theme: improvement/betterment (Fig 15.8).
3. Phone a friend: outside perspective (self-evaluation is biased toward who we want to be); a running friend who tells it like it is vets your list and pokes holes in themes.
4. Craft your personal WHY: one sentence, one theme (two at most); concrete, actionable, simple. Author's: "To leave things better than I found them."
5. Drill your WHY back to the PBM: WHY reinforces motivation, an anchor of PBM; stronger WHY = more purposeful motivation, driving the whole training process, especially in hard training and racing.

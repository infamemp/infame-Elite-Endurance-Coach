# Joe Friel — *The Cyclist's Training Bible* (5th ed., 2018) — Knowledge Base for Coach GEM

## 0. Document Provenance and Author's Self-Stated Scope

This 5th edition was, per the author, a complete rewrite begun from a blank page; only the table of contents was preserved from earlier editions. The book grew from ~70,000 to ~130,000 words across 20 years. Friel describes the book's heart as **Chapters 7–9 (seasonal planning)**: *"I consider this to be the hub of the book. The chapters that come before and after are intended to enhance your individual training plan."* He explicitly states the mental/attitudinal portion (Part I, Chapters 1–2) plays "only supporting roles" and that the book's focus is **physical performance**. Author's working definition of "high performance" is the simultaneous achievement of three outcomes: **fitness, speed, and goals** — supported by an aligned attitude and lifestyle.

Friel's central training philosophy, stated explicitly and used as the unifying thread for every planning chapter:

> *"The closer you are to your race, the more your training must be like the race."*

He also quotes Franz Stampfl as the underlying epistemological frame: *"Training is principally an act of faith."*

## 1. PART I — MIND AND BODY (Chapters 1–2)

### 1.1 Mental Performance (Ch. 1)

Friel's coaching premise: *"All athletes are limited more by their minds than by their bodies."* When evaluating an athlete for coaching, the first thing he assesses is mental fitness; without "at least a spark" of the right attitude, he believes success is unlikely.

#### 1.1.1 Motivation
- Must be **intrinsic**; the coach can offer suggestions and perspective but cannot supply it.
- A high goal demands a lifestyle aligned with it: *"At the highest level, everything in your life—from food to friends and beyond—must be focused on the goal."*
- Being around other motivated riders is "contagious."

#### 1.1.2 Dreams, Goals, and Missions (a progression, not synonyms)
- **Dream** — the persistent vision; precedes the goal, often by years.
- **Goal** — a committed dream with a plan and changes to one's life.
- **Mission** — the will to pursue the goal in the face of obstacles. *"There is a purposeful attitude about a person on a mission."*
- "Wishes" are vague desires that are NOT goals. *"Excellence is rare."*

#### 1.1.3 Confidence: Two Operational Tools

**(a) Saving Successes Account.** Every night before sleep, recall one thing done well that day and relive it. Deposit *only* positive experiences; *"Never deposit the bad experiences or unwelcome moments. Never."* Make withdrawals at moments of doubt (race week, start line, criticism).

**(b) "Fake It 'Til You Make It" (Act As If).** Always assume the posture and demeanor of a confident athlete: stand tall, head up, eye contact, calm, no denigration of others. *"It's not possible to be confident with a slumping posture and defeated demeanor. It's like saying no while shaking your head yes."*

#### 1.1.4 Mental Toughness — Graham Jones, PhD (Harvard Business Review study of Olympic medalists vs. non-medalists)
Friel cites this study explicitly. Podium-placing Olympians:
- Paid meticulous attention to their goals
- Had a strong inner drive to stay ahead of the competition
- Concentrated on excellence
- Were not distracted by other people or athletes
- Shrugged off their own failures
- Rebounded from defeat easily
- Never self-flagellated
- Celebrated their wins
- Analyzed the reasons for their success
- Were very confident of their abilities

#### 1.1.5 Patience — the missing eleventh attribute
> *"Cycling is a patience sport. And the longer your race, the more patience it takes. A time trial is not so much a race as it is a test of your patience."*

Impatient riders start workouts/intervals/races too fast and fade. *"Patience means controlling your emotions in the early stages of anything you do."* Friel calls pacing "emotion control." His pedagogical fix: when an athlete starts intervals too fast, he has them repeat the workout *again and again* until they get it right.

#### 1.1.6 Commitment, Tenacity, and the Central Governor

Friel draws on **Tim Noakes, PhD (South Africa) — Central Governor Theory**: *"The fatigue you experience actually occurs in the brain… the brain is constantly monitoring all of the signals from the body, and it decides when it's time to pull the plug."* Overcoming this brain-imposed limit is the trainable component of tenacity. Workouts that produce suffering "must prepare the mind as much as, if not more than, the body."

> *"Racing is all about managing suffering."*

#### 1.1.7 Consistency and Routines
- Body and mind prefer routines. Pre-race and pre-workout routines stabilize performance.
- Most serious riders place priorities as: family/friends → career → training (training third).
- *"If there are more than two higher-priority activities that come before training, you'll frequently miss workouts."*
- **Missed workouts are the first sign that a rider lacks commitment.**

#### 1.1.8 High-Performance Team (Friel's complete recommended roster)
- Family and friends — must be supportive
- Cycling team (training partners, encouragement, advice)
- **Coach or knowledgeable mentor** (single most impactful addition)
- Physical therapist (works with endurance athletes/cyclists)
- Sports medicine doctor
- Masseuse
- Mechanic
- Bike fitter
- Personal trainer (gym), nutritionist, chiropractor, sports psychologist (optional)
- *Rule:* every team member must be a "happy, positive, and successful person whom you like."

### 1.2 Physical Performance (Ch. 2)

#### 1.2.1 The Three Physiological Determiners of Endurance Fitness
Friel reiterates these throughout the book; they are the foundation:

1. **Aerobic Capacity (VO₂max)** — maximal oxygen processed at maximal effort for a few minutes.
2. **Anaerobic Threshold (AnT)** — the % of VO₂max sustainable for 40–70 minutes. *"sometimes called 'lactate threshold,' although physiologically these are not exactly the same thing."*
3. **Economy** — energy wasted while riding (physical structure, pedaling style, position, bike-handling skills). *"The less energy you waste, the better your economy."*

#### 1.2.2 Phenotypes (Author's Original Nomenclature)
Friel adopts "phenotype" to describe a rider's cycling-specific aptitude. **Every rider fits one of these four categories**:

- **Climber** — flat power-duration slope; very high aerobic capacity, high AnT, excellent climbing economy; defined by high **power-to-weight ratio (W/kg)**. Climbing places the greatest total physiological demands.
- **Sprinter** — very high left-end (short-duration) power, steep downward slope; great short-burst power requiring aerodynamic efficiency in/out of saddle; weak at climbing and TT.
- **Time Trialist** — high, sustainable power; FTP is the best predictor. Often relatively *big* riders (aerodynamic drag, not gravity, is the constraint on flat TT courses).
- **All-rounder** — competent in two or three disciplines, not exceptional in any; thrives when course conditions don't favor specialists.

> *"The cycling disciplines themselves are known as 'phenotypes.'"*

The power-duration curve (Figure 2.1, attributed to Tim Cusick of WKO) is the visualization Friel uses for all phenotypes.

#### 1.2.3 Training Beliefs (Author's caveats)
- *"Training is a research study with one subject: you."*
- Published research results are means; some subjects respond more, some less, some negatively.
- **Slow responders vs. fast responders** — gains may take weeks or months depending on the individual.
- **Slow-twitch vs. fast-twitch muscle composition** is largely genetic and bounds responsiveness.
- *"Training is also an act of faith. There is no promise…you must believe that you are going down the right path…or all is lost."*

#### 1.2.4 The Four Steps to Training with a Purpose (Friel's signature framework)

**Step 1: Clear Goal.** Every workout must have a "purpose" (distinct from a "goal" — the terminology is deliberate). Friel inserts a third intermediate level later in Ch. 5: training **objectives**. Hierarchy: **Season Goal → Training Objective → Daily Workout Purpose**.

> *"Before you start any training session, always ask yourself this key question: What is the purpose of this workout? Don't turn a pedal until you can answer it."*

**Step 2: Expert Instruction.** Options ranked by Friel: a personal coach (best), a generic purchased plan, a knowledgeable mentor/training partner, or self-coaching (steepest learning curve, prone to rookie mistakes).

**Step 3: Specific Practice.** Follow the plan to the letter. *"If they feel the need to make the workout easier, they can always make that decision and tell me about it later. But they should not make the session more challenging without talking with me beforehand."* Group rides typically destroy specificity; *"the best option is to turn and get away from the other riders."*

**Step 4: Immediate Feedback.** Analyze workout data as soon as possible. *"The key question you should always seek to answer is, did I accomplish the purpose of the workout?"*

## 2. PART II — TRAINING FUNDAMENTALS (Chapters 3–4)

### 2.1 Basic Training Concepts (Ch. 3)

#### 2.1.1 The Four Training Principles (verbatim names)

**(1) Progressive Overload.** *"In order to increase fitness, you must gradually and steadily increase the amount of training you do."* The workload must exceed the current adaptation level. Excessive overload = overtraining.

**(2) Specificity.** Sports-science distinction Friel deploys:
- **Central** changes: heart, lungs, blood — crosstrain-friendly. *"The heart doesn't know the difference between running and cycling."*
- **Peripheral** changes: muscles — sport-specific. *"You can't fool the quadriceps muscle in your thigh."*
- Training shifts seasonally from central (early) to peripheral (later).

**(3) Reversibility.** *"Use it or lose it."* A day off = a tiny, measurable-only-over-time loss of fitness. A taper involves reversibility deliberately because the goal is form, not fitness.

**(4) Individuality.** Athletes differ in muscle fiber type, leg geometry (thighbone length relative to total leg length), strength response, heat tolerance, recovery rate. *"You can't do what your training partner does and expect the same results."*

#### 2.1.2 Duration, Intensity, Frequency
- **Duration** — Friel uses time, not distance, throughout: *"Your body reacts to how long you maintain a high intensity, not to how many miles or kilometers you cover."* Intensity is *inversely* related to duration.
- **Intensity** — measured by speedometer, HRM, power meter, lactate analyzer, or RPE.
- **Frequency** — how often per week.

#### 2.1.3 Stage-Based Training Focus (used throughout the book)
- **Novice (Year 1):** focus on **frequency** — getting on the bike often. Power meter not critical.
- **Intermediate (Years 2–3):** focus on **duration** — building aerobic endurance.
- **Advanced (3+ years):** focus on **intensity** — the determiner of high-performance racing.

#### 2.1.4 Volume, Intensity, Dose, Density, Training Load
- **Volume** = frequency × duration (hours per week). Easy to measure → over-emphasized.
- **Friel's allocation rule (his opinion, no research cited):** for the advanced rider, **intensity ≈ 60%** of race-day fitness, **volume ≈ 40%**.
- **Dose** = how hard a single workout is (high duration and/or high intensity).
- **Density** = how closely the high-dose workouts are spaced.
- Young, fit riders → tolerate higher density. Juniors and 50+ → typically lower density.
- **Training Load** = volume × intensity, expressed as either hours or **TSS** (preferred).

#### 2.1.5 Supercompensation
Fitness is realized *during recovery*, especially during sleep. A high-dose workout only produces the **potential** for fitness. Forcing supercompensation faster than nature allows ≈ overtraining.

#### 2.1.6 Fitness, Fatigue, and Form (foundational triad)

| Concept | Friel's working definition |
|---|---|
| **Fitness** | Race readiness; measurable by lab tests, race results, "how you feel," or training-load progression. |
| **Fatigue** | The cost of training; the indirect indicator that the body has been overloaded. |
| **Form** | Freshness + race readiness. Originates etymologically from 1800s European horse-race betting forms. |

**Friel's three Iron Laws of fitness/fatigue/form:**
1. *"Fitness and fatigue trend in the same direction."* If fitness rises, so does fatigue. If you never become fatigued, you never become fit.
2. *"Fatigue always appears before fitness."* Fitness changes in weeks; fatigue changes in hours/days.
3. *"Fatigue is your friend."* But form requires shedding it while keeping fitness mostly intact (the taper).

Friel references **TrainingPeaks.com Performance Management Chart** (Coggan) as the operational tool for tracking all three.

### 2.2 Training Intensity (Ch. 4)

#### 2.2.1 The Three Intensity Tools — Use All Three
Title is deliberate: *"Training with Effort, Heart Rate, and Power"* — not "or."
- **Effort (RPE)** — the most basic, most useful in racing decisions. *"Road racing requires having a good sense of how hard you are working from a purely subjective point of view."*
- **Heart Rate** — measures **effort**; invented late 1970s (Finland, Nordic skiers); tipping point ~1990s.
- **Power** — measures **performance**; invented by Uli Schoberer (German engineer-cyclist) ten years after HRMs; revolutionized the sport.
- **Speed** — NOT a good training intensity gauge; too sensitive to wind/hills.

A power meter measures only two things: **torque (pedal force)** and **cadence (pedal speed)**. Watts = torque × cadence.

> *"I require the riders I coach to have both [HRM and power meter]."*

#### 2.2.2 Rating of Perceived Exertion (RPE) — 0-to-10 Scale (Table 4.1)
Friel notes that RPE in group rides is systematically *lower* than solo at the same power/HR — a motivation effect.

| RPE | Descriptor |
|---|---|
| 0 | Rest |
| ~3.5 | Moderate (≈ AeT) |
| 5–6 | Hard / Tempo (between thresholds) |
| 7 | Very hard (≈ AnT / lactate threshold) |
| 9 | Above AnT (aerobic capacity zone) |
| 10 | Maximal |

#### 2.2.3 Intensity Reference Points — AeT and AnT

**Aerobic Threshold (AeT) ≈ RPE 3–4**
- Blood lactate rises above resting baseline (~1 mmol/L) to ~**2 mmol/L**.
- Fully aerobic; fuel primarily from body fat.
- A fit athlete can sustain AeT for **several hours**.
- **Three ways to estimate AeT** *(note: book gives three; see Inconsistencies §10)*:
  1. ~65% of max HR
  2. 20–40 bpm below AnT
  3. Specifically, 30 bpm below AnT (Friel's preferred quick estimate)

**Anaerobic Threshold (AnT) ≈ RPE 7**
- Lactate ~**4 mmol/L**; hydrogen ion accumulation begins to restrict muscle contraction.
- *"An athlete in exceptionally good physical condition can maintain an intensity at AnT for roughly an hour."*
- *"Fatigue is NOT caused by lactic acid."* (Friel explicitly debunks this.)
- AnT/lactate threshold are NOT physiologically identical but Friel treats them as functionally equivalent: AnT measured by gas analysis, LT by blood draw.

**Race outcome rule:**
- Time trials: raced near AnT.
- Road race outcomes: determined by what happens *above* AnT — sprints, climbs, breakaways, gap-closing, guttering, attacks.
- Time trial pacing: steady, near-AnT — avoid surges.

#### 2.2.4 Intensity Distribution — Polarized Training (Friel's version)

> *"Over the course of a season, the total time you spent at or below your AeT should be around 70 to 80 percent. The remaining 20 to 30 percent should be above the AeT, with a sizable chunk of that above the AnT."*

**The Single Biggest Training Mistake** (verbatim emphasis):
> *"The single biggest mistake in training is to make easy workouts moderately hard (between AeT and AnT), thus decreasing recovery."*
> *"The easier your easy workouts, the harder your hard workouts can be."*

#### 2.2.5 Functional Threshold (Andrew Coggan, PhD — Early 2000s)
Created because (a) lab testing is expensive and inconvenient, and (b) AnT power changes with fitness.

**Functional Threshold Power (FTP)** = a field-test estimate of AnT power.
**Functional Threshold Heart Rate (FTHR)** = field-test estimate of AnT HR.

**Test Protocols** (multiple acceptable variants):

| Test Duration | FTP Calculation | FTHR Calculation |
|---|---|---|
| 1-hour race (e.g., 40 km TT, 1-hour crit) | Average power | Average HR |
| **30-minute test** | Average power | Average HR (no subtraction) |
| **20-minute test (Coggan's standard, preferred)** | Avg power − 5% | Avg HR − 5% |
| 8-minute test | Avg power − 10% | NOT reliable for FTHR |
| 5-minute test | Avg power − 12% *(but Ch. 4 text also says "−15%" — see Inconsistencies §10)* | NOT reliable for FTHR |

Tests shorter than 20 minutes are **not accurate** for FTHR. Test every **3–4 weeks** throughout the season. Day-to-day variability is normal (fatigue, lifestyle). Aim for a *"ballpark figure in a pretty small yard (think Fenway Park, not Dodger Stadium)."*

**FTP/FTHR Test Protocol Details** (Friel-prescribed):
- **Course**: stretch of road with bike lane, light traffic, no stop signs, few intersections; flat to slightly uphill (≤3% grade); 5–10 mi (8–16 km) total needed; same course every time. Indoor trainer acceptable.
- **Prep**: 2–3 days of reduced training before test (end of an R&R week is ideal).
- **Warm-up**: 20+ minutes, gradually increasing intensity. Last 5–10 minutes: progressively shorter, more intense accelerations at and above RPE 7 (starting at 2 minutes, gradually becoming 10-second efforts), with near-complete recoveries. Then 5 minutes very easy recovery. Then start test.
- **Pacing the test**: Start conservatively — most riders' biggest error is starting too fast. First 5 minutes should feel like RPE ~7. Every 5 minutes, decide whether to go slightly faster/slower for the next segment. For 5- and 8-minute tests, decision points are every 1 minute.
- **Cooldown**: low intensity until HR and breathing return to resting.

**Why both HR and Power zones are needed**:
- For advanced riders, **FTHR is stable** all season (small drift from diet/stress/fatigue/excitement).
- **FTP rises** with fitness — power zones shift up.
- Zone overlap *should* shift over the season (e.g., HR Z2 with Power Z3 = aerobic fitness gain). Greg LeMond, quoted: *"It never gets easier, you just go faster."*

#### 2.2.6 Training Zones — Five-Zone System (Tables 4.2 and 4.3, referenced)
Friel uses Coggan's standard zone framework. Zones are derived from FTHR (heart rate) and FTP (power). The book references the tables; the structural concept is what matters for the Coach GEM:

- **Zone 1** = Recovery, ≤55% FTP / well below FTHR
- **Zone 2** = Aerobic endurance, around AeT
- **Zone 3** = Tempo, between AeT and AnT
- **Zone 4** = Threshold, around FTP/FTHR
- **Zone 5** = VO₂max/anaerobic, above AnT
- **Zone 5b (HR)** referenced in some workouts
- **Zone 6 (Power)** referenced in sprint workouts

The five-zone weight system used for the manual TSS estimate: zone 1 = weight 1, zone 5 = weight 5, etc. (Table 3.1).

#### 2.2.7 Training Stress Score (TSS) — Andrew Coggan, PhD
- Inputs: workout normalized power, intensity factor, duration in seconds, rider's FTP.
- Software (TrainingPeaks.com) computes automatically.
- Slight emphasis given to intensity over duration.
- Reference values: **professional cyclists average ~150 TSS per week** during much of the season (per Ch. 10).
- **Long-term hourly TSS average ≈ 50** (range 45–55 depending on intensity emphasis).

**Friel's strong recommendation**: shift from hours-based to TSS-based training. *"Using TSS will change your perceptions of what training is about."* It will not only be more accurate, it will shift focus *from saddle time to intensity*.

## 3. PART III — PURPOSEFUL TRAINING (Chapters 5–6)

### 3.1 Getting Started — Pre-Season Protocol (Ch. 5)

#### 3.1.1 The Three-Part Assessment (every season, every athlete)

**(a) Physical Assessment and Bike Fit**
- Find a PT who works with endurance athletes/cyclists.
- Head-to-toe exam: posture, strength, range of joint motion, muscular balance, dynamic function, structural idiosyncrasies/asymmetries.
- PT produces a corrective exercise program AND inputs for the bike fitter.
- *"I've seen riders raise their FTP by more than 10 percent after simply having their bikes adjusted to their bodies."* (Even riders who thought their position was perfect.)
- **Repeat every season.** Things change: muscle, flexibility, range of motion, aging, weight, equipment.

**(b) Mental Assessment (Sidebar 5.1)**
A 30-item self-rating (Never = 1 to Always = 6) on motivation, confidence, thought habits, focus, visualization. Total scoring scale:
| Total | Rating |
|---|---|
| 32–36 | 5 |
| 27–31 | 4 |
| 21–26 | 3 |
| 16–20 | 2 |
| 6–15 | 1 |

Friel's caveat: most serious athletes will score high on **motivation**, but motivation has a "dark side" — refusing to rest/taper. He cites recommended books in Sidebar 5.2: Afremow, Bell, Cox, Fitzgerald, Gonzalez, Grover, LeUnes, Lynch, Mack/Casstevens, Orlick, Smith/Kays, Weinberg/Gould.

**(c) Fitness Assessment** — see Field Tests below.

#### 3.1.2 The Four Field Tests (Sidebar 5.3 / Appendix B "Field Tests")

Friel's mantra throughout the book: *"That which is measured improves."*

All tests require a thorough warm-up: 20+ minutes building intensity, last 5–10 minutes with progressive, increasingly intense accelerations to RPE 7+ (starting at 2 minutes down to 10 seconds), then 5 minutes very easy. Tests are done after 3–5 days reduced training. **Use the same course every time.**

| Test | Code (App. B) | Purpose | Required Tools | Protocol |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Functional Aerobic Threshold (FAeT) | T1 | Track aerobic endurance fitness via efficiency factor over time | HRM + power meter | Ride 1–3 hours steady at AeT HR ± 2 bpm (AeT estimated as FTHR − 30 bpm). EF = Normalized Power / Average HR. Repeat every 3–4 weeks. EF rises with aerobic fitness. |
| FTP and FTHR | T2 | Determine functional threshold(s) and set zones | HRM + power meter | 20-minute all-out (or 30/8/5 alternatives). Subtract appropriate %. Repeat every 3–4 weeks. |
| Functional Aerobic Capacity (FACP) | T3 | Estimate VO₂max power | Power meter | All-out steady 5-minute effort. Average power = FACP. |
| Sprint Power | T5 | Track sprint progress | Power meter | Rolling start; 8 right-side (16 total) pedal strokes at max effort. Standing or seated, hoods or drops. **NOTE**: Sidebar 5.3 says "average power," Appendix B T5 says "peak power" — see Inconsistencies §10. |
| Time Trial | T4 | Track AnE + ME ability | HRM + power meter | 10 km TT (flat to 1–2% uphill), max effort, on TT bike if applicable. |

Friel adds: lab tests and field tests are both valuable. Lab tests give physiological detail; field tests are "black box" but easy to repeat. *"I use both with the athletes I coach."*

#### 3.1.3 Seasonal Goals (Friel's Goal-Setting Rules)
- Limit to **three or fewer** season goals.
- **Avoid contradictory goals** (e.g., short criterium series + 40 km TT PR in the same window — different physiological adaptations).
- Goals must be: **specific, measurable, difficult-but-attainable, and time-bound** (have a deadline).
- Friel's coaching philosophy: *"That which is measured improves."*

**Common goal-setting errors:**
- **Measureless goals** ("ride faster this year") — vague, unmeasurable.
- **Unrealistic goals** ("win the masters national road race on June 2" for a novice) — knowing the goal is unreachable kills motivation.
- **Too-easy goals** — no challenge, no purposeful training.

#### 3.1.4 Hierarchy of Goals (Friel's Three-Tier System)
1. **Seasonal Goal** — the outcome (typically a race result).
2. **Training Objective** — a measurable sub-goal that fixes a specific **limiter**. Has its own deadline (usually start of taper for advanced abilities; start of build for basic abilities).
3. **Daily Workout Purpose** — what this single workout will accomplish toward the objective.

Examples of training objectives (verbatim from the book):
- *"Climb Lee Hill in less than 20 minutes by April 23"* (climbing limiter / ME)
- *"Weigh 154 pounds by April 23"* (weight as a climbing limiter)
- *"Average 1000 watts for sprint power test by May 30"* (sprint limiter / SP)
- *"Increase functional threshold power to 280 watts by July 15"* (TT limiter / ME)
- *"Increase functional aerobic capacity power to 430 watts by August 7"* (AnE limiter)
- *"Increase my efficiency factor by 10 percent by March 1"* (AE basic ability)
- *"Leg press 2.5 times my body weight by the end of base 1"* (MF basic ability)

Recommended resources: BestBikeSplit.com, Strava.com, AnalyticCycling.com for benchmarking power goals.

### 3.2 Preparing to Race — Abilities and Limiters (Ch. 6)

#### 3.2.1 The Six Abilities (Friel's Original Taxonomy — used throughout remainder of book)

These six workout categories are the entire workout vocabulary of the system.

**Three Basic Abilities** (foundation, emphasized in base period; corners of the ability triangle):
1. **Aerobic Endurance (AE)** — keep going at low-moderate intensity for long durations (zone 2, RPE <5). Most important basic ability. *"Poor aerobic endurance is a game-stopper."* Develops slow-twitch characteristics in fast-twitch fibers, capillary density, oxygen-carrying blood, muscle enzymes.
2. **Muscular Force (MF)** — overcome resistance: hills, headwinds, gravity. Built via weight lifting and on-bike force reps (high-gear, low-cadence, seated).
3. **Speed Skills (SS)** — efficient/effective movement, especially **pedaling cadence skills**. *"I'm NOT referring to bike speed—but rather to your leg speed when pedaling."* Also cornering, sprinting, climbing skills. Critical early in base period.

**Three Advanced Abilities** (race-specific, emphasized in build period; sides of the triangle, each built from the two adjacent basic abilities):
4. **Muscular Endurance (ME)** — moderately long at moderately high effort (≈ AnT, zones 3–4, RPE 5–7). Built from **AE + MF**. Critical for time trialing and steady climbs. 6-to-12-minute repeats with 90s–4min recoveries; 20- to 40-minute steady efforts.
5. **Anaerobic Endurance (AnE)** — short, very intense intervals (zone 5, RPE 9). Built from **AE + SS**. *"Very strong medicine. It's not candy."* Best workout type for raising aerobic capacity. Treat with caution; high risk.
6. **Sprint Power (SP)** — <20-second max-effort intervals with several-minute recoveries. Built from **SS + MF**.

#### 3.2.2 The Ability Triangle (Figure 6.2)
- AE—MF side: produces **Muscular Endurance**
- AE—SS side: produces **Anaerobic Endurance**
- SS—MF side: produces **Sprint Power**

**Sequencing rule**: Build basic abilities first (8–12 weeks). Then shift to advanced abilities (8–12 weeks).
- **Counterintuitive rule**: It's never wrong to extend basic training. *"You can never have too much base fitness."*
- **Limit**: Don't extend advanced training (especially AnE) beyond ~8–12 weeks — burnout/injury/illness risk.
- **Veteran-only option**: Insert *minimal* amounts of advanced ability training (short reps, long recoveries) into the base period, e.g., 3–4 × 20s AnE intervals with 20s recoveries after MF reps. **Risky for less-experienced riders.**

#### 3.2.3 Limiters — Friel's Most Important Operational Concept

> *"A limiter is a goal-specific weakness that stands between you and the successful accomplishment of a seasonal goal."*

Key distinction: a **weakness** ≠ a **limiter**. A weak climber doing a flat criterium series doesn't have climbing as a limiter — that weakness doesn't matter for the goal.

**Process**:
1. Compare your weaknesses (climber/sprinter/TT taxonomy) against your **A-race demands**.
2. Use **Table 6.1** to map race-specific limiter → required advanced ability:
   - Climbing limiter (power climber) → AnE
   - Climbing limiter (TT-style climber) → ME
   - Sprinting limiter → SP
   - Time trialing limiter → ME
3. The advanced ability identifies which **two basic abilities** to emphasize in base period (via the ability triangle).

**Non-physiological limiters**: power-to-weight, training inconsistency, limited training time, low confidence, family non-support, poor nutrition, insufficient equipment, inadequate training environment, propensity to overtrain, frequent illness/injury, body composition, insufficient sleep, unusual psychological stress, physically demanding job.

**Climbing-specific note**: Power-to-weight ratio is always part of the climbing limiter. **FACP (anaerobic endurance ability)** ≈ short-steep / power climbing; **FTP (muscular endurance ability)** ≈ long-steady / TT-style climbing.

**Tactical accommodation rule**: Even after improving a limiter, deploy tactics:
- Climbing limiter → start at the front of the pack on a climb, drift back to remain in the group.
- Sprinting limiter → choose whose wheel to follow.
- TT limiter → no tactical hiding; *"the race of truth."*

**Strength counterbalance rule**: When the limiter is great, select races that play to your strength to maintain it.

**Novice/intermediate rule**: Basic abilities are the typical limiters. **Don't rush into advanced ability work** before basic ability fitness is well established (this can take 1–3 years).

**Experienced athlete rule**: Even advanced riders must **rebuild basic abilities** every season; they erode during taper and transition.

## 4. PART IV — PLANNING YOUR SEASON (Chapters 7–9)

*Friel's self-stated "hub" of the book.*

### 4.1 Periodization Overview (Ch. 7)

#### 4.1.1 Conceptual History
- Origins: Soviet Union, 1950s–60s. State secret of Eastern Bloc countries through the early 1980s.
- First Western adoption: European distance runners, early 1970s.
- Western introduction via Bompa (*Theory and Methodology of Training*); Friel introduced classic linear periodization to amateur road cyclists in the 1st edition (1995).
- Pre-periodization era: *"Cyclists simply trained as their coaches had trained before them. There was little in the way of a scientific method."* Saddle time was the predictor.

#### 4.1.2 The Periodization Periods (Mesocycles)

| Period | Common Length | Workout Character | Purpose |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Prep** | 0–4 weeks | Very general; not race-like; mostly aerobic endurance + speed skills + weight lifting AA/MT | Return to structure after the transition period |
| **Base 1** | 3–4 weeks | General; focus on **AE + MF + SS**; weight lifting in MS phase (heaviest) | Build basic foundation |
| **Base 2** | 3–4 weeks | General; AE + MF + SS + early ME; weight lifting in SM (1×/week) | Continue basic abilities; introduce ME |
| **Base 3** | 3–4 weeks | Transitional; AE + MF + SS + ME + AnE introduced; group rides begin (1/week) | Bridge to specific/race-like work |
| **Build 1** | 3–4 weeks | Specific (race-like); advanced abilities dominant (ME, AnE, SP); basic abilities maintained only | Race-specific fitness development |
| **Build 2** | 3–4 weeks | Specific; very race-like; group rides become C-priority races; high intensity | Final pre-taper specificity |
| **Peak** | 1–2 weeks | Reduced duration, high intensity; every-3rd-day hard sessions | Shed fatigue; gain form |
| **Race** | 1 week (sometimes consecutive weeks if back-to-back A races) | Very short, race-intensity intervals (90s reps), days off | Final taper to peak form |
| **Transition** | 2 days – 6 weeks (depending on season placement) | No structured training; optional crosstraining; rest + rejuvenate | Recover physically and mentally |

#### 4.1.3 Mesocycle Length — Age and Recovery
**Table 7.4 (referenced):** Friel's two-column system based on recovery capacity:
- **Fast-recovery athletes (often under 50)**: 4-week mesocycles (~3 weeks training + 3–5 days R&R)
- **Slow-recovery athletes (over 50, novices, some intermediates)**: 3-week mesocycles (~2 weeks training + 3–5 days R&R), per Table 9.1

Friel emphasizes: **age is only a proxy for recovery capacity.** Some 60+ riders recover fast; some 30-year-olds recover slowly. The athlete must self-assess based on past experience. Hormone production (growth hormone, testosterone, estrogen, IGF, etc.) declines with age and slows recovery — by 50, it becomes obvious for most.

#### 4.1.4 Race Spacing Rules (Figure 7.1, 7.2, 7.3)
- **First A-race of the season** (Figure 7.1): full periodization sequence (prep → base 1 → base 2 → base 3 → build 1 → build 2 → peak → race).
- **Between A-races, with 12–16 weeks separation** (Figure 7.2): no repeat of prep/base 1/base 2. Short transition → base 3 (to rebuild basic abilities after taper-induced erosion) → build 1 → build 2 → peak → race.
- **7–11 weeks between races** (Figure 7.3): tighter, more challenging planning.
- **<7 weeks between races**: difficult; possibly only 2–3 days of transition, then return to the most appropriate period. The **FAeT (efficiency factor) test** decides: if EF dropped >10% from previous base 3, return to base 3. If <5%, skip directly to build period. <4 weeks remaining: build 2 only, then very short peak (3–5 days).

#### 4.1.5 The Annual Training Plan (ATP) — Six Steps

**Step 1: Establish Season Goals.** Top of the ATP. ≤3 goals (fewer is better).

**Step 2: Determine Training Objectives.** Up to 4 objectives. Each addresses one limiter with a deadline (advanced abilities by start of taper; basic abilities by start of build).

**Step 3: Set Annual Training Volume** (hours OR TSS — Friel prefers TSS).
Three methods to estimate:
- (a) Last season's hours/TSS, +10% if season went well (more for those <5 years in the sport).
- (b) Weekly average × 50 weeks (factor in known absences).
- (c) Table 7.2 — uses official federation racing category as a proxy.
- Multiply hours × 45 (low intensity), × 50 (avg), or × 55 (high intensity) for estimated annual TSS.

**Step 4: Prioritize Races (A/B/C — Table 7.3).**

| Priority | Taper | Treatment | Limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A** | 1–3 week peak + transition recovery | Best possible performance; major plan revolves around these | **≤3 per season** (stage races count as one) |
| **B** | 2–5 days mini-taper | Race hard; expect a small peak but not maximal | Several allowed |
| **C** | 1–2 days reduced training (≈ workout) | Treat as workouts; rest day-before only | Many allowed (test/tune-up/social) |

> *"You will lose fitness, especially basic abilities, with each taper. Three A races is about the maximum that can be managed and still allow time to rebuild fitness."*

**Alternative race-strategy options:**
- 1 A-race scheduled mid-season + a "rehearsal A" 12–16 weeks earlier
- Or: train base period only, repeat base 3 multiple times to bridge to the single late-season A race
- Or: no A races at all; many B races; less peak, more enjoyment, lower performance

**Step 5: Divide Season into Periods.** Working backwards from each A-race on the ATP, label each weekly row (Mon. date column) with the appropriate mesocycle, mesocycle length chosen by athlete's recovery profile.

**Step 6: Estimate Weekly Volume** (Tables 7.5 and 7.6).
- *"Your weekly volume is not a rigid number that must be accomplished. It's only meant to be a ballpark guide, albeit a small ballpark."*
- Weekly volume includes: bike + strength + aerobic crosstraining; EXCLUDES stretching, passive recovery, yoga, meditation.

### 4.2 Planning a Week (Ch. 8)

#### 4.2.1 Workout Assignment in the ATP — Eight Columns
After the period column, the ATP has 8 workout-type columns:
1. **Weight Lifting** (with phase code: AA / MT / MS / SM)
2. **Aerobic Endurance (AE)**
3. **Muscular Force (MF)**
4. **Speed Skills (SS)**
5. **Muscular Endurance (ME)**
6. **Anaerobic Endurance (AnE)**
7. **Sprint Power (SP)**
8. **Testing (T)**

Place an **X** in each ability column for each week where that ability will be trained.

#### 4.2.2 Ability-by-Period Assignment Map (Linear Periodization)

| Period | Marked Abilities (excluding last R&R week) | Weight Lifting |
|---|---|---|
| Prep | AE, SS (MF only if NOT lifting) | AA (most riders) → MT |
| Base 1 | AE, MF, SS | MS (4 weeks; 2–3×/week) |
| Base 2 | AE, MF, SS, ME | SM (1×/week) |
| Base 3 | AE, MF, SS, ME, AnE | SM |
| Build 1 | AE (maintain), MF (maintain via warm-up), SS (maintain), ME, AnE, SP — race-specific blend | SM |
| Build 2 | Same as Build 1; more race-like | SM |
| Peak | ME/AnE/SP (race-specific); AE in easy recoveries; NO MF | SM (low priority) |
| Race | Same as Peak | None (unless consecutive A races: SM only in 2nd race week) |
| Transition | None (blank row) | None |
| Last week of base 1/2/3 and build 1/2 (R&R) | AE + SS + Testing | (varies) |

#### 4.2.3 Weight Lifting Phases (Detail in Ch. 12 but Periodized Here)

| Phase | Code | Period(s) | Frequency | Load | Reps | Purpose |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Anatomical Adaptation** | AA | Prep | 2–3×/week (4–9 sessions total over 2 weeks) | Low | High | Master movement patterns |
| **Muscular Transition** | MT | Late prep / early base 1 | 2–3×/week (≥4–6 sessions over 2 weeks) | Increasing | Decreasing | Bridge to heavy lifting |
| **Muscular Strength** | MS | Base 1 (possibly spillover into early base 2) | 2–3×/week | **Heaviest** | Lowest (e.g., 4–6) | Build neuromuscular force |
| **Strength Maintenance** | SM | Base 2 through peak (and possibly race wk 2 of back-to-back A races) | **1×/week**, low-stress | Moderate | Moderate | Maintain MS gains without compromising on-bike work |

Friel's emphasis: *"Trying to do very demanding workouts in the weight room and on the bike concurrently will result in excessive fatigue and poor training in both categories."*

**Critical timing rule**: Complete MS *before* starting hard bike training of base 3. Maximum 4 back-to-back weeks of MS.

#### 4.2.4 Weekly Routine Design — Friel's Sequencing Logic

**Workout type categories:**
- Group rides / races
- Weight lifting
- **Breakthrough (BT) workouts** — *"workouts that help you break through to a new level of fitness. Every workout you do that results in physical stress and produces fatigue is a breakthrough workout."*
- Recovery rides / days off

**Breakthrough sequencing rules:**
- 24–48 hours of low intensity *before* and *after* each BT.
- Coming to a BT tired = unproductive (fatigue not relieved within 48 hours).
- **Maximum BTs per week**: young (20s–early 30s) advanced riders may handle 4. Slow-recovery / older / less experienced riders should do fewer.
- *"The most common mistake athletes make is not allowing enough recovery time between BT workouts."*

**Group ride placement:**
- **Build periods only**: 1–2 group rides per week (race-like for road race/crit/stage race riders).
- **Base 1 and 2**: avoid group rides at race pace. *"Save that for the base 3 and build periods."* If you ride with a group, make it strictly aerobic endurance.
- **Base 3**: 1 group ride per week max, "rather subdued."
- Cap: in build period, avoid back-to-back fast group rides on weekend.

**"Christmas Stars" warning:** *"Riders who don't have such patience [in base 1/2]…achieve peak race fitness by midwinter and go downhill from there. By late in the race season they are often burnt out."*

**Weight lifting day placement:**
- AA/MT/MS phases (2–3×/week): days separated by 2–3 days recovery (e.g., Mon+Thu, Mon+Fri, Tue+Fri). **NOT** Mon+Sat (only 1 day recovery).
- SM phase (1×/week): any convenient day.
- During MS, the day after a weight session may need to be a light recovery ride for some riders.

#### 4.2.5 Stage Race Training — "Crash Training"

> *"I call this method of closely spaced workouts over several days 'crash training.' That name is intended to imply a risk. You're likely to crash and burn by doing this."*

Mechanism: simulate back-to-back race-day stress, then ample recovery for adaptation.
Sample progression in build period:
- Week 1–2: 2 hard BT days back-to-back, then recovery
- Week 3–4: 3 consecutive BT days, then recovery
- Week 5–6: 4 consecutive BT days, then recovery

Each session simulates the actual stage type (TT, road race, criterium).

**Limits**:
- Most advanced riders: max **4 consecutive BT days** before extended recovery
- Some athletes: max 3
- **3–5 days rest** after a crash cycle (mandatory)
- Most vulnerable: novices, juniors, masters/seniors, any rider who handles cumulative stress poorly

Friel's reward note: properly executed crash training can produce fitness "far above where it was before the crash period."

#### 4.2.6 Weekly Volume Distribution (Tables 8.2, 8.3, 8.4)
- Daily hours or TSS distribution depends on weekly total (Tables 8.3 / 8.4).
- For *advanced* riders: many recovery days are normal. *"This doesn't mean going fast and hard all the time."*
- Friel suggests TSS over hours: blends frequency + duration + intensity in one number.
- Weight lifting TSS: not mandatory to track but can be estimated (see Ch. 12 §6.4 below).

#### 4.2.7 Missed Workouts — Recovery Protocols

| Workouts Missed | Recovery Action |
|---|---|
| 1–3 days | Continue plan, no significant change. If a BT was missed, rearrange — but never back-to-back BTs. |
| 4–6 days (lifestyle, not illness) | Treat as a R&R period. Reduce the current period by 1 week. If still desynchronized, reduce the next period by 1 week. Or: shorten peak from 2 weeks to 1. |
| 4–6 days (illness) | Treat as **>6 days** (see below) — body chemistry needs more time. |
| 1–2 weeks missed | If in build, return to base 3 for 2–4 weeks; if in base 2/3, return to previous base period; if in base 1, repeat base 1. Stay until **efficiency factor matches pre-illness levels**. Then repeat last hard-training week before interruption. Eventually drop build 2 or first week of peak. **Always complete the full base period.** |
| >2 weeks missed | If in build, return to base 3 and start over. If in base, back up 1 period. Omissions priority: peak week 1 → build 2 → build 1 (always complete base). If peak was missed: continue to race week as planned. If build 2 lost (2–3 wks): repeat as much of build 1 as possible; peak = 1 week. |

Illness recovery rule: re-enter at **zones 1 and 2 only**, short-to-medium duration, until EF (efficiency factor, see workout AE2) confirms aerobic fitness restoration.

### 4.3 Planning Alternatives (Ch. 9)

#### 4.3.1 The Four Principles Always Apply
Regardless of periodization model: **Progressive Overload, Specificity, Reversibility, Individuality.** The dictum *"workouts must become increasingly like your A-priority race as race day gets closer"* is non-negotiable.

#### 4.3.2 Linear Periodization Modifications

**(a) 3-Week Mesocycles for Slow Recovery (Table 9.1)** — base/build periods reduced to 3 weeks. Base 3 and Build 2 repeated to maintain total training time. Base = 12 weeks, Build = 9 weeks (vs. 8 for fast-recovery).

**(b) 9-Day Week** (Table 9.2) — 2 R-days after each hard day. 6 hard days per 18 days vs. 5 per 14 in a 7-day week. Solves slow-recovery problem. Downside: conflicts with standard work schedules; best for retired athletes. (Detailed in Friel's *Fast After 50*.)

**(c) Time-Limited Periodization** — for time-constrained riders. Keep the higher annual hours/TSS as written, but cap weekly volume at maximum available (e.g., 15 hours). Don't downgrade the annual target — *"the training load…simply won't be great enough in most weeks to produce an increase in fitness."* Optional add-on: increase intensity of one workout per high-cap week by one zone.

**(d) Inverse Weeks Periodization** — invert the weekly volume progression within a mesocycle (week 1 = biggest, week 3 = smallest). Useful for athletes who get cumulatively fatigued by the standard increasing-volume pattern.

**(e) Reverse Linear Periodization** — base period is HIGH intensity / LOW duration; build period is LOW intensity / HIGH volume.
- **Use case**: long, relatively low-intensity events (centuries, gran fondos, long TTs, long stage races) where the race is mostly at/below AnT.
- **Common motivation**: winter weather restrictions → short indoor workouts at intensity in base, long outdoor rides in summer build.
- **Limitation**: violates Friel's race-specificity principle for *short, high-intensity races* (crits, road races). For these, do NOT use reverse linear.

#### 4.3.3 Nonlinear Periodization

**(a) Undulating Periodization** — week-to-week alternation between high-duration and high-intensity weeks. *"There aren't many endurance athletes using this model, but I've seen it work well for a few."* (Common in weight lifting.) Pattern (Tables 9.3, 9.4):
- Early season (12 weeks): 2 weeks high-duration, 1 week high-intensity (per 3-week block) — base focus
- Late season (12 weeks): 2 weeks high-intensity, 1 week high-volume — build focus
- Critical rule: high-duration weeks = low intensity; high-intensity weeks = low duration. **Never mix both in the same workout.**

**(b) Block Periodization** — Vladimir Issurin (Russian sports scientist), 1980s.
- *"Created for truly elite athletes."*
- Each "block" focuses on a **single dominant ability**; a secondary ability is *maintained* (at half the frequency).
- The athlete progresses to the next block only when the dominant ability is well established.
- **Why elite-only**: pro/elite riders have established fitness across all abilities; linear training "spreads the stress too thin" for tiny gains. Cited study: block athletes achieved equal fitness in **half the weeks** vs. linear.
- **Why NOT for intermediates/novices**: their other abilities will erode rapidly during focused blocks. Block periodization is **not suggested** for podium-aspirants below national/world-championship level.
- Friel uses "early" / "late" in the block sequencing rather than base 1/2/3 etc. (Tables 9.5, 9.6).

**(c) "Random Training"** — Friel admits some highly advanced athletes effectively train randomly because they have an unwritten plan in their heads. For everyone else: random training is "loosey-goosey" and produces mediocre racing.

#### 4.3.4 The Simple Plan (Friel's Beginner-Friendly Distillation)

> *"Periodization can be boiled down to one simple sentence: 'The closer you get to race day, the more like the race your workouts must become.'"*

The Simple Plan:
1. Lay out a standard weekly schedule that fits your lifestyle.
2. Repeat it for 2–3 weeks, then 3–5 days R&R.
3. In the **base period** (~10–12 weeks): gradually lengthen workouts, intensity mostly zones 1–2. Develop AE + MF + SS.
4. In the **build period** (~10 weeks): increase race-like intensity, slightly shorten durations; 2–3 race-intensity workouts/week. Include fast group rides / races. Develop ME + AnE + SP.
5. **Taper** by shortening workout durations for 2–3 weeks pre-race (see Ch. 13).

## 5. PART V — STRESS AND RECOVERY (Chapters 10–11)

### 5.1 Training Stress (Ch. 10)

#### 5.1.1 Eustress vs. Distress (Hans Selye)

Friel cites Hans Selye (mid-20th century Austro-Hungarian endocrinologist):
- **Eustress** — healthy, manageable stress → fitness via fatigue/adaptation.
- **Distress** — excessive stress → overtraining.
- Society conflates "stress" with "distress" — Friel uses Selye's distinction throughout.

> *"It's sometimes necessary to flirt with distress in order to produce a high level of performance."*

#### 5.1.2 Risk vs. Reward (Figure 10.1)
Friel's analogy: like investing in stocks.
- Low risk = low reward (blue chips ≈ short easy rides → never break down, never improve).
- High risk = high reward (high-volume/high-intensity crash periods → injury OR fitness leap).
- Most serious riders err toward over-investing.

**Avoiding "too much, too soon"** — sports medicine term for breakdowns. Common cause: violating dose-density rules.

#### 5.1.3 Overreaching vs. Overtraining

Critical Friel distinction:
- **Overreaching** = "managed overtraining"; tired but recovery still produces adaptation/improvement. Reversible in days.
- **Overtraining (overtraining syndrome)** = fatigue with declining performance/fitness, recovery inadequate, body in distress. *"One of the worst physical conditions an athlete can experience."*

How long can an athlete remain overreached before overtraining? Variable:
- Young riders: several weeks.
- Senior riders / novices: <3 weeks possibly.

> *"Most self-professed overtrained riders are actually overreached."*

**Friel's Overtraining → Overreaching Progression (Table 10.2, paraphrased order from text):**
- **Stage 1 — Acute Fatigue** — needs ≤24 hours recovery, no performance loss → normal training.
- **Stage 2 — Functional Overreaching** — needs 24–48+ hours recovery, slight short-term performance decline → planned R&R restores performance.
- **Stage 3 — Non-functional Overreaching / Early Overtraining** — performance declines persist despite rest → must back off significantly.
- **Stage 4 — Overtraining Syndrome** — long-term performance decline, weeks-to-months recovery, possibly career-ending.

**Symptoms of overtraining (Table 10.1, partial paraphrase)**: poor performance, deep fatigue, listlessness, grumpiness, low motivation, appetite changes (paradoxically: hungrier than normal OR loss of appetite — author notes the contradiction reflects progression and source), sleep changes, immune compromise.

Many overtraining symptoms overlap with **chronic fatigue syndrome, Lyme disease, early infectious mononucleosis** — Friel recommends physician differential.

#### 5.1.4 Deliberate Overreaching

> *"Essentially, overreaching is managed overtraining."*

Mechanism: high training load + constant attention to response. The single most important daily question: *"How do I feel today?"* If "tired" for several consecutive days → recover NOW, regardless of where the plan says you are.

> *"The cure for greed is patience."*

### 5.2 Fatigue, Recovery, and Adaptation (Ch. 11)

#### 5.2.1 Recovery and Adaptation — Distinct Processes

Friel explicitly states (Ch. 11):
> *"So far I have been referring to recovery and adaptation as essentially the same process. This is likely not so."*

Adaptation (the body's structural physiological rebuilding) **may take longer than recovery** (the felt sense of being rested). The implication is critical: artificially shortening recovery (e.g., aggressive recovery aids that mask fatigue) may *cut adaptation short*.

#### 5.2.2 Time to Adapt
- Generally **~48 hours** after a high-stress workout (muscle protein synthesis, DOMS dissipation, central system adaptation).
- **20s riders**: fastest.
- **50s+**: ~2× longer.
- **Novices**: longer than intermediates; intermediates longer than advanced.
- Friel's worked example: A 25 y/o cat 1 rider may need 36 hours; a 2nd–3rd year masters rider may need 60 hours.

#### 5.2.3 Recovery Detractors (Research-Backed Surprises)
- **Vitamin C and E antioxidant supplements** — reduce fitness benefits of adaptation. *"Eat a healthy diet rich in micronutrients—vitamins, minerals, and phytochemicals—and avoid supplements altogether."*
- **Ice on tired/sore muscles**: delays removal of creatine kinase; reduces muscle glycogen replenishment by ~30% in iced leg. *Use ice only for injuries.*
- **NSAIDs (ibuprofen)**: inhibit positive muscular adaptations of exercise and delay injury healing. **Avoid except for extreme injury.**

#### 5.2.4 Measuring Fatigue — Friel's Methods

**(a) Subjective: Fatigue Scale 0–10.** A score ≥7 implies need for recovery day.

**(b) Heart Rate Variability (HRV).** Tools (HRV4Training [phone camera], chest straps, finger/wrist sensors). Measures autonomic nervous system balance:
- High sympathetic dominance → low HRV → high fatigue
- High parasympathetic dominance → high HRV → recovered
- Best measured first thing in morning, standardized conditions.

**(c) Muscle Glycogen Content** — MuscleSound device (clinic-priced). Not practical for most riders yet.

**(d) Apps**: HRV4Training, Restwise (assesses 11 markers including resting HR, weight, sleep, hydration, appetite, muscle soreness).

**(e) Morning Warnings (Table 11.1) — Friel's Primary Tool**

Place the list at bedside. Each morning, before getting up, mark yes/no for each warning. Yes-score totals:
- **<7 = normal training day**
- **≥7 = recovery needed** (active or passive)

Friel emphasizes:
- One severe warning (e.g., sore throat) → recovery regardless of total.
- **Never zero warnings every day** = not training hard enough.
- Frequent 7+ scores every few days = appropriate training stress.

#### 5.2.5 Recovery and Adaptation — Daily Lifestyle

> *"Never stand if you can lean, never lean if you can sit, and never sit if you can lie down."*

Three **natural recovery aids** (in priority order):

**(1) Sleep — #1**
> *"Sleep is the most powerful form of recovery and fitness enhancement there is."*
- Anabolic hormones released during sleep (growth hormone, testosterone) rebuild tissues.
- *"Forget about trying to 'catch up' on sleep on the weekends. The body doesn't operate that way."*
- More sleep = #1 thing besides riding hard.

**(2) Nutrition — #2**
- Whole foods, micronutrient-rich (vitamins, minerals, phytochemicals).
- Junk food = nearly no micronutrients (processing strips them; manufacturer re-additions are inferior).
- Avoid supplements.
- *"There is no evidence that supplements contribute to improved performance or prevent illness."*
- Sports drinks/bars/gels = junk food; only acceptable for on-bike convenience.

**(3) Hydration — #3**
- Drink to thirst — no need to weigh-in/out, except possibly for athletes >60 (decreased thirst sensation).
- Water is best.
- **Coffee and alcohol** do NOT cause dehydration (research contradicts the old belief).
- Carbohydrate during ride: only needed for rides >90 min for high-carb-diet riders; chronically low-carb riders may go several hours with little/none.

#### 5.2.6 Alternative Recovery Aids (Friel's evaluation)

| Aid | Evidence | Friel's Assessment |
|---|---|---|
| **Compression garments** | Mixed studies | Performance benefit minimal; recovery benefit possible (perhaps placebo) |
| **Pneumatic compression devices** (NormaTec, Recovery Boots, RevitaPump) | Mostly positive | Beneficial; could be placebo; pricey |
| **Massage** | Mixed (mostly subjective benefits: well-being, anxiety, mood; less measurable physiological benefit) | Highly individual |
| Hyperbaric oxygen, EMS, ultrasound, hot/cold immersion, music, meditation, leg elevation, foam roller, stretching, Jacuzzi, chocolate milk | "All have been demonstrated in one or more studies—but not in all" | Try and find what works for you |

> *"The only ones you must apply consistently are sleeping, eating, and drinking. Don't ever skip those."*

#### 5.2.7 Strategic Recovery — Periodization of Recovery

**Hard-Easy Day Patterns:**
- Standard: hard-easy-hard-easy-hard-easy + 1 day choice
- Slow-recovery option: very hard-easy-easy-hard-easy-hard-easy

> *"The harder the hard workouts, the easier—and longer—the recovery break must be."*

**Base/Build Period R&R Block:**
- Fast-recovery: ~3 weeks training + 3–5 days R&R
- Slow-recovery: ~2 weeks training + 3–5 days R&R (or up to 7)
- R&R workouts: short, easy rides (workout AE1)
- After 2–4 days, when "legs and attitude feel normal," do a cautious harder workout with a few short intervals appropriate to the upcoming period.
- Test fitness on the first day after full recovery.

**B-race scheduling**: Time R&R to end immediately before the B-race (e.g., start R&R Wednesday for a Saturday B-race).
**C-race scheduling**: Treat as hard workout. Pattern with 3-day R&R earlier in week: rest-rest-rest-test-rest-race-rest.

#### 5.2.8 Transition Period — Post-A-Race Recovery

- **First A-race transition**: very short (2–3 days, possibly up to 7).
- **Mid-season**: slightly longer.
- **Last A-race of the year**: 2–6 weeks (sometimes more for highly stressed seasons).
- **No structured training**; optional crosstraining; **no weight lifting** (unless transitions are very short during the season + going into base 3 with MS, in which case MT/MS may begin).

**Cyclocross consideration**: if doing ’cross after road season, treat as an extension of a shortened road season (avoid all-year racing burnout). Limit: 1–2 A road races + 1–2 A ’cross races OR B/C-only ’cross.

#### 5.2.9 Recovery on Demand

> *"When you feel the need for recovery, you back off from training for a few days regardless of what the plan calls for."*

Only suitable for athletes truly in tune with their bodies. Most riders are not. Default to scheduled R&R unless you have demonstrated self-awareness.

**Periodization on Demand** — similar concept: when training objectives for a period are achieved, move to the next period regardless of weeks elapsed.

#### 5.2.10 Overcompensation (the physiological mechanism)
During recovery from a hard workout, the body strengthens:
- Muscle strength
- Heart stroke volume (blood per beat)
- Capillary density in muscles
- Blood volume (oxygen carrying capacity)
- Aerobic enzymes
- Glycogen stores

> *"The biggest mistake you can make is to destroy an easy day or a recovery week by training hard through it."*

> *"Another common mistake shared by serious riders is to make easy-day workouts slightly too hard. These days MUST be easy."*

## 6. PART VI — THE COMPETITIVE EDGE (Chapters 12–14)

### 6.1 Muscular Force Training (Ch. 12)

#### 6.1.1 The Power Formula

> *"P = F × v"*

Where in cycling:
- **F (force)** = pressure applied to the pedal (torque)
- **v (velocity)** = cadence

> *"Riding fast is nothing more than turning a high gear with a high cadence."*

Ch. 12 is exclusively about the **force component**; cadence/skill is addressed elsewhere.

#### 6.1.2 Nervous System Contribution to Force (three mechanisms)
1. **Muscle Recruitment** — number of fibers recruited from the available pool.
2. **Muscle Coordination** — recruiting the *right* fibers when needed; suppressing inappropriate ones (which waste energy).
3. **Muscle Synchronization** — timing/sequencing of fiber firing. ≈12 muscle groups must fire correctly in <1 second per crank revolution.

> *"Neuromuscular training is the most effective form of physical preparation for high-performance racing you can do off the bike."*

#### 6.1.3 Should YOU Lift? (Friel's targeting)

**Strongly recommended for:**
- **Women**
- **Novices**
- **Skinny ectomorphs**
- **Riders over 50** — *"Older endurance athletes' race performances are often limited by a loss of muscle mass."* Also rebuilds bone (cyclists known to have weak bones).

**Less critical for:**
- 20-something cyclists who race at high level (innate physiology, less marginal gain)
- Young males already over-muscled in upper body
- Riders who simply don't enjoy gym work

**For novices specifically**: Skip the MS phase in year 1 (use AA/MT only) — risk of injury too high.

**Juniors under 16**: extra caution required (growth plates still developing); light loads, focus on technique, fun-driven.

#### 6.1.4 Weight Lifting Guidelines

**Gym choices**: health club, YMCA, school, pay-as-you-go, or home gym (a few dumbbells suffice).

**Free weights vs. machines**: Free weights strengthen small balancing muscles (core), but riskier (drops/falls). Beginners → start with machines, transition to free weights.

**When to lift**:
- Best: after riding, or on a day off the bike.
- If before another workout: lift early morning, ride late in the day (several hours separation).
- During MS phase: even the day after may be too soon for some.

**Sets and reps**:
- Each exercise: several reps = 1 set. Typical 3 sets per exercise.
- Recover between sets per phase prescription (longer recovery = more strength focus).

**Set progression**: complete all sets of one exercise before moving to the next (or "superset" two non-overlapping muscle group exercises in light gyms).

**Load determination — 1RM (one-repetition max)**:
- Direct 1RM test is risky → use Table 12.1 (multi-rep estimation).
- Example: 9 squats @ 80 lb. Table 12.1 factor for 9 reps = 0.775. Predicted 1RM = 80 ÷ 0.775 = 103 lb.
- Simpler: guess and adjust based on prescribed reps.

**Spotter**: required for risky high-load exercises (squats).

**Concentric vs. eccentric contraction**:
- Concentric: muscle shortens as load is lifted.
- Eccentric: muscle lengthens as load is lowered (e.g., setting weight down).
- Eccentric: heavier loads possible but greater strain — set heavy loads down cautiously.

**Speed of movement — Ballistic ("explosive") lifts**:
- Concentric: as fast as possible.
- Eccentric: slow (≈ 1:2 time ratio).
- **Loads ≤50% of 1RM** to reduce injury risk.
- Highly risky — start with very light loads, master technique first.

**Simulating pedaling**: Multi-joint exercises (squats, leg presses, step-ups, lunges, dead lifts) > single-joint (knee extensions). The hip, knee, and ankle must extend simultaneously.

**Multi-joint principle**: matches pedaling biomechanics.

**Balancing muscle strength (agonist/antagonist)**:
- Quadriceps (agonist for leg extension) can outpower hamstrings (antagonist).
- Add leg curls to strengthen hamstrings — protect against strains, especially in women (greater quad/hamstring imbalance).
- Equal strength NOT the goal; quads will be ~2× stronger; just avoid extreme imbalance.

**Workout spacing**: e.g., Mon+Fri (2-day or 3-day separation). NOT Mon+Tue (consecutive). NOT Mon+Sat (single day separation after weekend).

**Warm-up**: (1) 10–15 min on stationary bike gradually increasing load; (2) light reps of first exercise.
**Cooldown**: 5 min easy bike spinning at high cadence to restore pedaling neuromuscular firing.

**Exercise order**: heaviest loads first (freshest), alternate muscle groups (e.g., don't do squats then step-ups back-to-back).

**Starting a new phase**: conservative loads for first 1–2 sessions of any new (higher-load) phase.

**Lifting to failure**: Friel advises **against** for cyclists; stop 1–2 reps short. Marginal extra strength not worth injury risk.

**Number of exercises**: minimum that achieves results — limited time.

**Muscle mass**: avoid bulking (counterproductive for cycling). Limit serious lifting to a few weeks (MS phase), then maintenance.

**Food and strength** (research-backed):
- Within 3 hours post-lift: **10–25 g of leucine-rich protein**.
- Approx. 10 g protein + 1000 mg leucine sources: 2 medium boiled eggs, 1.5 oz cheddar, 10 oz milk.
- Body size + workout difficulty determine the exact dose.

#### 6.1.5 The Exercise List (Friel's recommended menu — choose 1–2 per session)

**Traditional Hip-Knee-Ankle Extension Lifts** (pick at least one):

| Exercise | Figure | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Double-Leg Squat** | 12.1 | Most common for experienced riders. Feet ~7–8 in (18–20 cm) apart (pedal width), toes forward. Squat until thighs ~parallel to floor (2 o'clock pedal knee bend). Back straight, head up, eyes forward. Wear weight belt during MS. |
| **Single-Leg Squat** | 12.2 | Lighter loads → safer. Mimics one-leg-at-a-time pedaling. Rear foot on knee-high bench. Twice as many sets needed. |
| **Dead Lift** | 12.3 | Highest injury risk (back overuse) — get expert assistance. Light plates can over-bend the knees → use racks/adaptors. Feet pedal-width apart. |
| **Step-Up** | 12.4 | Bench 13–15 in (33–38 cm). Simulates one pedal at 6 o'clock / other at 12 o'clock. Raised knee slightly below hip joint. NOT higher (counterproductive at heavy loads). |
| **Lunge** | 12.5 | Step distance matters — too short = can't get low; too long = strains rear leg's hip flexor. Alternate legs until reps complete with EACH leg. |
| **Leg Press** | 12.6 | Safest leg extension lift (machine, no balance). Heaviest loads possible. Feet pedal-width, pointing straight up. Lowered position = 2 o'clock pedal angle. Knees stay in line with feet. |

**Antagonist Exercise**:
- **Leg Curl** | Fig. 12.7 (machine) or 12.8 (elastic band) | Lighter loads than the extension exercises. Hamstrings protect against quad imbalance.

**Core Exercises** (Friel: torso = transmission for legs/arms; weak core = unstable hip rocking at low cadence/high effort, also LBP risk):

| Exercise | Figure | Protocol |
|---|---|---|
| **Front Plank** | 12.9 | Straight line head→toes; elbows under shoulders, shoulder-width. Hold 30 sec. 3 sets, 1-min rest. |
| **Side Plank** | 12.10 | Forearm + lower foot, straight line. Hold 30 sec each side, 3 sets per side. |
| **Front Plank with Rows** | 12.11 | Advanced. Push-up position with dumbbells. Slowly alternate raising each weight to shoulder = 1 rep. 5 reps per set, 3 sets. |

**Ballistic / Explosive Lift**:
- **Power Clean** | Fig. 12.12 | Olympic lift. Bar from shins to shoulders in one explosive movement; slow return. Master with broomstick → empty bar → loaded bar. **Always ≤50% 1RM.** Not for weight-lifting beginners.

#### 6.1.6 Weight Lifting Periodization (Table 12.2, 12.3)

Phases progress: AA → MT → MS → SM (race period: no lifting).

| Phase | Sessions/wk | Sets × Reps | Load (% 1RM) | Recovery Between Sets | Purpose |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **AA (Anatomical Adaptation)** | 2–3 | 2–3 sets × 20–30 reps | Light (~30–40%) | 1 min | Master movements |
| **MT (Muscular Transition)** | 2–3 | 3 sets × 10–15 reps | Moderate (~50–60%) | 1–2 min | Bridge to heavy lifting |
| **MS (Muscular Strength)** | 2–3 (early), then 1×/wk later | 3–4 sets × 4–6 reps | Heavy (~80–90%) | 3–5 min | Build force |
| **SM (Strength Maintenance)** | 1×/wk | 1–2 sets × 6–12 reps | Moderate-high (~70–80%) | 1–2 min | Maintain MS gains |

(Specific table values are referenced in Table 12.4 in the book and approximate values are commonly used; Friel emphasizes the **principle of progressive load with decreasing reps**.)

#### 6.1.7 Functional Force Training (On-Bike Alternative)
For riders who can't/won't go to the gym. **Caution: high knee strain — riders prone to knee injuries should avoid.**

**MF1: Flat Force Reps** (also workout MF1 in Appendix B)
- High gear (e.g., 53×14), near-stopped start.
- 6–8 total pedal strokes (3–4 per leg), extreme force, **stay seated**.
- First strokes ~50 rpm or lower.
- 1 set first time → max 2 sets initially; 3–4 min recovery between sets in low gear.

**MF2: Hill Force Reps**
- Requires prior 4+ MF1 sessions and confirmed no knee complaints.
- Hill: 30–50 yd/m, ~8% grade, light traffic, safe U-turn at top/bottom.
- High gear (e.g., 53×16). Brief balanced near-stop at base.
- 6–12 total pedal strokes per set. **Stay seated.** No standing.
- Up to 3 sets; 3–5 min recovery in low gear between sets.
- **Stop at any sign of knee tenderness.**
- Max 2×/week, ≥48 hours separation.

#### 6.1.8 TSS for Weight Lifting (Friel's two methods, both imperfect)

**Method 1 — RPE**: Multiply session RPE (0–10) × 10 → estimated TSS. E.g., MS session @ RPE 9 → ~90 TSS.

**Method 2 — Tonnage**: For each exercise: load × reps = set tonnage. Sum all sets. Divide by 2,000. Multiply by 10 → estimated TSS.

Note: Core exercises don't fit easily into tonnage. Friel concedes the method is "kludgy."

### 6.2 Tapering to Race (Ch. 13)

#### 6.2.1 The Central Postulate of Tapering

> *"The best predictor of race success is not fitness, but form."*

**Form** = race readiness + freshness. Freshness = absence of fatigue. The only effective way to remove fatigue is to reduce training load.

**What to reduce**: **DURATION (not intensity)**.
**What to maintain**: **race-like intensity** (frequent but brief).

> *"What tapering means, in a nutshell, is a reduction in workout duration resulting in an increase, percentage-wise, of your training load intensity."*

#### 6.2.2 The Two-Period Taper Structure

**Peak Period (1–2 weeks)** — final adaptation, fatigue shedding, form generation.
- **2-week peak**: late-season races, very long races, stage races.
- **3- to 5-day peak**: highly fit/experienced fast-recovery riders; early-season races; <1-hour A-races (crits, short TTs).

**Race Period (typically 1 week)** — final freshness.

#### 6.2.3 Peak Period Routine (Table 13.1 example)

Pattern: **every-3rd-day high-intensity, 2 days easy recovery between**.

| Day Type | Workout |
|---|---|
| Hard / race-like | Workout simulating A-race demands (ME for TT, AnE for road race/crit, SP for sprinter) — 80% of build 2 duration |
| Easy day 1 | Zones 1–2; ~50% normal duration |
| Easy day 2 | Zones 1–2; ~50% normal duration |

Volume drops 30–50% from build 2 by end of peak.

Optional C-priority race during peak: yes — but **shorter than goal race**, abandon if necessary, not back-to-back with high-intensity day.

Use the bike(s) you'll race on. Get dialed in.

#### 6.2.4 Race Week (Table 13.2 example)

Workouts shift to **90-second intervals at race intensity** with 3-min recoveries, very short sessions.

Pattern depends on whether race is Saturday or Sunday:
- 3 or 4 interval workouts in the week.
- Number of intervals **decreases** through the week.
- **Easiest day = 2 days before the race** (day off or short low-intensity ride; may include travel to venue).
- **Day before the race**: very brief, includes some race-like intensity.

**Sprinters' interval variation**: 90 seconds with two parts — sustained high effort (like a leadout man / corner exit) + final all-out sprint.

**Heart rate is NOT useful for these intervals** — too brief; lag prevents accurate zone targeting. **Use power**.

Travel: arrive earlier if venue is significantly hotter or higher altitude than usual training.

After race week, record in training diary: travel details, sleep, when/what eaten, mental/physical stressors, equipment selection, pre-race warm-up. Repeat winning patterns; adjust failing ones.

#### 6.2.5 When Tapering Goes Wrong
Common adjustments for next A-race:
- **Flat on race day** → shorter taper OR harder workouts in last 3 days.
- **Tired on race day** → lower total training load in final week.

> *"Even Eddy Merckx didn't win the road world championship on his first try. But he did win it the next time."*

### 6.3 The Training Diary (Ch. 14)

#### 6.3.1 Diary vs. Log

- **Log** = bunch of numbers; statistical record.
- **Diary** = data + thoughts, feelings, training assessments, reactions.

> *"For the well-informed rider, [a diary] is much like having a coach."*

**Formats**: paper notebook, electronic calendar, TrainingPeaks.com (Friel's primary recommendation), other web-based.

#### 6.3.2 Planning with a Diary
Store ATP in the diary. Update weekly. End of each week (typically Sunday): write next week's workouts using **Appendix B workout codes** (AE1, MF1, ME2, etc.) + weight lifting phase + planned duration/TSS.

> *"It's not the plan that builds confidence, but rather the act of planning."*

#### 6.3.3 What to Record (Six Categories — record minimum needed)

1. **Morning Warnings** — daily score from Table 11.1.
2. **Basic Workout Data** — date, time of day, planned workout, actual workout, course/venue, duration, TSS, equipment.
3. **Performance-Specific Data** — phenotype-relevant (Table 14.1 organizes by climber/sprinter/TT). All-rounders pick/choose.
4. **Physical Notes** — workout completion grade (A–F); how it felt; recovery status; future adjustments.
5. **Mental Notes** — emotions, satisfaction, motivation, training concerns. *"The best indicator of how you are progressing on a daily basis is likely to be your emotions and gut feelings."*
6. **Miscellaneous Notes** — travel, work stress, illness/injury, family, equipment changes, diet changes, race info.

> *"Don't record more data than you are likely to scrutinize later on."*

#### 6.3.4 Training Analysis — the Three Question Categories

Categorize observations into:
- **Fitness** — workout performance-specific data + physical/mental notes. Phenotype mix progressing for the A-race?
- **Fatigue** — morning warnings + physical/mental notes around hard rides. Recovered enough? Frequent 7+ scores → working at limit.
- **Form** — last 2–3 weeks pre-race. Race readiness × freshness balance.

Primary analytical tool: **Performance Management Chart (TrainingPeaks.com)** — Andrew Coggan, PhD.

#### 6.3.5 Race Analysis
Wait 1 day after the race before doing in-depth analysis (avoid emotional decisions).

**Critical questions**:
- Did you achieve your race goal? If not, why not?
- (Criterium/road race) Key episodes — how did you manage/initiate/respond?
- (Time trial) Was your pacing as planned?
- Unanticipated factors (equipment, weather, mechanicals, crashes, road, nutrition, etc.) — preventable? Properly handled?
- What would you do differently for similar races?

**Race planning by event type**:
- **Criterium**: prepare for frequent early attacks; later strategic attacks. Be physically AND mentally ready for episodes.
- **Time Trial**: usually a pacing problem. Break course into segments by terrain/distance. First segment = controlled effort. Pacing rule: *"You can't win a time trial in the first few minutes, but you can lose it."*
- **Road Race**: tactical moves around hills, corners, wind. Plan responses + initiations. Goal: nothing in race is unexpected unless you/team initiated it.

#### 6.3.6 Trends to Spot (most common causes of negative trends)
- **#1 most common cause of plateau/decline**: missed workouts → inconsistency.
- **#2 most common cause**: training density too high → fatigue limits power despite training. Solution: more recovery.
- Other: nutrition, sleep, stress, etc.

## 7. EPILOGUE

> *"Drive is still the ultimate determiner of high performance. In the final analysis, it comes down to how badly you want it."*

**Nature × Nurture** framing: many born with elite genes never had cycling opportunity; many love-the-sport riders never had the genes but persist anyway ("completers, not competers" — both are OK). Friel positions himself as "fair amount of nurture, lacking nature."

**Two final practical edicts**:

**Flexibility**: change your plan when needed (especially when tired); never blend two contradictory methodologies (don't mix Friel's workouts with another author's intensity zones — *"that won't work"*).

**Meticulousness**: once a workout is chosen, follow it precisely. *"For example, an interval workout is composed of two parts: 'work intervals' and 'recovery intervals.' The intensities and durations of both of these components are spelled out in the workouts. Maintaining those intensities and durations is critical to reaping the intended rewards of the workout."*

Friel's recommendation for further reading: his own *The Power Meter Handbook* (introductory), then Allen & Coggan, *Training and Racing with a Power Meter* (advanced).

## 9. APPENDIX B — Workouts (Functional Catalog)

> *"Workout intensities are described using power and heart rate… The power meter is the preferred intensity gauge for most workouts. There are exceptions as described below."*

All workouts assume: warm-up + main set + cooldown. The more intense the main set, the longer the warm-up.

### 9.1 Aerobic Endurance Workouts (AE)

#### AE1 — Recovery
- **NOT** marked on ATP but used routinely between BTs.
- Intensity: HR zone 1 OR ≤55% FTP; RPE 2–3.
- Shorter than average ride.
- Flat course, small chainring, comfortably high cadence.
- Can be done on trainer/rollers; alternative: low-key crosstraining (XC skiing on flat, health-club machines).
- **Novice/intermediate alternative**: take day fully off instead.
- May combine with a Speed Skills workout.

#### AE2 — Aerobic Threshold (AeT)
- Use HR early in base period to gauge intensity.
- AeT HR estimate: FTHR − 30 bpm, ± 2 bpm.
- Course: flat to gently rolling OR trainer.
- Duration: **1 to 4 hours** at AeT HR.
- Race-length match: TT/crit goal → 1–1.5 hrs at AeT; 3-hr road race goal → 3 hrs at AeT.
- **Efficiency Factor (EF) Computation**: EF = Normalized Power (AeT segment) / Average HR (AeT segment). Rising EF = improving aerobic threshold.
- After EF plateaus for several sessions, switch from HR to power gauge: maintain NP plateau power ± 5 W.
- **Year-round workout**: emphasized in base, maintained later (~half-frequency).
- Also serves as the **T1 (FAeT) test** — see §9.6.

#### AE3 — Intensive Endurance
- Boosts aerobic endurance + muscular endurance.
- Flat to gently rolling, ≥1 hour.
- Mostly HR zone 2 OR power zone 3.
- RPE 4–6 (intensive endurance range).
- **Stay seated on hills.**
- Trainer alternative: frequently shift gears to simulate hills.

### 9.2 Muscular Force Workouts (MF)

#### MF1 — Flat Force Reps
- Mostly prep period; can be done any time.
- After warm-up: shift to high gear (e.g., 53×14), nearly stop.
- Accelerate with only **6–8 total pedal strokes** (3–4 per leg). Extreme force per stroke. **Stay seated.**
- First strokes ~50 rpm or lower; cadence rises with speed.
- 1 set on first session; 2 sets if knees OK. 3–4 min recovery in low gear between sets.

#### MF2 — Hill Force Reps
- Done AFTER at least 4 MF1 sessions with no knee complaints.
- Hill: 30–50 yd/m, ~8% grade; light/no traffic; safe U-turn capability.
- High gear (e.g., 53×16), brief near-stop at base.
- Stay seated, alternate driving pedals: **6–12 total strokes (3–6 per leg)** per set.
- Up to 3 sets; 3–5 min in low gear for recovery (don't shorten — reduces benefit).
- **Stop at first sign of knee tenderness.**
- Power, not HR, is the gauge — wattage should be very high per stroke.
- Max 2×/week, ≥48 hr separation.

#### MF3 — Hill Repeats
- Hill: 6–8% grade, 20–30 sec climb.
- 3–8 repeats; 2–4 min recovery between.
- Maintain **power zone 5**.
- HR not useful (too short).
- Climb in saddle, minimal upper-body movement. **No standing.**
- Gear allows cadence ≤70 rpm.
- Coast down for recovery.
- **Stop if knee tenderness develops.**
- Max 2×/week, ≥48 hr separation.
- **Avoid if knee-injury-prone.**

### 9.3 Speed Skill Workouts (SS)

#### SS1 — Spin-Ups
- Goal: improve max cadence without bouncing in saddle.
- Flat or slightly downhill, low (easy) gear; OR trainer with light resistance.
- 1 min gradual cadence build to max → hold max for a few seconds → ≥1 min recovery → repeat several times.
- Relax lower legs and feet (especially toes).
- Best done with cadence display on handlebar. HR/power not meaningful.

#### SS2 — Isolated Leg
- Flat / slightly downhill OR trainer with light resistance + foot of resting leg on chair/stool.
- 90% of work with one leg, the other rests.
- Highest cadence possible.
- Switch legs when fatigue sets in.
- Focus: eliminate dead spots at top/bottom of stroke.
- HR/power not meaningful.

#### SS3 — 9-to-3
- Imagine driving pedal forward from 9 o'clock (back) to 3 o'clock (front) without going through 12 o'clock.
- Low gear, comfortable high cadence, low force.
- Targets the most challenging part of pedal stroke (top).

#### SS4 — Fixed-Gear
- Requires fixed-gear bike (1 chainring, 1 cog, no derailleurs, no freewheel — always pedaling, no coasting).
- First few sessions: flat area, no traffic, no stop signs (empty parking lot).
- Short workouts initially.
- **Dangerous until mastered** — avoid busy streets.

#### SS5 — Cornering
- Curbed street, clean surface, 90° turns, low traffic.
- Practice three cornering techniques: lean both bike + body; lean body but keep bike upright; keep body upright but lean bike.
- Various speeds, various approach angles.
- Include some sprint efforts in/out of corners.
- HR/power not important.

#### SS6 — Bumping
- Firm grassy field. Bike-on-bike contact with training partner at slow speed; gradually increase.
- Includes leaning on each other, slightly touching overlapped wheels.

### 9.4 Muscular Endurance Workouts (ME)

#### ME1 — Tempo Intervals
- Mostly flat or 1–3% uphill; OR trainer.
- 3–5 work intervals: 12–20 min each in **HR zone 3 OR power zone 4** (or RPE 5–6).
- Recoveries: 1/4 of work interval (e.g., 4 min recovery after 16-min interval).
- HR lag: timing starts when hard effort begins.
- Recover in zone 1.
- Avoid heavy-traffic roads with stop signs.
- **Stay seated each interval.**

#### ME2 — Cruise Intervals
- Flat or 1–3% uphill OR trainer.
- 3–5 work intervals: 6–12 min each at **AnT** (RPE 7–8; at or slightly below FTP/near FTHR).
- Recovery: 1/4 of work interval duration (e.g., 90 sec after 6 min).
- Total work first session of season: ≤12 min (e.g., 2×6 min). Build gradually to 30–50 min total (e.g., 5×6 min, 5×10 min).
- Cadence: similar to 40 km TT pacing.
- **Key TT-specific workout.**
- Optional variation: alternate every 20–60 sec between normal gear and one harder gear.

#### ME3 — Hill Cruise Intervals
- Like ME2 but on long, low-gradient hill (2–4% grade) OR strong headwind.
- Light traffic, no stop signs.
- **Stay seated; smooth stroke; minimal upper-body motion.**
- Recovery: turn around, descend in zone 1. Longer recovery vs. ME2 BUT slightly higher intensity and more MF benefit.
- Variation: alternate every 30 sec between normal and one harder gear.

#### ME4 — Sweet Spot Intervals
- 2 work intervals on flat or 2–3% uphill.
- Intensity: **0.88–0.93 × FTP** (sweet spot zone).
- Duration: 12–20 min.
- Recovery: 1/4 work interval duration (e.g., 4 min after 16-min interval).
- Start of base period: 12-min intervals; lengthen as adaptation occurs.
- **Stay seated.**

(Note: ME5 and ME6 are referenced in the book's TOC but not in the Appendix B workout catalog as printed — the printed Appendix B contains ME1–ME4. See Inconsistencies §10.)

### 9.5 Anaerobic Endurance Workouts (AnE)

#### AnE1 — Group Ride
- Unstructured; simulates racing.
- Could be a B- or C-priority race.
- Include frequent maximal efforts to **power zone 5 / HR zone 5b+** for several seconds to a few minutes.
- These are the "matches" of racing.
- Analyze post-ride: total time in goal zone.
- **Safety**: traffic + unskilled group riders are risks.
- Power is preferred metric (HR not indicative of performance during surges).

#### AnE2 — VO₂max Intervals
- Flat to rolling course (frequent small hills), no stop signs, light traffic.
- 30 sec to 4 min work intervals; equal-length recoveries in zone 1.
- Cadence: high end of comfort range.
- Build: start ~5 min total work (e.g., 10×30 sec) → 15–20 min total (e.g., 5×3 min, 5×4 min).
- **Intensity: power zone 5; RPE 9** (HR not useful — too short).
- As fitness improves, gradually shorten recoveries to 1/2 work interval.

#### AnE3 — Pyramid Intervals
- Like AnE2 but progression: **1-2-3-4-4-3-2-1 min** (all in power zone 5; RPE 9).
- Recovery: equal to preceding work interval.
- After a few sessions, shorten recoveries (e.g., 1 min after 2-min interval).

#### AnE4 — Hill Intervals
- Hill: 6–8% grade, 2–4 min climb, light traffic, no stop signs.
- 5–7 climbs in power zone 5 → 10–20 min total climbing (e.g., 5×2 min OR 5×4 min).
- Steady intensity throughout climb.
- Cadence: comfortably high.
- RPE 9 if no power meter.
- Recovery: coast down (every 2–4 min start, e.g., 2-min climb + 2-min descent).
- Variation: surge to zone 6 within each work interval.
- **Very hard.** Once/week max; ≥48 hr recovery follow-up.

### 9.6 Sprint Power Workouts (SP)

#### SP1 — Form Sprints
- Early in ride after warm-up.
- 6–10 sprints, 5–10 sec each, on varied terrain (flat, uphill, downhill).
- 3–5 min recovery between.
- Alternate standing and sitting; focus on posture and technique.
- **Hold back slightly on intensity** — this is for form.
- Power zone 6, RPE ~9.
- Best alone (no competing while refining technique).

#### SP2 — Jumps
- Indoor trainer OR road.
- 10–20 total brief surges (2–4 sets of 5).
- Each jump: 8–12 crank revolutions (each leg), high cadence, **maximal power**.
- 1 min recovery between jumps; 5 min between sets.
- Road version: vary terrain + include cornering.

#### SP3 — Group Sprints
- Several 5–15 sec race-like sprints during ride.
- With another rider OR group → simulates racing intensity and tactical maneuvering.
- Designate sprint zones (road signs as markers).
- Use form-sprint techniques BUT maximal power/effort.
- Recovery: several minutes between sprints.

(Note: SP4 is referenced in the book's narrative but Appendix B contains SP1–SP3. See Inconsistencies §10.)

### 9.7 Field Tests (T1–T5)

| Test | Tools | Duration | Computation | Frequency |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **T1: Aerobic Threshold Test** | HRM + power meter | 1–3 hrs (matches race goal duration) at AeT HR ± 2 bpm | EF = NP / Avg HR | Every 6–8 weeks (Appendix B) OR every 3–4 weeks (Sidebar 5.3 — see Inconsistencies §10) |
| **T2: FTP/FTHR Test** | HRM + power meter | 20 min all-out (or 30/8/5) | 20-min: FTP = Avg Power − 5%; FTHR = Avg HR − 5%. 30-min: averages without subtraction. 8-min: Avg P − 10%. 5-min: Avg P − 12% (or −15% per Ch 4 text) | Every 3–4 weeks |
| **T3: Functional Aerobic Capacity Test** | Power meter | 5 min all-out steady | FACP = Average Power | Every 4–6 weeks |
| **T4: Time Trial** | HRM + power meter | 10 km flat-to-1–2% TT on TT bike | Time, Avg HR, Avg Power | Periodic |
| **T5: Sprint Power Test** | Power meter | 8 right-side (16 total) strokes from rolling start | Sprint Power | Periodic |

All field tests preceded by:
- 3–5 days of rest/reduced training.
- 20-min warm-up: 5–10 min progressive accelerations at/above RPE 7 (durations from 2 min down to 10 sec, near-complete recoveries) + 5 min easy.
- Same course every time (or stable trainer).

## 10. Detected Internal Inconsistencies

*Note for the downstream Coach GEM: Resolve these internally based on user context. Friel acknowledges several of these as "art" — and consciously declines to reconcile some. Do not surface to the athlete unless directly relevant.*

### 10.1 The 5-Minute FTP Test Coefficient
- **Chapter 4** (line 982, conceptual narrative): *"I've even used a 5-minute test, subtracting 15 percent."*
- **Chapter 4** (line 1018, prescriptive): *"For the 5-minute test, subtract 12 percent."*

The book contains both −15% and −12% as the 5-min FTP coefficient, without resolution. The 8-min test is consistently −10%, the 20-min test consistently −5%. Default recommendation: use −12% (the prescriptive value), and prefer the 20-min test entirely.

### 10.2 Sprint Power Test — Average vs. Peak Power
- **Sidebar 5.3** (Chapter 5): *"Your **average power output** for the 8 strokes is a measure of your sprint power."*
- **Appendix B T5**: *"Your **peak power output** for the 8 strokes is a measure of your sprint power."*

Average and peak are different metrics. They would yield different absolute numbers but **trend similarly within an athlete**, so the inconsistency doesn't break the test's utility for tracking progress — only for cross-athlete comparison.

### 10.3 AeT Estimation — Three Different Methods Without Preference
Chapter 4 offers all three without ranking:
- ~65% of max heart rate
- 20–40 bpm below AnT
- 30 bpm below AnT (used in workouts AE2 and T1)

For coaching purposes, default to FTHR − 30 bpm, ± 2 bpm range (this is the value used in Friel's actual workout specifications).

### 10.4 T1 Test Frequency
- **Sidebar 5.3** (Chapter 5): test "every 3 to 4 weeks throughout the season."
- **Appendix B T1**: test "at least every 6 to 8 weeks."

For practical purposes: do T1 (FAeT) at the end of each R&R week (3-4 wk cadence in fast-recovery model) — the more frequent interval works in either reading.

### 10.5 Recovery and Adaptation — Same or Different?
- **Chapter 11** (early): *"Recovery and adaptation are, to some extent, the same thing. Both take time and can't be hurried."*
- **Chapter 11** (later): *"So far I have been referring to recovery and adaptation as essentially the same process. This is likely not so."*

The author explicitly hedges. The practical implication: don't artificially accelerate recovery via aids that mask fatigue, as adaptation may be incomplete even when the athlete feels recovered.

### 10.6 Volume-Intensity Ratio
Friel offers his 40/60 (volume/intensity) ratio in both Ch. 3 and Ch. 4, while explicitly stating in both: *"Can I prove that? Is there research that backs this up? No, there isn't. This is only my opinion based on more than 30 years of training athletes of all abilities."*

It is presented as a guideline but is explicitly NOT empirically validated.

### 10.7 Polarized Distribution Terminology
Friel's "polarized 80/20" stuffs the AeT-to-AnT zone into the "above AeT" 20–30% bucket: *"the total time you spent at or below your AeT should be around 70 to 80 percent. The remaining 20 to 30 percent should be above the AeT, with a sizable chunk of that above the AnT."*

Strict polarized training in the sports science literature typically means **<AeT vs. >AnT** with minimal time between thresholds. Friel's framing is closer to "Zone 2 vs. above-Zone 2" rather than strict polarized. He does separately criticize "the single biggest mistake — making easy workouts moderately hard" so he ultimately points the user toward strict polarized in practice, but the terminology is loose.

### 10.8 Block Periodization vs. Random Training in Advanced Athletes
- Block periodization (Ch. 9): for *truly elite* athletes only, not intermediates.
- "Random training" (Ch. 9): some highly advanced athletes effectively train "randomly" because they have unwritten plans in their heads.
- Yet Chapter 2 says random training is the worst possible approach.

Resolution implied by Friel: the "random" elite riders aren't actually random — they have internalized periodization. For coaching purposes, never recommend literally random training.

### 10.9 20-Something Elite Athletes and Weight Lifting
- Friel: *"Twenty-something cyclists, both male and female, who have been training and racing for a long time probably don't need as much resistance training as older riders."*
- Same paragraph: *"I am aware of many riders at the highest levels of performance in the sport who swear by their weight lifting programs."*

Not a contradiction so much as a hedged recommendation. Resolution: default to including AA/MT/MS/SM for most riders, with reduced frequency for 20-something elite male riders.

### 10.10 Missing Workout Codes in Appendix B
The book references **ME5** and **ME6** in the table of contents and chapter narratives, and **SP4**, but Appendix B contains only **ME1–ME4** and **SP1–SP3**. This appears to be either an editorial omission or a TOC mismatch. The published workout catalog is the operational source.

### 10.11 Mesocycle / Microcycle / "Week" Definitions
- **Mesocycle**: 3–4 weeks in linear; variable in Block; up to 21 days in 9-day-week model.
- **Microcycle**: ~1 week (i.e., 7 days) per the glossary — but in Ch. 9 a "week" may be 9 days for slow-recovery athletes, and in Ch. 11 the "R&R week" *"should not be literally taken to mean 7 days."*

The terms are operational rather than calendrical. The Coach GEM should treat "week" as a flexible unit.

### 10.12 "Form" — Two Definitions
- **Ch. 3 / Glossary**: form = race readiness AND freshness (no fatigue).
- **Ch. 13**: form is described as "the absence of fatigue" with race readiness as a *separate* component — *"Race readiness embraces topics beyond physical fitness, including, for example, a well-planned strategy and a wide array of tactics."*

In Ch. 13 Friel expands "race readiness" to include strategy, tactics, environmental adaptation, nutrition, hydration, equipment. The simpler glossary definition is more useful for everyday coaching.

### 10.13 The Author's Stance on Self-Coaching
Throughout the book, Friel both:
- Strongly recommends having a real coach as the most impactful single decision an athlete can make.
- AND writes the book as a comprehensive self-coaching guide.

The book's value proposition is: self-coaching with this book is much better than self-coaching without it, but a real coach is still better. The Coach GEM (an AI coach) should position itself transparently within this hierarchy.

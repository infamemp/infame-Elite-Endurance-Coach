## C01 Overview & Scope

### [TPOP-C01-001] Overview & Scope

**What the book is.** "Tapering and Peaking for Optimal Performance" (Iñigo Mujika, PhD, Human Kinetics, 2009; foreword by Miguel Indurain). It is the first compilation of the scientific literature on the pre-competition taper (the final phase of reduced training before a major event), its physiological and psychological effects, how those effects relate to performance, and the practical experience of elite coaches and athletes.

**Author authority.** Sport physiologist and university associate professor (University of the Basque Country). He has researched tapering since 1992 and has coached world-class triathletes. He was senior physiologist at the Australian Institute of Sport. Most of the physiological evidence comes from his own group's work plus a meta-analysis (Bosquet et al. 2007).

**For whom.** Scientists and students (Parts I-II, fully referenced), and coaches and athletes at any level (glossary, "At a Glance" summaries, Part III narratives written by elite coaches and athletes).

**Problem it solves.** Coaches feel most insecure about the taper: when to start, how much to cut, which method, and whether the athlete will detrain instead of peak. Taper design has been trial and error, a "shotgun approach" that changes several factors at once so that the cause of success is unknowable. The book offers a science-based alternative.

**Central thesis.**
- A well-designed taper is a progressive, non-linear reduction of training load, made mainly at the expense of volume (about 41-60% of pretaper training).
- Training intensity is maintained, and frequency is kept high in highly trained athletes.
- Duration is about 2 weeks by default (8-14 days best in meta-analysis), and it is individualized.
- The taper dissipates accumulated physiological and psychological fatigue and restores training tolerance, so adaptations are enhanced and not merely preserved.
- The typical competition gain is about 3% (usual range 0.5-6.0%). That is small but can change medal placings.
- No single ideal taper exists.

**Organization.**
- Part I (Ch 1-4): scientific bases.
  - Ch 1: definitions, aims, models and studies.
  - Ch 2: cardiorespiratory and metabolic changes.
  - Ch 3: biochemical, hormonal, neuromuscular and immunological changes.
  - Ch 4: psychological changes.
- Part II (Ch 5-8): performance.
  - Ch 5: reducing intensity, volume, frequency and duration, plus environmental factors.
  - Ch 6: design, expected gains, goal setting.
  - Ch 7: mathematical modeling.
  - Ch 8: team sports.
- Part III (Ch 9-12): elite practitioners.
  - Endurance: Bowman, Fiz, Van Lierde.
  - Sprint and power: McFarlane, Queally, Rollason, Vatkin, Winckler.
  - Precision: Fairweather, Olazabal.
  - Team: Charlesworth, the Springboks staff, McFadden, Matutinovic.
- Part III is experience-based and does not quantify effects.

**What it does NOT cover.**
- No universal prescription or single validated protocol.
- Little evidence on sex effects, on interactions with heat, cold or altitude, or on multiple peaking and team sports.
- No taper research for precision sports.
- Nutrition detail is limited to energy balance, carbohydrate and glycogen remarks.
- Mathematical models are explicitly not yet practical design tools for coaches.
- Several Part III tables lost their cell content in the source (see LOG).

### [TPOP-C01-002] Part I purpose: scientific bases of tapering

Because the taper is the final training phase before a major competition, it is of paramount importance to performance and event outcome. Coaches are more insecure about the right strategy in this phase than in any other and have relied almost exclusively on trial and error. Limitations of trial and error: (1) ample possibility of error (hit-or-miss); (2) imprecision of a "shotgun approach" in which several training factors are changed simultaneously; if successful, it is almost impossible to identify the real reason for success (Hawley and Burke 1988). A scientific approach, based on knowledge of the factors behind effective tapering and athlete adaptations, should improve individual programs.
Part I (four chapters) presents: the scientific knowledge on tapering strategies and models; the physiological and psychological effects of the taper; how these effects relate to taper-induced performance changes.
Research-based training principles combined with experience-based knowledge of successful coaches and athletes often yield sport-specific recommendations (Hawley and Burke 1988).

### [TPOP-C01-003] Chapter 2 scope: taper-associated cardiorespiratory and metabolic changes

Chapter 2 compiles and synthesizes knowledge on cardiorespiratory and metabolic changes during the pre-event taper and assesses their possible relationship with the performance benefits of the taper. Performance improvement is the ultimate goal of an efficient taper, but physiological changes must occur for it to happen. The underlying mechanisms are not yet completely understood. Reviews on the subject were few (Mujika 1998; Mujika et al. 2004; older: Houmard 1991, Houmard and Johns 1994, Neufer 1989). The most relevant changes occur at cardiac, respiratory and metabolic levels, letting the athlete deliver more oxygen and fuel to working muscles and use them more efficiently.

### [TPOP-C01-004] Chapter 3 summary of conclusions

Biochemical, hormonal, muscular, and immunological changes take place during a taper and can correlate with competition performance.
- Biochemical: CK is usually reduced after a taper (indicating recovery from training stress and muscle damage); other biochemical indexes (urea, uric acid, creatinine, blood gases) are unaffected and not useful for coaches.
- Hormonal: T, C, and T:C provide information on stress, recovery, and performance capacity, but analyses are invasive/expensive and performance gains occur without changes in them. Urinary C:Cn ratio, catecholamines, GH, and IGF-I show promise but need more research.
- Neuromuscular: Muscular strength and power, suppressed by intensive training, markedly increase in the taper, tied to local increases in oxidative enzyme activity (e.g., citrate synthase) and positive single-fiber changes (especially Type IIa hypertrophy, force, velocity, and power).
- Immunological: Research in the taper is limited, but small transient changes in immune cells, immunoproteins, and cytokines are unlikely to have clinical significance; athletes can train with confidence during the taper without compromising immunity.

### [TPOP-C01-005] Chapter 4 scope: psychological changes with the taper

Chapter 4 argues that the physiological changes of chapters 2 and 3 probably do not fully explain the performance benefits of a successful taper. Competition performance is the result of a conscious effort (Noakes 2000), so psychological and motivational factors contribute heavily to post-taper performance. A well-designed taper is presumably accompanied by beneficial psychological changes in mood state, perception of effort and quality of sleep. The chapter also covers periodization of psychological skills training and its implications for the tapering and peaking phases (described as somewhat unexplored territory).

### [TPOP-C01-008] Chapter 8 scope: team sport tapering

Chapter 8 addresses tapering and peaking in team sports. Periodization lets individual-sport athletes peak for the most important competitions of the season. Individual athletes can afford to perform below expectations, or even miss competitions outside their major goals (always in the best interest of those goals). Team sport athletes usually need to perform at a high level week after week to be in contention for the championship when it counts, so the individual-sport approach may not always be the most suitable.

### [TPOP-C01-009] Part III: elite coaches and athletes on tapering and peaking (scope and caveat)

Part III collects the applied views of world-class coaches and athletes on tapering to peak for major competitions. It is split into four chapters: endurance sports, sprint and power events, precision sports, and team sports.
Caveat stated by the author: unlike parts I and II, part III does not rest on scientific research. The value is in showing how different approaches can succeed on the world stage.
Chapter 9 background: most scientific knowledge on tapering concerns individual endurance sports (swimming, cycling, running, rowing, triathlon). Two reasons: (1) moderate to large correlations exist between physiological capacities, basic training factors (volume, intensity) and competitive performance; (2) training and performance factors are easier to isolate and quantify than in team sports (Pyne et al. 2009).

### [TPOP-C01-011] Chapter 11 Tapering for Precision Sports - scope and evidence gap

Precision sports (shooting, archery, golf, curling) are sports in which performance is a measure of an athlete's accuracy in activities that do not rely on cyclic movements. In most other individual sports, performance is related to physiological capacities and basic training variables.
- No scientific knowledge is available on optimal tapering and peaking strategies for these sports.
- Open questions the author lists: Should an archer shoot more or fewer arrows before the Olympic Games? Should a golfer concentrate on driving or putting in the days before a major tournament? How do strength and endurance training affect shooting accuracy?
- Until research answers these questions, the chapter relies only on the personal experiences of champions (Simon Fairweather, archery; José María Olazabal, golf).

## C02 Philosophy & Core Principles

### [TPOP-C02-001] Core principles (summary)

**What the author optimizes for:** peak competition performance on the target day, by removing accumulated fatigue while continuing, not just preserving, adaptation.

**Principles and reasoning**
1. **The taper has two aims.** (a) Maximally reduce accumulated physiological and psychological stress. (b) Restore training tolerance and further enhance adaptations. Reasoning: models show that fatigue removal alone may be insufficient (Thomas and Busso 2005). Best taper = optimal compromise between fatigue reduction and continued adaptation.
2. **Athletes should be fully adapted before the taper starts.** Gains come mainly from a big drop in the negative influence (fatigue) plus a slight rise in the positive influence (fitness). Fatigue fades faster than fitness.
3. **Reduce load by cutting volume, not intensity.**
   - Intensity maintenance retains aerobic power, anabolic hormones, feelings of speed and confidence.
   - Not decreasing intensity gave a better effect size in every case (Bosquet 2007).
   - Volume cut of 41-60% is the "safe bet".
4. **Keep frequency high in highly trained athletes** (more than 80%) to avoid "loss of feel". Moderately trained people can keep adaptations at 30-50%.
5. **Progressive tapering beats step tapering** (Banister 1999; meta-analysis), with a caveat on small samples and cycling.
6. **Duration is individual.** Default is 2 weeks when the profile is unknown. Pretaper overload requires a longer taper, not a bigger percentage cut. Use simple anxiety and perceived-fatigue questionnaires, key-set times and conversation to tune it.
7. **Individualize; there is no ideal taper.** Responses vary (biphasic, early, late responders and non-responders), and adaptation is unstable over time. The same protocol will not necessarily work again.
8. **Psychology is half the taper.** Performance is a conscious effort. Mood, effort perception and sleep improve with taper. Confidence, trust in the plan and realistic goals matter.
9. **Peaking begins on day one of the season**, and the taper is the natural outgrowth of the whole year (Bowman, Winckler, McFarlane). Do not introduce new exercises or disrupt the familiar rhythm in the last weeks.
10. **Match energy intake to reduced expenditure** and watch iron status.
11. **Be realistic.** The typical gain is about 3% (0.5-6.0%). Unrealistic targets harm motivation. Small gains still matter because Olympic gaps are around 1.6-2.0%.
12. **Simple monitoring beats invasive markers:** "a chronometer and verbal communication".

**What the author rejects:** trial-and-error "shotgun" changes; complete rest or step cuts used as the only strategy; reducing intensity or racing hard as a fitness test; over-reliance on modeling or single hormonal or biochemical markers.

**Non-negotiables:** maintain intensity; reduce volume progressively; avoid detraining; individualize; maintain psychological readiness.

### [TPOP-C02-002] Aims of the taper and the fatigue-plus-adaptation principle

Main aim: reduce the negative physiological and psychological impact of daily training, i.e., eliminate accumulated or residual fatigue, which translates into additional fitness gains. Key objectives of an effective taper: (1) maximally reduce accumulated physiological and psychological stress of daily training; (2) restore training tolerance and further enhance training-induced adaptations.
Performance enhancement relates to recovery of physiological capacities impaired by past training and restoration of tolerance to training, giving further adaptations during the taper (Mujika et al. 2004; Thomas and Busso 2005).
Thomas and Busso 2005 (simulations, nonlinear model, laboratory training data): simply reducing fatigue without simultaneously enhancing adaptations through training done during the taper could be insufficient for best possible performance. Taper adjustment should be the optimal compromise between reducing fatigue and continuing, not just preserving, positive training adaptations.

### [TPOP-C02-003] Chapter 1 conclusions: individualization and no ideal model

- Gains during taper mostly come from decreasing accumulated fatigue, but further performance-enhancing adaptations are also possible.
- Adaptations: positive hormonal environment (more testosterone, less cortisol), more red blood cells (oxygen-carrying capacity), less muscle damage, better psychological preparedness.
- Most efficient: reduce load progressively and gradually rather than suddenly by a fixed amount; the latter may still help, particularly in cycling.
- Progressively reducing then increasing load in the final days, when athletes are rested and can do quality training, may also be efficient.
- A moderate load increase before competition may help some, but athletes respond differently, so strategies must be individualized.
- Open research: whether differences relate to total training load; whether models suit particular sports/athletes; alternative designs need experimental testing.
- It is likely that an ideal tapering model does not exist; adopt designs to suit each athlete's individual adaptation profile.

### [TPOP-C02-004] Taper principle: reduce load without causing detraining

Training load (training stimulus) = combination of training intensity, volume and frequency (Wenger and Bell 1986). In the taper it is markedly reduced to dissipate accumulated fatigue, but the reduction must not be detrimental to training-induced adaptations. An insufficient stimulus can cause partial or complete loss of anatomical, physiological and performance adaptations (detraining). Athletes and coaches must determine how far load can be reduced, and at the expense of which variables, while retaining or slightly improving adaptations and avoiding a detrained state.
Summary rule of the chapter: maintain intensity ("quality training"); reduce mainly volume; keep frequency high in highly trained athletes; 2 weeks as default duration.

### [TPOP-C02-005] Shared principle: peaking starts early in the season, not in the taper

- McFarlane: tapering and peaking involve being prepared at the start of the season and being in good condition at competition; the training program should prepare the athlete for competition from the beginning of the season, not just during the taper. "Train wisely, race wisely."
- Winckler: the process of peaking should begin on the very first day of training in the general preparation segment. The habits, conditioning and technical and tactical skills instilled early are the pedestal from which the final taper is launched. - Queally: lay foundations of strength and power to develop track speed for the whole season; the taper protocol begins only in the last 4 weeks and is very similar to specific core training.
- Vatkin (gymnastics): the final phase is heavy, not a conventional taper; competitive fitness should equal training fitness.

### [TPOP-C02-006] Queally: individuality of protocols; cake analogy

- Protocols are individual and adaptation profiles change over time: what works for Queally is not necessarily ideal for others, nor for himself in the future. Finding what works is trial and error; understand how your mind and body work.
- Cake analogy: the correct ingredients and quantities (training protocols), their order and mixing time correspond to specific core training; the timing (amount of training vs recovery) is up to the athlete. Putting the cake in the oven at the ideal temperature for the ideal time is the peaking-tapering phase.
- Key concepts: develop strength, power and speed for the whole season, then work on maintaining speed for longer during the taper; maximal efforts followed by complete recovery; listen to body and mood to know when recovered for key sessions.

### [TPOP-C02-007] Winckler: why not a special taper phase; timing of the taper

- Goal: peak performance of the year at the major championship for the event (could be college conference, Worlds or Olympic final).
- Early in his career he was led to try: delaying the highest intensity until the last 14 to 21 days; dramatically decreasing volume and increasing rest days 14 to 21 days before; using new training means such as swimming or running in water while reducing track sessions; devoting a larger proportion of the last 3 weeks to psychological skills training.
- All have value, but the common flaw was emphasizing them only in the last 14 to 21 days, which interrupts the natural rhythm and flow of training and recovery.
- Instead: improve critical skills across the entire year, from very closed teaching environments to very open, competitive ones. As athletes gain confidence in skills it becomes a focal point in competition. Athletes in his sport have almost total control over performance, so staying focused on the most important skills improves performance.

### [TPOP-C02-008] Fairweather: confidence, mental energy and preparedness are the core of the archery taper

- The concept of the taper is not formally or informally discussed among archery competitors and coaches; no academic research exists for the sport.
- Optimizing mental energy is as critical as peaking physical energy levels, if not more so. Peaking for the 5 competition days is highly individual and depends on experience and support systems.
- The pre-competition week is shaped by competition timetable, transport logistics, and weather conditions; adaptability during final preparations is non-negotiable.
- Confidence, mental energy, and preparedness represent the core of the archery taper. This is accomplished via a calm, measured approach to daily tasks, providing enough structured activity to keep the athlete's mind occupied without generating fatigue.
- In high-skill, low-physicality sports (archery, shooting), performance stems from confidence and routine: an archer who feels confident, comfortable, and positive will defeat a technically superior opponent who is plagued by doubt.
- Sport-science interventions and testing during taper/preparation frequently disrupted training and damaged confidence. State-of-the-art technology is not the determinant of success (evidenced by under-resourced European nations remaining world powers on basic equipment).
- Confidence and peace of mind are fragile: poor planning or mismanagement by officials (even failing to secure water during transit and upon arrival) impairs mental balance.
- Fairweather reports never having heard an archer attribute a poor competition to an incorrect physical taper; the key challenge is the elusive mental taper.

## C03 Integral Model

### [TPOP-C03-001] Integral Model

**1. Chain: beliefs → mechanisms → method → decisions**

*Beliefs.* Performance is the balance between fitness and fatigue. Performance is also a conscious effort. The taper is a distinct phase whose design should be scientific, individualized and tied to the entire season.

*Mechanisms* (see entries in C04):
- Fatigue (negative influence) dissipates faster than adaptation (positive influence) when load drops (Fatigue and adaptation model).
- The variable dose-response model adds that fatigue from a given dose depends on prior load. Restored tolerance lets taper training add adaptation (Variable dose-response model).
- Observable consequences:
  - Hormonal: T:C up, cortisol down, testosterone up. Intensity helps testosterone, while low-intensity continuous training may hinder it.
  - Hematological: blood volume, red cells, hemoglobin, hematocrit, haptoglobin and reticulocytes up.
  - Muscular: strength and power up, Type IIa fibers most responsive, citrate synthase up with a high-intensity low-volume taper.
  - Metabolic: muscle glycogen up, peak lactate up.
  - CK down.
  - Psychological: mood, RPE and sleep improve.
  - Immune function is not compromised.
- Complete rest, or too small a reduction, or loss of intensity or frequency, causes detraining or loss of feel.

*Method.*
- Progressive (linear or exponential) reduction of load.
- Volume is cut 41-60%, with a range of 50-90% reported as beneficial.
- Intensity is maintained.
- Frequency is kept at more than 80% in highly trained athletes.
- Duration is about 2 weeks (8-14 days best in meta-analysis).
- Optionally, a moderate load increase in the last 3 days (see "Alternative taper: reduce then increase load before competition (two-phase taper)").

**2. Variables and priority order**
1. Intensity: hold.
2. Volume: cut (primary lever).
3. Frequency: hold high in elite or technique sports.
4. Duration: individualize. Default is 2 weeks, longer after overload.
5. Shape: progressive over step. Fast exponential decay (τ = 4 days) outperformed slow (τ = 8 days) in triathletes.
6. Context: travel, heat, altitude, sex, event type, level.
Bosquet's evidence supports each: see "Volume reduction dose: 41-60% (key recommendation)", "Training frequency during the taper by athlete level", "Taper duration: evidence and recommendation".

**3. How the tools fit**
- Evidence tables (C06, C15) justify choices.
- Expected-gain table and the goal-time formula set targets (see "Typical size of taper-induced performance gains", "Goal-time calculation from expected percent gain").
- The McNeely and Sandler duration guidance by weekly training hours (Table 5.13) is experience-based and unconfirmed.
- Monitoring uses key-set times, conversation, RPE, HR:RPE, mood and recovery-stress questionnaires, sleep, and optionally CK, T:C and peak lactate. Invasive markers are costly and inconsistent.
- Library plans (Phelps, Henry, Fiz, Queally, Winckler, Vatkin, hockey, water polo) show practitioner implementations.

**4. Decision flow for a new athlete**
1. Define the target event and a realistic goal (apply 0.5-6% to the pretaper time; about 3% typical). See "Using the data to set realistic performance goals".
2. Check that pretaper training has produced adaptations. Was there overreaching or overload? Check HR and other signs. See "Effect of pretaper overload on taper duration (Thomas 2008 simulations)".
3. Choose duration. If the profile is unknown, use 2 weeks. If overload preceded it, plan about 3 weeks (step-equivalent). Fiz, Bowman and Rollason used about 21 days; Queally 4 weeks; Winckler 21 days.
4. Choose shape: progressive.
5. Set the volume cut at 41-60% (check athlete level and sport) and keep intensity and race-specific work.
6. Set frequency: more than 80% for highly trained athletes. Cut only for moderately trained ones.
7. Adapt to context: jet lag (taper integrated, time to adjust), heat, altitude (camps are not a taper), event length (Van Lierde), sex (no evidence for a different design), team vs individual.
8. Match energy intake to expenditure; carbohydrate in the last days; monitor iron.
9. Monitor through key sets, communication, RPE, mood and sleep, and adjust. Do not treat final sessions as a test.
10. Psychological plan: visualization, routines, trust, no new exercises, a familiar environment.
11. Review afterwards. Not improving may mean a poor plan, non-response or expectancy; it cannot usually be separated.

**5. Tensions, trade-offs, exceptions**
- Step vs progressive: step had a larger but non-significant effect (0.42 vs 0.30; n = 98 vs 380) and cyclists favored step; the author warns of small samples.
- Volume above 60% was less effective than 41-60%. Smaller or larger cuts still help.
- Fiz argues for reduced recovery times and no anaerobic work; Queally and sprinters use maximal efforts with full recovery. Both keep intensity, but the form depends on the event.
- Gymnastics and some team sports use heavy final loads, so no taper as such (Vatkin; Denmark 1992).
- Team sports: multiple peaking is unstudied. Choose between recover-and-rebuild and maintaining training depending on fatigue and available time.
- Hormonal markers and mood correlate with performance in some studies and not in others.
- Expectancy (placebo) effects cannot be excluded.
- Modeling is insightful but not yet a coaching tool. It is based on non-athletes in places, and the last-3-days load increase needs experimental confirmation.
- Optimal duration may be longer for swimmers; a 2-week limit before detraining was suggested for female swimmers (Kenitzer), but modeling found no sex difference.

### [TPOP-C03-002] Cross-chapter links that shape taper prescription

- **Which volume to cut.** Three independent findings point the same way: total testosterone during a 6-day taper fell as low-intensity continuous training (LICT) distance rose (r = -0.78) and rose with high-intensity interval distance (r = 0.68) (Mujika et al. 2000); plasma CK rose with continuous running distance in the taper (Mujika et al. 2000); and Hellard et al. 2005 found low-intensity load should fall to 40-50% of the athlete's maximal load. Practical reading: the volume cut falls mainly on low-intensity continuous work, while high-intensity and race-pace work is kept (see C06-008, C09-006, C13-002, C15-018). Note: Table 5.4 shows Mujika's swimmers also cut zones II-V in volume (repetitions), never in pace.
- **Why 41-60%.** The meta-analysis optimum (Bosquet 2007, 41-60%) matches the 7-day cycling data: -50% volume improved VO2max (+6.0%), 20 km TT (+5.4%) and muscle oxygen extraction (NIRS), while -30% and -80% did not (Neary 2003a, 2005).
- **Why intensity is held.** High-intensity low-volume tapers produced bigger gains in blood and red cell volume, glycogen, citrate synthase, Type II fiber enzymes and performance than low-intensity tapers (Shepley 1992; Neary 2003b), which supports the Ch 5 rule and the Bosquet intensity table (C15-010).
- **Fitness-fatigue model to individual variability.** The Ch 1 NI/PI model is developed in Ch 7 (C04-047, C04-048); Avalos 2003 shows the individual response is unstable over time, which explains why identical tapers give different results (C09-009).
- **Practitioners agree with the science.** Charlesworth, the Springboks staff and McFadden all keep intensity, cut volume, take a few days off in the last 2 weeks and track individual load (C09-021). Exceptions that confirm the sport-specific caveat: gymnastics keeps heavy loads (C08-015), golf treats peaking as mental timing (C12-010).

### [TPOP-C04-001] Fitness-fatigue model of taper (Mujika et al. 1996a): NI and PI

Mujika et al. (1996a) analyzed three taper segments in national- and international-level swimmers with a mathematical model computing fatigue and fitness from a negative and a positive function: NI (negative influence) = initial decay in performance after a training bout; PI (positive influence) = subsequent phase of supercompensation (Fig 1.1).
Result: performance gains during tapers were mainly due to marked reductions in NI, coupled with slight increases in PI (Fig 1.2; significant pre vs post taper differences, p<.01; NI and PI expressed in the performance unit). Investigators suggested athletes should have achieved most or all expected physiological adaptations by the time they start tapering; performance improves as soon as accumulated fatigue fades and adaptations become apparent.

### [TPOP-C04-002] Biological and psychological markers of reduced stress during taper

Markers indicating reduced training stress/enhanced recovery:
- Hormonal: increased testosterone, decreased cortisol, increased testosterone–cortisol ratio.
- Hematological: increased red cell volume, hematocrit, hemoglobin, haptoglobin, reticulocytes.
- Biochemical: decreased blood creatine kinase.
- Psychological: decreased perception of effort, mood disturbance, perception of fatigue; increased vigor; improved sleep quality.
Detail: Mujika et al. 1996d: significant correlation (r = 0.81, P < 0.05, N = 8) between % change in total testosterone:cortisol ratio (TT:C) and % performance improvement during a 4-week taper in elite swimmers (Fig 1.3; TT:C change ranged about -40 to +60%, performance gain 0-6%). T:C ratio is used as a marker of training stress (androgens = anabolic, cortisol = catabolic index; Adlercreutz et al. 1986); a rise indicates enhanced recovery whether due to lower cortisol (Bonifazi 2000, Mujika 1996c) or higher testosterone from enhanced pituitary response after intensive training (Busso 1992, Mujika 1996d, 2002a).
Hematology: increases in red cell volume, hemoglobin, hematocrit (Shepley 1992, Yamamoto 1988), related to taper performance gains (Mujika 1997). Haptoglobin (glycoprotein binding free hemoglobin to conserve iron) is often below normal in highly trained endurance athletes (chronic hemolysis; Selby and Eichner 1986) and rises during taper (Mujika 2002a). Reticulocytes increase at taper end in middle-distance runners (Mujika 2000, 2002a). Overall: positive balance between erythropoiesis and hemolysis.
Creatine kinase usually decreases with taper (Millard 1985, Yamamoto 1988).
Psychological changes (Hooper 1999, Morgan 1987, Raglin 1996); sleep quality improved in swimmers (Taylor 1997). Details in chapters 3-4.

### [TPOP-C04-003] Blood lactate kinetics as an indicator of taper adaptation

When exercising muscles use carbohydrates, they produce lactic acid, which immediately dissociates into lactate and hydrogen ions. Increasing exercise intensity translates into higher lactate production, diffusing from muscle into the blood. Blood lactate serves as an index of the physiological demands of an exercise bout. Fatigued and carbohydrate-depleted muscles (typical of heavy training phases) struggle to produce power and lactate. In contrast, well-recovered muscles can work fast and push hard, generating high lactate concentrations. Measuring blood lactate after intense exercise therefore provides a sensitive marker of an athlete's physiological adaptation to the taper and indicates anaerobic capacity supercompensation.

### [TPOP-C04-004] Blood ammonia kinetics and adenine nucleotide degradation during taper

When the rate of muscular ATP hydrolysis exceeds the rate of ADP rephosphorylation (oxidative or nonoxidative), ATP is resynthesized via the myokinase reaction: 2 ADP <-> ATP + AMP. AMP is subsequently deaminated, producing inosine monophosphate (IMP) and ammonia (Lowenstein 1990, Sahlin and Broberg 1990, Tullson and Terjung 1991). Ammonia is also generated when muscle protein reserves are broken down and oxidized during exercise, particularly when carbohydrate availability is compromised. Postexercise blood ammonia concentration serves as a marker of exercise-induced adenine nucleotide degradation, muscle energy state, protein catabolism, and overall training stress/overtraining (Warren et al. 1992).

### [TPOP-C04-005] VO2max during the taper: increase or unchanged; intensity maintenance is key

VO2max can increase or remain unchanged during a taper in highly trained athletes. A decrease would most likely indicate a poorly planned taper in endurance athletes. Studies collectively show improved or stable VO2max and performance gains, particularly where training intensity was maintained (see chapter 5). Performance can improve without VO2max change.
Cyclists, 7-day taper (Neary et al. 2003a): volume -50% gave VO2max +6.0% and simulated 20 km TT +5.4%; volume -30% or -80% gave no VO2max increase or performance gain.
Neary et al. 2003b: maintained intensity, reduced volume: VO2max +2.5%, simulated performance +4.3%. Maintained volume, reduced intensity: VO2max +1.1% and performance +2.2% (statistically nonsignificant). Supports training intensity as key for maintaining/enhancing adaptations (Mujika and Padilla 2003a).
Jeukendrup et al. 1992: 2-week step taper (nonprogressive standardized reduction): cyclists VO2max +4.5%, peak power +10%, 8.5 km outdoor TT 7.2% faster.
Triathletes 2-week taper: VO2max +9.1%; laboratory running (1.2-6.3%) and cycling (1.5-7.9%) performance gains (Banister et al. 1999, Zarkadas et al. 1995). Margaritis et al. 2003: +3% in VO2max and simulated duathlon during 14-day taper in long-distance triathletes.
Unchanged VO2max with performance gain: high school swimmers tapering 2 or 4 weeks, TT +4-8% (D'Acquisto 1992); male cross-country/middle-distance runners, enhanced treadmill performance (Shepley 1992); runners 7 days: 5K treadmill TT +2.8%, time to exhaustion +4.8% (Houmard 1994); runners 4 weeks: 8 km +1.1% (Harber 2004); cyclists with training frequency -50% for 10 days: VO2max +2.5%, simulated 20 km TT +1.2% (Dressendorfer 2002a,b); college swimmers 20 days before US Nationals: 65.4 vs 66.6 ml·kg-1·min-1, performance not reported (Van Handel 1988). No VO2max or peak power improvement in cyclists with step taper continuous or intermittent (Rietjens 2001), runners 3-week (Houmard 1990a,b) or 4-week step taper (McConell 1993).

### [TPOP-C04-006] Economy of movement during the taper

Results are disparate, probably due to differences in training/tapering programs and athlete caliber.
Running: Houmard 1994: 18 male + 6 female distance runners, progressive 7-day run or cycle taper, total volume -85%: 7% (0.9 kcal/min) decrease in calculated submaximal energy expenditure at 80% VO2peak on treadmill; evident in 7 of 8 subjects in run taper, not in cycle taper. Suggested explanation: increased muscle mitochondrial capacity plus neural, structural and biomechanical factors. Confirms Houmard 1990a (lower O2 cost of running after 3 weeks reduced training). Harber 2004: no change in running economy at 16 km/h in university cross-country runners after 4-week taper, possibly because volume reduction was only 25%, whereas studies suggest a 41-60% volume reduction is needed for optimal results in most athletes (chapter 5). Houmard 1989: unchanged submaximal VO2 after 10-day taper. McConell 1993: no change after 4-week step taper at 65%, 85%, 95% VO2max.
Swimming: gains appear inversely related to athlete caliber. High school swimmers 2 or 4 weeks: downward shifts of VO2-velocity curves of 4.9-15.6% and 8.5-16.7% respectively (D'Acquisto 1992), attributed to volume reduction and possibly better stroke mechanics. Johns 1992: 5-8% decline in O2 cost of swimming after 10 or 14 days in intercollegiate swimmers. Van Handel 1988: no change in much higher caliber, already very economical swimmers.
Cycling: Dressendorfer 2002a: no marked economy improvement at 200 W after 10-day taper vs previous high-volume and high-intensity phases, but O2 cost remained lower after taper (2.82 L/min) than baseline (2.95 L/min). Rietjens 2001: no change in O2 cost at 165 or 270 W after 3-week step taper.
Practical: economy improvement is more likely in lower-caliber athletes.

### [TPOP-C04-007] Muscle oxygenation (NIRS) after taper (Neary et al. 2005)

Near-infrared spectroscopy (NIRS; optical absorption of Hb and Mb at 700-1,000 nm) was used to measure right vastus medialis oxygenation during a simulated 20 km cycling time trial before and after a 7-day taper with 30%, 50%, or 80% weekly volume reduction (Neary et al. 2005). Mean tissue oxygenation signal during the test was significantly greater (indicating increased muscle deoxygenation / oxygen extraction) following the 50% volume reduction taper (Figure 2.2). A high correlation was found between change in tissue oxygenation and 20 km TT performance time (r = 0.82, R^2 = 0.6804, y = 3.7435x - 249.32, N = 11; Figure 2.3; note: figure axis indicates delta time in seconds, while figure caption states minutes; negative delta indicates faster ride). This demonstrates that the taper facilitates peripheral adaptations in working muscle (enhanced oxygen extraction), which directly contribute to whole-body performance gains.

### [TPOP-C04-009] Maximal heart rate during the taper

Results inconsistent: decrease, constant or increase. D'Acquisto 1992 swimmers: lower after 2-week (187 vs 192) or 4-week (185 vs 194 beats/min) taper. ### [TPOP-C04-010] Submaximal heart rate during the taper

Mostly little change. Unchanged: swimmers at 1.0-1.3 m/s after 2 or 4 weeks (D'Acquisto 1992); postexercise HR after 200-yard swim at 90% of season-best after 1 and 2 weeks (Costill 1985); runs at 265 or 298 m/min after 10-day taper (Houmard 1989); collegiate runners at 75% VO2max 161 vs 163 beats/min after 3 weeks (Flynn 1994); 3-week step taper at 65%/85% VO2max (Houmard 1990a); 4-week step taper at 65/85/95% (McConell 1993); cyclists at 200 W after 10-day taper vs preceding high-intensity phase (though lower than baseline, 137 vs 152 beats/min, a positive training adaptation; Dressendorfer 2002a); cyclists at 165 and 270 W after 3-week step tapers (Rietjens 2001); HR-power slopes identical before/after 1-week taper but shifted right vs baseline (Martin and Andersen 2000); HR during simulated 20 km TT identical before/after taper, but O2 pulse increased 23.1 to 24.8 ml O2/beat in the -50% volume group, who improved 5.4% (Neary 2003a); weightlifters 1 or 4 weeks (Stone 1996).

### [TPOP-C04-011] Blood pressure and cardiac dimensions during the taper

Resting BP: only three reports, none showing substantial effect. Flynn 1994: systolic 112 vs 114 mmHg (8 male runners), 118 vs 116 (5 male swimmers); diastolic 73 vs 74 (runners), 76 vs 78 (swimmers). Hooper 1999 swimmers: modest declines of 3.4% (systolic) and 2.2% (diastolic), SD 12.5% and 12.2%. Stone 1996: no change in elite weightlifters.
Cardiac dimensions: only one study (Haykowsky 1998): 2-week swimming taper at 1,050 m and 1,848 m, progressive 73% volume reduction with slight increase in percentage of high-intensity training. No marked change in diastolic/systolic cavity dimensions, septal wall thickness, LV mass, stroke volume, cardiac output, cardiac index or fractional shortening. Authors concluded 3 weeks altitude/control training followed by 2 weeks taper did not alter structural/functional cardiovascular dynamics. More studies needed, but change is unlikely given the limited duration and relatively high training demands of tapers, in contrast with complete training stoppage (Mujika and Padilla 2001).

### [TPOP-C04-013] Hematology: hemolysis versus erythropoiesis during the taper

Red cell content = balance between production (erythropoiesis) and destruction (hemolysis); more red cells mean better O2-carrying capacity. Intensive training can cause substantial hemolysis, and iron stores are often compromised by excessive training or poor nutrition. Intensive training can decrease red blood cells, hemoglobin and hematocrit (Coutts 2007a; Mujika 2004), attributed to hemodilution from plasma volume expansion, imbalance between hematopoiesis and intravascular hemolysis, or iron deficiency. The taper can be accompanied by a positive balance between hemolysis and erythropoiesis.
Evidence: Shepley 1992, 7-day high-intensity low-volume taper in runners: total blood volume +15%, red cell volume +14%, hematocrit +2.6% (possibly increased hematopoiesis with decreased intravascular hemolysis). Hypervolemia possibly linked to higher plasma renin activity and vasopressin during exercise and chronic increase in blood water-binding capacity. Hemoglobin and hematocrit increased in swimmers (Burke 1982a, Rushall and Busch 1980, Yamamoto 1988) and triathletes (Coutts 2007a, Margaritis 2003, Rudzki 1995), attributed to decreased hemolysis and net erythrocyte increase facilitated by reduced training load. Mujika 2000: reticulocyte count +40% after 1-week taper in middle-distance runners, indicating enhanced red cell production (release of immature cells with lower hemoglobin and MCH).
Indexes of positive red cell balance: increased serum haptoglobin (low in middle/long-distance runners and swimmers, suggesting chronic hemolysis; increased with a 6-day taper in middle-distance runners, Mujika 2002a, with a trend to higher reticulocytes) and decreased red cell distribution width (fell slightly over 12 weeks intensive training + 4-week taper in highly trained swimmers, significantly lower at end of taper, Mujika 1997; slight reductions in runners and swimmers, Pizza 1997). Lower RDW is a positive adaptation because high RDW is associated with decreased deformability, decreased osmotic resistance and increased mechanical fragmentation of erythrocytes.

### [TPOP-C04-015] Substrate utilization (RER) during the taper

Submaximal: RER declines or is unchanged. Club cyclists at 175 W: RER 0.99 to 0.96, higher fat contribution (Neary 1992). Unchanged RER: 10-min run at 80% VO2peak (Houmard 1994); running 265 and 298 m/min (Houmard 1989); fat oxidation during 90 min steady-state cycle after 7, 14, 21 days of step taper (Rietjens 2001). 3- and 4-week step tapers gave slight but significant RER increases at 65, 85, 95% VO2max (Houmard 1990a; McConell 1993).
Maximal: RER unchanged in simulated 20 km TT (Neary 2003a) and in treadmill running after 7-day run or cycle taper (Houmard 1994): substrate contribution to maximal power appears unmodified, perhaps due to stable aerobic-anaerobic work production and O2 deficit (Morton and Gastin 1997), or to higher muscle glycogen theoretically raising carbohydrate use. Six days of taper with high-carbohydrate diet increased carbohydrate oxidation and RER at 80% VO2max (Walker 2000).

### [TPOP-C04-016] Blood lactate after maximal exercise during the taper

Peak blood lactate after maximal exercise can increase with taper, possibly through higher muscle glycogen by mass-action effect (Houmard 1994), underpinning better maximal performance (Bonifazi 2000; Mujika 2000, 2002a).
- Middle-distance runners: % 800 m performance change correlated with change in peak postrace lactate, r = .87 (Mujika 2000); later peak lactate +7.6% (Mujika 2002a).
- International swimmers, two seasons: r = .63 (Bonifazi 2000).
- Cyclists, 14-day step taper: peak lactate +78%, 8.5 km TT +7.2%, peak power +10.3% (Jeukendrup 1992).
- High school swimmers: +20% peak lactate, TT +4-8% (D'Acquisto 1992).
- Shepley 1992: peak lactate +7.2% (high-intensity taper) and +9.8% (low-intensity); time to exhaustion at 1,500 m pace +22% and +6%.
- Collegiate swimmers 6.9-7.5 mM (Van Handel 1988); elite junior rowers 14.4 to 15.8 mM after 1-week taper (Steinacker 2000).
- No change: rowers 500 m indoor after 1-week taper following 3 weeks overload, volume -25% judged insufficient or taper too short (Smith 2000); 16-year-old swimmers 11 days, 6.8 vs 7.2 mM, speculated creatine phosphate supercompensation (Papoti 2007).
Peak postcompetition lactate may be a useful index of anaerobic capacity (Lacour 1990; Mujika 2000) and a sensitive marker of taper-induced change.

### [TPOP-C04-021] Chapter 2 At-a-Glance syntheses and chapter summary

- Carrying O2: VO2max may increase and contribute to gains, but performance can improve without change. Lower O2 cost (better economy) can contribute, more likely in lower-caliber athletes. Resting, maximal and submaximal HR do not change unless athletes show clear overreaching before the taper. BP, cardiac dimensions, ventilatory function generally unaffected; observed change reflects adaptation to preceding training (delayed adaptation) rather than the taper.
- Hematology: reduced loads facilitate positive balance between red cell production and destruction, contributing to gains, but may compromise iron status.
- Energy: reduced loads lower daily energy expenditure; attend to energy intake to avoid imbalance and body composition change. Substrate contribution usually stable; carbohydrate contribution may rise due to increased glycogen. Higher peak lactate after taper relates to improved maximal performance in swimming, running, cycling, rowing; submaximal lactate unchanged or reduced after an efficient taper. Ammonia results inconclusive.

### [TPOP-C04-022] Chapter 3 overview: systems adapting to the taper

Besides the cardiorespiratory and metabolic changes (Chapter 2), other systems adapt to the taper: biochemical, hormonal, neuromuscular and immunological. These changes contribute to enhanced performance capability and can indicate an athlete's preparedness to perform. Few biochemical parameters change markedly in the pre-event taper, which limits their use as markers of physiological recovery and increased performance capacity.

### [TPOP-C04-023] Creatine kinase during the taper: study findings

- Most studies show CK falls during the taper (Table 3.1).
- Millard et al. 1985 (10 male and 10 female collegiate swimmers, 4-week taper): males 70% lower posttraining CK and 30% lower resting serum CK; females 28% and 7% lower. Posttaper absolute values did not differ between genders and were the lowest of the season. Suggests CK reflects training volume rather than intensity.
- Yamamoto et al. 1988: CK decreased after swimming tapers, correlating with daily workout volume during the taper.
- Flynn et al. 1994: CK -38% over 3 weeks of taper.
- Mujika et al. 1996d: plasma CK -43% over 4 weeks; did not correlate with performance improvement (0.4-4.9%).
- Costill et al. 1991: CK 28% lower after 2-3 weeks of taper; mean performance +3.2%.
- Burke et al. 1982b: CK declined but stayed in the high-normal range.
- Child et al. 2000 (male distance runners, simulated half-marathons before/after 7-day taper or normal training): lower serum CK after the second half-marathon in tapered athletes. Unlikely due to lower force generation, reduced oxidative stress or more antioxidant protection; speculated the taper facilitated muscle recovery. Performance did not improve.
- Houmard et al. 1990b: 3-week step taper lowered CK in well-trained runners; performance unchanged.
- Hooper et al. 1999: nonsignificant +17% plasma CK over a 2-week taper, very large interindividual variation. They argued plasma CK is not a reliable marker of training stress and reflects the acute response to a single session rather than homeostatic status; large variation may reflect differing responses to the taper.
- Very large interindividual variation with unchanged means also in triathletes (2-week taper, Coutts et al. 2007b) and middle-distance runners (6-day tapers, Mujika et al. 2000, 2002a). In the runners, plasma CK rose with increasing low-intensity continuous training distance during the taper, so reducing continuous running volume in the taper could limit exercise-induced muscle damage before competition (Mujika et al. 2000).
Conclusion: CK may help assess recovery from acute training stress and muscle damage during the taper, but its validity as a marker of an individual's performance capability is limited.

### [TPOP-C04-024] Other biochemical markers during the taper

- Yamamoto et al. 1988: serum GOT and GPT in male swimmers over two tapers; GPT declined in the first 4-9 days of each. Speculated increased posttaper ATP availability maintains cell membrane integrity and reduces enzyme efflux.
- Banister et al. 1992: marked declines in elevated serum enzyme activity (CK, lactate dehydrogenase, aspartate aminotransferase) during 32 days of taper after 28 days of normal training, in two subjects.
- Blood urea, uric acid and creatinine unchanged in 6-day tapers in middle-distance runners (Mujika et al. 2000, 2002a) and 14-day taper in triathletes (Coutts et al. 2007b). Serum uric acid unchanged in 7-day taper in distance runners (Child et al. 2000).
- Costill et al. 1985: no taper-induced change in swimmers' blood pH, PCO2, PO2, HCO3 and base excess after a 200-yard (183 m) submaximal swim at 90% of season's best performance.
At a glance: reduced CK after a taper suggests recovery from training stress and muscle damage, but does not necessarily correlate with improved performance. Other biochemical markers are largely unaffected by the taper.

### [TPOP-C04-025] Hormones as markers of training stress: rationale

Strenuous exercise causes short- and long-term endocrine alterations. Because of responsiveness to training stress, hormones (testosterone, cortisol, catecholamines, growth hormone, others) are used to monitor training stress, evaluate training responses and predict performance capacity. Such markers should reflect load variations across a season (e.g., the taper) and relate to competition performance changes. Plasma testosterone (T) and cortisol (C) could represent anabolic and catabolic tissue activity respectively. The T:C ratio has been proposed as a marker of training stress (Adlercreutz et al. 1986; Kuoppasalmi and Adlercreutz 1985), but data on androgen and C responses to tapering are inconclusive (Table 3.2).

### [TPOP-C04-026] Testosterone, cortisol and T:C ratio during the taper: study findings

- Flynn et al. 1994: runners no change in TT, FT, TT:C, FT:C over 3 weeks. Swimmers' TT and FT returned toward baseline after blunted values during intensive training; ratios unchanged. Changes in TT and FT (not ratios) paralleled performance changes.
- Dressendorfer et al. 2002a: male cyclists, 10 days reduced training: serum T +5.3%, 24 h urinary C -4.6%, performance +1.2%; T to urinary C ratio unchanged.
- No change in resting T, C, TT:C in runners on 3-week step taper (Houmard et al. 1990b); none in T, C, TT:C, SHBG in elite weightlifters after 1 or 4 weeks taper, while performance improved by 8.0 and 17.5 kg (Stone et al. 1996); none in TT, FT, C in strength-trained athletes tapering 4 weeks after 16 weeks heavy resistance training (Izquierdo et al. 2007).
- Mujika et al. 1996d (swimmers, 4-week taper after 8 weeks intensive training): TT, NSBT (non-SHBG-bound testosterone = FT plus albumin-bound T, the biologically active fraction), C, TT:C, NSBT:C stable despite large volume variation; performance +2.3%. Percent performance variation during taper correlated with TT:C (r = .81), NSBT:C (r = .76) and NSBT concentration (r = .71).
- Coutts et al. 2007b: well-trained triathletes, 2-week taper: decreased C and increased FT:C, not correlated with performance gains.
- Mujika et al. 2000 (800 m runners, 6-day taper): TT, FT, C, TT:C, FT:C stable; individual changes did not parallel performance. TT correlated inversely with low-intensity continuous training (LICT) distance during taper (r = -0.78, P < 0.05, N = 8) and positively with high-intensity interval training (HIIT) distance (r = 0.68, P = 0.06, N = 8) — suggesting LICT may hinder testosterone-stimulated anabolic processes in the taper, whereas HIIT may facilitate them.
- Mujika et al. 2002a (800 m runners, 6-day taper): increased TT after taper, attributed to eliminating low-intensity continuous training from the main part of sessions. Proposed mechanism: enhanced pituitary response to preceding intense training, positively influencing androgenic–anabolic activity in the subsequent low-stress taper (Busso et al. 1992; Mujika et al. 1996d, 2002a).
- Mujika et al. 2004 suggested measuring ACTH (LH–T regulated by hypothalamic–pituitary–testicular axis; C partly by hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenocortical axis). Coutts et al. 2007b (triathletes, 4 weeks intensified training then 2-week taper): ACTH trends suggested an enhanced pituitary response during the taper.

### [TPOP-C04-027] Resting cortisol as a marker of taper response

- Mujika et al. 1996b: swimmers who improved >2% with a 4-week taper showed slight reductions in resting cortisol; less-responding swimmers (<2%) had a significant cortisol increase (Fig 3.3; GE = group efficient, GLE = group less efficient; p < .05).
- Costill et al. 1991: collegiate swimmers' resting C fell 23-30%, T rose 22% in the first taper; performance +3.2% average in two 2-3 week tapers within a season.
- Tanaka et al. 1993: no changes in TT, C, TT:C over 6 weeks progressive increase plus 2 weeks gradual decrease in volume in well-trained swimmers.
- Bonifazi et al. 2000 (elite swimmers, two seasons): performance gains of 1.5-2.1% in tapers correlated positively with 22-49% increases in postcompetition peak lactate and negatively (r = -.66) with 19-29% change in resting precompetition plasma C. Conclusion: low C is a prerequisite for improved performance in events relying largely on anaerobic metabolism. (Fig 3.4: cortisol significantly different at 12 weeks vs initial, p = .003, and 18 weeks vs 12 weeks, p = .029; lactate and performance 18 vs 12 weeks p < .001.)
- Mujika et al. 2002a: changes in peak blood lactate after an 800 m race during a 6-day taper correlated with changes in serum C (r = -.75) and TT:C and FT:C ratios (r = .82) (Fig 3.5). Conclusion: an anabolic-favorable hormonal milieu is necessary for optimal function of the glycolytic power system and performance in middle-distance events.
- Steinacker et al. 2000: elite junior rowers showing overreaching and hypothalamic down-regulation in a high-load phase recovered during a week of taper; 2,000 m time trial improved 6.3% while central and peripheral steroid hormone concentrations rose about 10% (Fig 3.6), with positive changes in the Recovery–Stress Questionnaire for Athletes. Authors: the hypothalamus integrates different stress influences and responds via endocrine system (pituitary–adrenocortical and pituitary–gonadal axes), autonomic nervous system and behavior.

### [TPOP-C04-028] Why cortisol findings conflict across taper studies

- Female collegiate swimmers' salivary C returned to baseline after a 4-week taper with a progressive 63% volume reduction (O'Connor et al. 1989); male swimmers' salivary C fell 4.8% to the season's lowest (Tharp and Barnes 1990).
- Unchanged or slightly increased C reported in swimmers (Mujika et al. 1996d, 2002a), cyclists (Martin et al. 2000), and rowers (Steinacker et al. 2000).
- Plasma C responds to physiological and psychological stressors; physical stress produced by pretaper intensive training may be replaced by psychological stress associated with the oncoming major competition (Mujika et al. 1996c).
- Atlaoui et al. (2004) proposed physiological explanations: C binding with corticosteroid binding globulin, varying receptor density in target tissues, and prereceptor metabolism by the tissue-specific enzyme 11β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase (11β-HSD). Two isoenzymes of 11β-HSD interconvert active cortisol (C) and inactive cortisone (Cn) within target cells, modulating autocrine C action.
- 24 h urinary C:Cn ratio is a valid index of renal 11β-HSD activity (Best and Walker 1997). Atlaoui et al. (2004) showed a decline in C:Cn during 3 weeks of taper subsequent to 4 weeks of intensive training; a large negative correlation was found between competition performance changes and C:Cn changes during taper (r = -.69), indicating utility as a monitoring tool.

### [TPOP-C04-029] Catecholamines during the taper

Plasma and urinary catecholamines have been used to monitor training stress and identify overreaching/overtraining.
- Hooper et al. 1993: plasma noradrenaline higher during taper in overtrained swimmers who failed to improve.
- Hooper et al. 1999: small time-trial change during taper predicted by plasma noradrenaline change (82% of variance). Authors: useful marker of taper recovery, but role in recovery is not well established and expense and complexity preclude routine screening.

- Coutts et al. 2007b: no significant changes in urinary adrenaline and noradrenaline during intensified training and taper in well-trained triathletes.

### [TPOP-C04-031] Other hormones (thyroid, insulin, C-peptide, leptin) during the taper

Studies are inconclusive about hormonal monitoring usefulness.

### [TPOP-C04-032] Strength and power increase during the taper

Strength/power are usually suppressed by chronic intensive training and most likely recover when load is markedly reduced. Possible mechanisms: local changes in enzymatic activities and single-fiber characteristics improving neuromuscular, biomechanical and metabolic efficiency; reduced training may allow greater maximal tension via contractile mechanisms or neural control of fiber recruitment (Costill et al. 1985).
Findings:
- Costill 1985: 17 collegiate swimmers, 2-week taper: swim bench power +18%, actual swim power +25%; swim power gain correlated with 3.1% performance gain (r = .68).
- Johns 1992: tethered swimming power +5%, performance +2.8% after 10-14 days taper.
- Cavanaugh and Musch 1989: arm and leg mean power increased in 4-week taper, especially in the first 5-24 s of exercise; performance +2.6% average.
- Raglin 1996 (4-5 week taper): swimming peak power +16%, mean power +20%, neuromuscular function (soleus Hoffmann reflex, indicator of alpha-motoneuron pool excitability) +23%, correlated with power changes; competition velocity +2.0%. Neurological adaptations may contribute.
- Trappe 2001 (6 male collegiate swimmers, 3-week taper): swim bench power +7-20%, swim power +13%, performance +4%.
- Papoti 2007: 11-day taper with 48% reduction in weekly volume: swim force +3.8%, 200 m time trial +1.6%; swim force correlated with performance before and after.
- Trinity 2006 (elite swimmers): maximal arm power +10% and +12%, correlated with performance gains of 4.4% and 4.7%. Biphasic response: about 50%, 5%, 45% of total increase in taper weeks 1, 2, 3. Biphasic was most common; also early responders and late responders (Fig 3.9: biphasic n = 8, early n = 6, late n = 5), three nonresponders, two with constant increase.
- Prins 1991 and Hooper 1999: unchanged force, concluding pretaper force was not compromised by training load; differences may relate to swimmer caliber and programs.
- Shepley 1992 (cross country and middle-distance runners): maximal voluntary isometric knee extensor strength rose after both a high-intensity low-volume taper and a low-intensity moderate-volume taper, with unchanged motor unit activation; evoked peak twitch torque +13% and +19% respectively. Similar positive changes also occurred with one week of no training, which gave a 3% performance decline.
- Hooper 1998: peak tethered swimming force +12% without significant competition improvement; Houmard 1994: treadmill performance +2.8% despite no change in leg peak isometric/concentric force. Muscular force involvement depends on many physiological and environmental factors.
- Gibala 1994 (strength-trained): evoked contractile properties unchanged after 4 and 10 days; isometric elbow flexor peak torque +7.5% (day 6) and +6.8% (day 10) with unchanged motor unit activation; concentric peak torque at low velocity +7.7% (day 4), +2.8% (day 10). Attributed to enhanced contractile performance, neural activation, or both.
- Martin 1994: collegiate cyclists, 2-week step taper: isokinetic quadriceps strength improved at 30° and 120°/s but not correlated with lab cycling performance.
- Izquierdo 2007: 4-week taper: 1RM bench press +2%, concentric 1RM thigh-parallel squat +3%, no improvement in countermovement jump height.

### [TPOP-C04-034] Muscle fiber adaptations to the taper: size, metabolic and contractile properties

Fiber size:
- Trappe 2001 (male collegiate swimmers, 3-week taper): Type I diameter and CSA unchanged; Type IIa diameter +11%, CSA +24%.
- Neary 2003b (male cyclists, 7 days): high-intensity (85-90% max HR), low-volume (sessions progressively reduced 60 to 20 min) taper: Type I CSA +6.9%, Type II +14%. High-volume (60 min sessions), low-intensity (85% to 55% max HR) taper: Type I +7.0%, Type II +11%, not significant.
- Harber 2004 (cross country runners, 4-week taper): Type I diameter -4%, Type IIa unchanged.
Metabolic properties (Neary 2003b, only study available):
- High-intensity low-volume taper: myofibrillar ATPase +11% and succinate dehydrogenase +12% in Type I; in Type II, myofibrillar ATPase, SDH, beta-hydroxyacyl CoA dehydrogenase and cytochrome oxidase +15-16%; simulated 40 km cycling TT +4.3%.
- High-volume low-intensity taper: Type I cytochrome oxidase +10%, beta-hydroxyacyl CoA dehydrogenase +17%; Type II only beta-hydroxyacyl CoA dehydrogenase +18%; TT +2.2% nonsignificant.
- SDH and cytochrome oxidase activities of combined fibers correlated with 40 km TT performance after both tapers (Fig 3.12). Changes are more pronounced with a high-intensity taper; Type II fibers more responsive (specific contractile properties, greater potential to raise oxidative capacity).
Contractile properties:
- Trappe 2001 (deltoid, 3-week taper): Type IIa peak isometric force +30%, shortening velocity +67%, absolute fiber power +250%; Type I shortening velocity +32%. Type II fibers produced twice the peak power of Type I before the taper and five times after. Linked to whole-muscle strength/power gains.
- Harber 2004 (4-week taper): Type IIa peak force +9%; Type I peak force -9%; force normalized for size unaltered; Type I shortening velocity -17%, absolute power -25%, size-corrected power -15%; Type IIa velocity and power unchanged. Authors: 25% volume reduction with 67% increase in interval training was insufficient load reduction to elicit robust single-fiber power and run performance changes.
- Malisoux 2007 review: taper has little influence on size and force of Type I fibers; Type IIa more responsive (equal or increased CSA, increased force and power); adequate adjustment of volume and intensity seems necessary to improve single-fiber contraction velocity.
At a glance: oxidative enzyme increments and positive single-fiber changes are closely related to whole-muscle strength/power and performance.

### [TPOP-C04-035] Immune function during training and taper: background

Training responses of the immune system: increased leukocyte counts (neutrophils, lymphocyte subsets); decreased functional activity of the neutrophil respiratory burst and natural killer cytotoxicity; decreased mitogen-induced T-lymphocyte proliferation; decreased mucosal parameters such as secretory IgA; impaired delayed-type hypersensitivity (T-cell function); unchanged or increased circulating cytokines (interleukins, TNF-α, interferon-γ).
Few studies examine immunity during the taper; most look at acute postexercise effects. Temporary suppression after intensive exercise led to the J-curve (Nieman 1993) and open window (Hoffman-Goetz and Pedersen 1994) models. T helper subsets TH1 (cell-mediated) and TH2 (humoral/antibody) are thought to regulate control through cytokine signaling. Exercise-induced perturbations are relatively transient, but failure to restore baseline after repeated fluctuations may lead to chronic immunosuppression and increased illness risk in elite athletes over years (Gleeson 2000; Smith and Pyne 1997). Key issues are magnitude and duration of changes and whether they have clinical consequences or impair performance.

### [TPOP-C04-036] Immune cells and antioxidant status during the taper

- Short intensified training segments lower resting cell counts in controlled studies, but observational studies of athletes in training do not show the same.
- Dressendorfer et al. (2002a): 9 well-trained cyclists, 18-day taper: cycling efficiency +6%, simulated 20 km TT +6%; resting lymphocyte subsets and respiratory illness incidence unchanged over 10-week program.
- Coutts et al. (2007b): cell counts stable in 16 triathletes (4 weeks intensified training, 2-week taper). Kajiura et al. (1995): 12 male runners over 40 days heavy and easy training; transient reductions in CD4+ and CD4/CD8 mainly during high-intensity (1,000 m intervals) vs doubled volume.
- Mujika et al. (1996c): swimmers, 4-week taper after 8 weeks intense training: neutrophil percentage decreased, lymphocytes tended to increase; lymphocyte rise correlated with volume reduction (r = .86).
- Mujika et al. (1996c, 2002a): 6-day taper (80% reduction in HIIT) in 9 male middle-distance runners: neutrophils +13%, granulocytes +11%, too small to be immunologically significant.
- Wilson et al. (1996): 20 collegiate swimmers, 3-week taper (50% reduction in volume and intensity after 22 weeks at 18-20 h/week): total leukocytes up, B-cell lymphocytes down; total T-cells, neutrophils, lymphocyte proliferation unchanged — overall immunoprotection largely unchanged.
- Margaritis et al. (2003): 16 male triathletes, double-blind antioxidant supplementation: antioxidant supplementation during taper enhanced plasma antioxidant protection (Fig 3.14, total antioxidant status). Two weeks of taper decreased resting blood glutathione, erythrocyte superoxide dismutase activity and plasma antioxidant status, with no effect on lipoperoxidation or muscle damage markers.
- Child et al. (2000): 7-day taper did not enhance serum free radical scavenging capacity in 7 distance runners. Vollaard et al. (2006): no taper-induced changes in resting or exercise-induced oxidative stress markers.
Conclusion: small transient changes in immune cell distribution during the taper are unlikely to have substantial clinical consequences.

### [TPOP-C04-037] Immunoglobulins (SIgA) and cytokines during training and taper

SIgA:
- Marked reductions after acute exercise (Mackinnon and Hooper 1994) and late in a prolonged training season (Gleeson et al. 1995, 1999) are associated with increased upper-respiratory illness risk. Elite swimmers: SIgA fell 4% per month over a 7-month season; values below 40 mg/L were associated with increased illness risk (Gleeson et al. 1999).
- Other studies of swimmers and rowers did not show this association (Gleeson et al. 2000; Mackinnon and Hooper 1994; Nehlsen-Cannarella et al. 2000; Tharp and Barnes 1990). Interpretation must consider biological variability, experimental design, sampling, and assay differences.
Cytokines:
- Little is known about longitudinal changes during training and taper. Cytokines have roles in acquired and innate host defense and can be proinflammatory or anti-inflammatory. Most exercise work covers IL-1α, IL-6, and TNF-α/β; blood concentrations are usually unchanged or increased after acute strenuous exercise (e.g., marathon, Ostrowski et al. 1999).
- Eccentrically biased exercise (bench press and leg curl in untrained males): postexercise cytokine response (increases in IL-6 and IL-10) was less pronounced and occurred later (72-144 h postexercise) (Smith et al. 2000).
- Cytokine action is regulated by the balance between active molecules and their inhibitors, not circulating concentration per se. IL-1 and IL-6 form communication links among systemic circulation, energy metabolism, and skeletal muscle adaptation (Malm 2002).

### [TPOP-C04-038] Mood state and the taper: mechanism and findings

- Habitual physical activity is associated with positive mental health. Programs with high intensity, duration and frequency cause mood disturbances (alterations in the athlete's psychological state; Mondin et al. 1996).
- Mood states are sensitive to variations in training load (Morgan et al. 1987). Alterations are expected when the load is markedly reduced in a taper.
- Most reports show the taper induces positive mood changes, contributing to better performance (Table 4.1).
- POMS (Profile of Mood States): Morgan et al. 1987 and Raglin et al. 1991 found decreased global mood (disturbance) scores in college athletes tapering 4 weeks, with lower perceived fatigue, depression, anger and confusion and higher vigor.
- Raglin et al. 1996: decreases in mood disturbance were related to reductions in training load, with identical effects in males and females.
- Some individual athletes did not respond to the taper. Tension did not decline (higher in females than males) and was the only mood variable that stayed above baseline after the taper. It is speculated to reflect anxiety about the pending major championship (O'Connor et al. 1989).
- Taylor et al. 1997 (gender difference): female swimmers gained only 1.3% in competition, with mood deterioration: tension-anxiety +56%, depression-dejection +218%, confusion-bewilderment +86%, vigor-activity -20%.
- Flynn et al. 1994: 17% reduction in global mood state in male swimmers after a 3-week taper.
- Raglin et al. 1996: 16% decline in total mood disturbance in 12 collegiate female swimmers tapering 4-5 weeks. It correlated moderately with mean swimming power (r = .34), which rose 20%; competition swimming velocity improved 2.0%.
- Hooper et al. 1998 (state-level swimmers): reduced tension, depression and anger after 1 week; total mood disturbance 10% lower after 2 weeks; performance gains of 0.2% (100 m) and 0.7% (400 m).
- Hooper et al. 1999 (international-caliber swimmers): no change in total mood disturbance after a 2-week taper.
- Berger et al. 1997: young swimmers showed an acute drop in total mood disturbance (lower depression, confusion, tension) after shorter-than-usual practices in the precompetition taper week. These benefits did not appear related to subsequent competition performance.
- Berger et al. 1999: track cyclists tapering 2 weeks after overreaching had total mood disturbance -21%, 4 km pursuit +2.0% and mean power +2.3%. No substantial correlations between psychological and performance changes.
- Berglund and Säfström 1994: 9 male and 5 female world-class canoeists tapering 3 weeks had a lower POMS total. One week before the Olympics it matched their basal off-season value.
- Margaritis et al. 2003: 20 long-distance triathletes' POMS fell 10-12% after 14 days of taper, with total training load progressively reduced by 32-46%.
- Martin et al. 2000: cyclists tapering 1 week after 6 weeks of high-intensity intervals showed no change in total or specific mood scores (tension, depression, anger, vigor, fatigue, confusion), despite major differences in training load and performance. Some athletes with large mood disturbances responded well and others poorly, so the POMS has low predictability for taper response.

### [TPOP-C04-039] Recovery-stress measures and the taper

- Steinacker et al. 2000: 10 world-class male junior rowers with clear overreaching signs after 18 days of intense training recovered with a 1-week step taper. Somatic complaints and somatic relaxation (Recovery-Stress Questionnaire for Athletes, RESTQ-Sport) returned to baseline. Maximal incremental rowing power rose 2.7% and 2,000 m time trial improved 6.3%.
- Eliakim et al. 2002: junior handball players' self-assessed physical conditioning score, reduced by preceding intensive training, returned to baseline during 2 weeks of less intense training.
- Coutts et al. 2007b: triathletes did 4 weeks of normal or intensified training, then a 2-week progressive taper. The intensified group showed a marked decrease in total stress and increase in total recovery versus normal training. Recovery-stress state (total recovery minus total stress) tracked training load changes. Stress subscales Lack of Energy, Physical Complaints and Fitness/Injury decreased; recovery subscale Fitness/Being in Shape increased.
- Coutts et al. reported that an improved psychological state is an important contributor to post-taper performance gains and that a 2-week taper was sufficient to restore psychological recovery measures to baseline.

### [TPOP-C04-041] Perception of effort and the taper (RPE, HR:RPE, HLa:RPE)

- A fatigued, overstressed athlete perceives a given load as heavy; a fresh, recovered athlete perceives the same load as light. This is perception of effort, influenced by physiological and psychological variables (Borg et al. 1987; Noble and Robertson 2000; Watt and Grove 1993), some affected by taper. The most used measure is Borg's RPE (Borg 1970, 1982), which changes with tapered training.
- Morgan et al. 1987: perceived effort decreased in swimmers of both genders after a 4-week taper.
- Flynn et al. 1994: swimmers' RPE at 90% of preseason VO2max fell from an average of 14 (somewhat hard to hard) after 2 weeks of hard training to 9 (very light) at the end of taper. No RPE change in 8 male cross-country runners on a treadmill at 75% of preseason VO2max despite major load changes.
- No RPE change in distance runners at 80% VO2peak before/after a 7-day taper (Houmard et al. 1994) or in cyclists in a simulated 20 km time trial before/after a 7-day taper (Neary et al. 2003a).
- HR:RPE relationship may be a more valid monitoring marker. Neary et al. 2003a: HR:RPE ratio -4.5% after a 7-day stepwise taper (volume -50%), performance +5.1%. Martin and Andersen 2000: HR:RPE -3.2% after a 1-week taper in collegiate cyclists, with a 6% improvement in graded exercise to exhaustion.
- Predictor: change in HR:RPE during 6 weeks of high-intensity interval training strongly predicted taper performance response. Subjects with the greatest decrease in HR for a given RPE tended to have the greatest taper performance gains (r = .72) (Martin and Andersen 2000).
- Snyder et al. 1993: the submaximal blood lactate (HLa):RPE ratio is a valid physiological-psychological index of fatigue. Values reduced by 2 weeks of high-intensity interval training returned to baseline during 2 weeks of recovery training.

### [TPOP-C04-042] Why intensity must be maintained in the taper (mechanisms)

- Intensity is the key parameter for maintaining training-induced adaptations in reduced training. Hickson et al. 1985 (moderately trained; cycling and treadmill running): 10 weeks, 6 days/wk, 40 min/day at intensity approaching VO2max; then 15 weeks with frequency and duration kept but intensity reduced by one third or two thirds. Gains in VO2max, endurance (cycling time to exhaustion) and cardiac growth were NOT maintained. VO2max decreases were greater with the two-thirds reduction. Performance loss: 21% (one-third reduction) and 30% (two-thirds reduction).
- Shepley et al. 1992 (middle-distance runners): high-intensity low-volume taper vs low-intensity moderate-volume taper vs rest-only taper. Total blood volume, red cell volume, citrate synthase activity, muscle glycogen concentration, muscle strength and running time to fatigue were optimized only with the high-intensity, low-volume taper.
- Proposed mechanism: intensity regulates concentrations and activities of fluid-retention hormones (Convertino 1981, Mujika 1998).
- Mujika et al. 2000: high-intensity interval training during a 6-day taper correlated positively with % change in circulating testosterone in well-trained middle-distance runners, which could facilitate recovery before competition.
- Psychological role (McNeely and Sandler 2007): race-pace training in the final days is as important psychologically as physiologically; race-pace intervals give feelings of speed, power and confidence but should leave the athlete energized rather than fatigued.

### [TPOP-C04-043] Volume reduction: evidence

- Hickson et al. 1982 (moderately trained): after 10 wk training, 15 wk with duration cut by one or two thirds (40 → 26 or 13 min/day), intensity kept: VO2max, peak blood lactate, calculated left ventricular mass and short-term endurance (to exhaustion at VO2max intensity) maintained. Long-term endurance (cycling to exhaustion at ~80% VO2max) maintained with one-third cut (26 min) but fell ~10% (significant) with two-thirds cut (13 min).
- Standardized volume reductions of 50-70% retain or slightly improve adaptations in well-trained runners (Houmard 1989, 1990a, 1990b; McConell 1993) and cyclists (Martin 1994; Rietjens 2001). Progressive reductions up to 85% have produced performance-enhancing changes.
- Mujika et al. 2000 (6-day taper, middle-distance runners): progressive 75% reduction improved 800 m by 0.95%; 50% reduction was 0.40% slower. Negative correlation between distance of low-intensity continuous training and % change in testosterone in the taper.
- Shepley 1992: low-volume taper better than moderate-volume. Swimmers: positive relationship between performance gain and % volume reduction in a 3-week taper (Mujika 1995; r = 0.61, P < 0.01, N = 17).
- Bosquet 2007: gains highly sensitive to volume reduction (computed as area under volume–time curve); maximal gains with 41-60% reduction of pretaper training, though benefits occur with smaller and bigger reductions.
- Progressive 50-90% reductions reported beneficial in swimming, running, cycling, triathlon and strength training.

### [TPOP-C04-044] Frequency reduction: evidence and 'loss of feel'

- Hickson and Rosenkoetter 1981: recently trained individuals kept 20-25% VO2max gains from 10 weeks of training for at least 15 weeks with frequency cut by one third or two thirds (6 → 4 or 2 days/wk). Similar in strength-trained (Graves 1988). Physiological/performance measures retained or improved with 2-4 weeks of reduced frequency in cyclists, runners, swimmers.
- Johns 1992: swimmers cutting frequency 50% over 10 and 14 days of taper increased power and performance. Dressendorfer 2002b: significant improvement in 20 km cycling time trial simulation after 50% frequency reduction in a 10-day taper.
- Highly trained 800 m runners, 6-day taper: daily training (high frequency) gave significant 1.93% gain; resting every third day (33% reduction) gave nonsignificant 0.39%. No physiological differences between groups; attributed to potential "loss of feel" (Mujika 2002a); no psychometrics measured.
- Bosquet 2007: decreasing frequency not shown to significantly improve performance; frequency interacts with volume and intensity, so its effect is hard to isolate.

### [TPOP-C04-045] Meaningfulness of small taper gains

- Gains seem small and are sometimes statistically nonsignificant, but are worthwhile in practice. An improvement of about one half of the typical within-subject variation in competition performance substantially increases a top athlete's chance of a medal.
- Practical worthwhile magnitudes: 0.9% track sprinters (Hopkins et al. 1999); 1.4% highly trained swimmers (Stewart & Hopkins 2000); 1.5% cross-country runners; up to 3% marathon runners (Hopkins & Hewson 2001).
- Lab or field tests are much less reliable than competition, and gains in them may not translate to competition (Hopkins et al. 1999).
- Sydney 2000: improvement was similar in medal winners, finalists, semifinalists and heat swimmers. Among heat-only swimmers, males improved more (2.84% ± 1.67%) than females (1.23% ± 1.44%) (Fig 6.3, p<.01).
- Time gap 1st to 4th place: 1.62% ± 0.80% (males 1.48% ± 0.67%, females 1.77% ± 0.90%). 3rd to 8th: 2.02% ± 0.81% (males 1.69% ± 0.56%, females 2.35% ± 0.91%).
- The mean taper gain exceeded these gaps, so a very successful taper could take an athlete from last in the final to bronze, or from no medal to champion, provided the other finalists tapered less successfully.
- Bosquet et al. 2007: specific event performance is the best measure of taper effectiveness (performance is a system greater than the sum of its parts). Their meta-analysis excluded studies not using competition or field criterion data. The 1.96% could be meaningless for non-competitive populations but may have a major impact in major competitions.

### [TPOP-C04-046] Systems theory applied to training and tapering

- Banister and colleagues (Banister et al. 1975, Calvert et al. 1976, Morton et al. 1990) went beyond descriptive experiments by mathematically analyzing the relationship between performance and the training program. The approach is based on systems theory (Busso and Thomas 2006).
- Systems theory abstracts a dynamic process into a mathematical model. A system is an entity with at least one input and one output, related by a mathematical law called the transfer function. The output is the system's response to the stimuli (input). The transfer function characterizes the system's behavior through parameters estimated from real-life observations (figure 7.1).
- Applied to athletic performance: a whole-body approach in which performance is the system's output and past training is the input (figure 7.2: Athlete = System; training in, performance out).
- Performance is the balance between two antagonistic training effects: a positive effect ascribed to fitness adaptations and a negative one ascribed to fatigue (Fitness +, Fatigue -, summed). All model variables are in the arbitrary unit of the training impulse; the criterion performance is converted to a points score.
- The model has constant parameters that are unique to each individual and characterize that athlete's response to training. They are fitted for each athlete from the response to a given training program. Knowing them allows computation of fatigue and adaptation indexes over a specified training duration (Thomas and Busso 2005, Thomas et al. 2008).
- Uses of the models:
  - Explain taper-induced performance gains (Mujika et al. 1996a, competitive swimmers).
  - Identify the theoretical optimal taper duration (Fitz-Clarke et al. 1991).
  - Establish that a progressive reduction of training can be more effective than a step reduction, in agreement with data in triathletes (Banister et al. 1999, Zarkadas et al. 1995).
  - Simulate performance changes for training variations other than those actually done (Fitz-Clarke et al. 1991, Morton et al. 1990, Morton 1991, Thomas and Busso 2005).

### [TPOP-C04-047] Fatigue and adaptation model (Banister)

- Taper effects are described by the different time courses of fatigue and adaptation induced by exercise bouts. Training and performance measurements established a dose-response relationship based on the negative and positive influences of training (Banister et al. 1975). Performance = balance of fatigue (negative) and adaptation (positive).
- Controlled experiment (Morton et al. 1990): after 28 days of strenuous training, total work was reduced. Repeated training bouts could produce fatigue accumulation greater than adaptation, causing a transient performance decrease during intensified training.
- When training load is then reduced, fatigue (negative influence) dissipates more quickly than adaptation (positive influence), yielding criterion performance peaking (figure 7.3: fatigue dies away quickly, training effect persists longer; if training stopped, very good performance would occur at the point where fatigue has gone but fitness remains).
- Mujika et al. (1996a): 18 elite swimmers modeled over a full season including three tapers. A progressive reduction of training over 3 or 4 weeks alleviated accumulated fatigue without compromising adaptations. The performance gain was mainly attributed to reduction of the negative influence of training, although small gains in the positive influence also occurred and contributed (figure 7.4).
- The scheme should apply to many sports. Model adequacy was tested in long-distance running (Banister and Hamilton 1985), triathlon (Banister et al. 1999), weightlifting (Busso et al. 1990, 1992) and hammer throwing (Busso et al. 1994). Goodness-of-fit indicators showed acceptable description of training responses.
- Summary (At a Glance): fatigue increases more than adaptation in response to a bout; when load is reduced fatigue fades faster than adaptation, allowing peaking. In elite athletes the peak occurs mainly through fatigue dissipation, though adaptation can also increase. Models show the adaptation to the taper is individual and unstable over time (the same taper will not necessarily elicit the same adaptation every time).

### [TPOP-C04-048] Variable dose-response model (Busso 2003)

- Limitations of Banister's original (linear, time-invariant) model: it assumes the response to a given dose is independent of accumulated fatigue from past training. This implies:
  - taper durations should be identical whatever the severity of preceding training;
  - any training around 2 weeks before competition would be detrimental, so training should stop completely during the taper (Fitz-Clarke et al. 1991).
  These contradict evidence on detraining (Mujika and Padilla 2003b) and tapering (Mujika and Padilla 2003a; Mujika et al. 2004). The linear model describes training-induced fatigue imperfectly when training regimens change markedly (Busso et al. 1997, 2002; Thomas and Busso 2005).
- Banister's model nevertheless gave useful information: optimal taper typically between 2 and 4 weeks in competitive swimmers (Mujika et al. 1996a) and progressive reduction more efficient than step reduction (Banister et al. 1999).
- Fix: a recursive least-squares algorithm let parameters vary over time (Busso et al. 1997, 2002). Responses to one session varied with the difficulty of previous sessions. Busso (2003) proposed a nonlinear formulation: fatigue induced by a session depends on the severity of prior sessions. A session after repeated demanding sessions induces more fatigue than the same session during lower loads. Magnitude and duration of fatigue grow with repetition of sessions and reverse when training is reduced. Implication: restoring tolerance to training can contribute to taper peaking.
- Validation: six previously untrained subjects, controlled cycle ergometer program. The nonlinear model described responses more precisely than linear models, and suggested an inverted-U relationship between daily training amount and performance (figure 7.6): above the optimal amount, gains decline from fatigue (oversolicitation); large amounts of intensive exercise with insufficient recovery give smaller gains than expected; doses below the optimum also give smaller gains. The nonlinear model is better especially for the taper after a brief overload period.
- Elite application (Thomas et al. 2008): 4 female and 4 male national/international 100 m and 200 m swimmers, two seasons. Modeled performance fit real performance significantly in all eight. The fit supports the theory that a session well endured in habitual training is harder when training is intensified, and a more effective response is expected when training is reduced and fatigue fades. The example swimmer (representative of the group) reached best performances after a taper, when negative influence was low and positive influence high (figures 7.7, 7.8).
- Limitation: the model underestimated some of the better performances and overestimated the poorer ones. Fit in highly trained athletes in real conditions was poorer than in less-fit lab participants, probably because of more frequent standardized performance testing in nonathletes and the complexity of elite training, which precludes accurate quantification of all variables.
- Author's conclusion: despite insights, modeling cannot be considered a fundamental tool for coaches and athletes to design training programs; strong simplifications are barely compatible with program design requirements (Busso and Thomas 2006).
- Interpretation of taper: peaking arises from both recovery from past training and restoration of tolerance to training. Transient performance drops with intensified training are due to a changed response to the dose; progressive reduction lets the athlete respond more effectively. Intensification delays the positive influence of training; a subsequent reduction improves performance through delayed responses to past training plus early reaction to training done in the taper. This highlights the importance of maintaining sufficient training amounts during the taper.
- Possible physiology: neuroendocrine responses. Heavy training modulates the endocrine and autonomic nervous systems and could reduce adaptive capacity to training bouts (Kuipers 1998; Viru and Viru 2001). Reduced training changes the neuroendocrine environment, amplifying recovery and adaptation, so taper training can maintain or increase adaptation (Mujika et al. 2004).
- At a Glance: fatigue from a dose is bigger and lasts longer depending on prior training severity, reversed when training reduces; tolerance rises when well rested, so taper training contributes to peaking. The model describes training and taper responses better than the fatigue-adaptation model, but is still far from a practical tool for designing programs.

evidence: clinical-trial (Busso 2003; Thomas et al. 2008)

### [TPOP-C04-050] Why little research exists on tapering in team sports

Per Pyne et al. (2009), most tapering research is in individual (mainly endurance) sports, for two reasons: (1) in these sports there are moderate to large correlations between physiological capacities, basic training factors (volume, intensity) and competitive performance; (2) these factors are easier to isolate and quantify than the multifaceted demands, training and performance of team sports (Mujika 2007a). Team sports combine physical, physiological, psychological, technical and tactical factors. Since most team sports require speed, acceleration, power, endurance and agility, an effective taper would likely improve many or all of these attributes (Mujika 2007b).
Problems listed by Mujika (2007a) for team sport research:
- Physiological determinants of team sport performance are not clearly understood, unlike running, swimming, cycling or rowing. Physiological qualities are necessary but not the only requirement.
- Performance is hard to define (scoring more goals or points? higher tempo than the opposition? showing skills under pressure?). Actual performance is a relatively abstract concept versus precise numerical data.
- Training is hard to quantify: diverse activities, variable environmental conditions, interindividual variability in responses and adaptations.
- Long competitive seasons: e.g., a major European football club player competes domestically and internationally from mid-August to mid-May or June, and every other year national team competition follows. Elite players take part in more than 60 matches per season, so additional experimental physical demands are hard to impose.
- Relatively high injury risk makes longitudinal studies during the competition season difficult.
The author urges sport scientists to seek solutions to these problems.

evidence: author-experience (Pyne et al. 2009; Mujika 2007a, 2007b)

### [TPOP-C04-051] Taper gains are transient: detraining is fast

The adaptations behind the taper (hypervolemia, red cell production, oxidative enzymes, glycogen 13-34% higher in men and women) appear quickly when load falls, but they are also lost quickly if the stimulus becomes insufficient. A few days of insufficient training are enough to reduce blood volume significantly, lowering stroke volume and cardiac output; oxidative enzymes and glycogen synthase also drop very rapidly, contributing to fast losses of VO2max and glycogen stores. Practical meaning: the taper must keep enough training stimulus throughout (intensity held, frequency high) and should not be stretched without reason; this is why 8-14 days is the borderline between fatigue removal and detraining.
Mechanisms proposed for the gains: higher VO2max (hypervolemia, red cells, oxidative enzymes, greater O2 extraction); better energy cost of exercise in swimming and running (not shown in cycling), possibly via neural factors and strength/power gains; better aerobic endurance via glycogen (no change in fiber-type distribution or substrate use; RER usually unchanged).

### [TPOP-C05-015] Muscle, energy metabolism and oxidative stress terms

- ATP hydrolysis: Breakdown of adenosine triphosphate (ATP) to adenosine diphosphate (ADP) and inorganic phosphate (Pi), with addition of elements of water and releasing a large amount of energy.
- ADP rephosphorylation: Addition of a high-energy phosphate group to a molecule of adenosine diphosphate (ADP) to form ATP.
- creatine kinase: Muscle enzyme that facilitates the breakdown of phosphocreatine to creatine and inorganic phosphate; is occasionally increased in the blood following strenuous or eccentric exercise, most probably as a result of altered permeability of tissue cell membranes.
- muscle glycogen: A highly branched polysaccharide composed of a long chain of glucose molecules that is the major form of carbohydrate storage in the body.
- muscle oxygenation: Amount of oxygen that is present in the muscle.
- mitochondrial capacity: Capacity of mitochondria to generate adenosine triphosphate through oxidative phosphorylation.
- mitochondrial enzymes: Biomolecules, generally proteins, that catalyze chemical reactions inside mitochondria, which aerobically generate most of the cell's supply of adenosine triphosphate.
- Type I muscle fibers: Muscle fibers that have a high oxidative and a low glycolytic capacity, associated with endurance-type activities.
- Type II muscle fibers: Muscle fibers that have a low oxidative capacity and a high glycolytic capacity, associated with speed or power activities.
- free radical scavenging: Action of antioxidant chemical substances that protect body cells from the damaging effects of free radicals.
- glutathione: Antioxidant amino acid produced in the liver to protect body cells from the damaging effects of free radicals.
- lipoperoxidation: Oxidative degradation of lipids occurring when free radicals capture electrons from the lipids in cell membranes, resulting in cell damage.

### [TPOP-C05-017] Overreaching versus overtraining (Halson and Jeukendrup 2004)

Athletes experience minor fatigue and acute performance reductions from normal training. When training stress and recovery are imbalanced, overreaching and possibly overtraining may develop. Overreaching normally results from intensified training and can be a normal outcome for elite athletes; recovery takes a relatively short time (several days or weeks) and should result in a supercompensatory effect. Overtraining is a pathological outcome of long periods of excessive training and insufficient recovery, with long-term reduction of performance capacity; recovery can take months or years.

evidence: literature (Halson and Jeukendrup 2004)

### [TPOP-C05-020] Performance, perception and psychological assessment terms

- Borg's Rating of Perceived Exertion (RPE): A numerical scale for rating perceived exertion.
- perception of effort: Stress a subject perceives when performing physical exercise.
- Profile of Mood States (POMS): A 65-item Likert-format questionnaire that provides a measure of total mood disturbance and six specific mood states (tension, depression, anger, vigor, fatigue, confusion).

### [TPOP-C05-024] Red blood cell, iron and hemolysis terms

- hemoglobin: Protein composed of four globular polypeptide chains, each bound to a single molecule of heme, located in red blood cells and responsible for the transport of most blood oxygen.
- hematocrit: Percentage of total blood volume occupied by blood cells or formed elements.
- hematopoiesis: Blood cell formation and differentiation.
- erythropoiesis: Erythrocyte (i.e., red blood cell) production.
- reticulocyte: Immature red blood cell.
- mean corpuscular hemoglobin: The average mass of hemoglobin per red blood cell in a sample of blood, calculated by dividing the total mass of hemoglobin by the number of red blood cells in a volume of blood.
- deformability: The ability of cells, such as red blood cells, to change shape as they pass through narrow spaces such as the microvasculature.
- osmotic resistance: Degree of resistance of red blood cells to a decrease of the salt content of their environment.
- hemolysis: Breakdown (lysis) of red blood cells with liberation of hemoglobin.
- intravascular hemolysis: Breakdown of red blood cells within the blood vessels.
- extravascular hemolysis: Breakdown of red blood cells with release of hemoglobin in the spleen and liver.
- mechanical fragmentation: Breakdown of red blood cells by the continuous buffeting in the circulation.
- hemolytic condition: State is which there is a predominance of red blood cell breakdown over red blood cell production. [sic "is" as printed]
- haptoglobin: A glycoprotein that binds free hemoglobin released into the circulation to conserve body iron.
- glycoprotein: Protein containing a relatively small carbohydrate group attached to a large protein.
- ferritin: Iron-binding protein that stores iron in the body.
- transferrin: Iron-binding protein carrier for iron in plasma.
- erythrocyte superoxide dismutase: In red blood cells, enzyme that catalyzes the destruction of superoxide anions to hydrogen peroxide, playing a critical role in the defense of cells against the toxic effects of oxygen radicals.

### [TPOP-C05-027] Serum haptoglobin

A circulating glycoprotein that binds free hemoglobin released into the circulation (from red cell destruction) to conserve body iron. The haptoglobin-hemoglobin complex is rapidly cleared from the circulation by the liver. When high amounts of red blood cells are destroyed (heavy training), haptoglobin concentration decreases. When few red cells are destroyed (light training or taper), haptoglobin concentration increases because it circulates freely without released hemoglobin to bind. Serum haptoglobin thus serves as an index of the balance between red blood cell production and destruction.

### [TPOP-C05-029] Taper

Segment of reduced training before a major competition. Author's current view: a taper is, or should be, a time of reduced training load in the lead-up to a major competition; the load should be reduced progressively, mainly at the expense of training volume, and for a variable segment of time that depends on individual profiles of adaptation. It is intended to reduce accumulated physiological and psychological fatigue, enhance training adaptations and optimize sport performance. Mujika and Padilla 2000 definition: "A progressive, nonlinear reduction of the training load during a variable amount of time that is intended to reduce the physiological and psychological stress of daily training and optimize sport performance."

### [TPOP-C05-030] Taper-related training terms (taper, progressive taper, reduced training, periodization)

- taper: A reduction of the training load during several days prior to a major competition.
- progressive taper: A taper in which the training load is reduced in a gradual manner, as opposed to a sudden constant reduction.
- reduced training: A nonprogressive, standardized reduction in the quantity of training. This procedure has also been referred to as "step taper."
- periodization: Organized approach to training that involves progressive cycling of various aspects of a training program during a specific amount of time.
- intramuscular creatine phosphate supercompensation: Hypothetical elevation of creatine phosphate concentration inside the muscle cells beyond baseline values.
- open window: Time of impaired immunological function following strenuous exercise that increases the risk of subclinical and clinical infection.
- J-curve: Model describing the relationship between exercise workload and infection, which suggests that moderate exercise training may decrease the frequency of infections whereas excessive, exhausting exercise can lead to the opposite, a situation that has been described by a J-curve.
- neuroendrocrine fatigue: Fatigue associated with dysfunction of the central nervous system and the endocrine system.
- bye: In a tournament, the position of a player or team not paired with a competitor in an early round and thus automatically advanced to play in the next round.
- Super 14 club competition: The largest rugby union club championship in the southern hemisphere, consisting of four teams from Australia, five from New Zealand, and five more from South Africa.
- biphasic response: Two differentiated and distinct responses that are separated in time and characterized by an immediate reaction, a time of quiescence, and a recurrent reaction.
- meta-analysis: A statistical procedure in which the results of several studies are pooled together and analyzed as if they were the results of one large study.

### [TPOP-C05-034] VO2max (maximal oxygen uptake)

Fuel use depends on O2 availability and produces CO2 and water. Measuring volume and gas concentrations of inspired and expired air gives O2 consumed (VO2). VO2 rises with exercise intensity; at some intensity VO2 does not increase further, showing the subject has reached his maximal ability to increase VO2. This peak VO2 is maximal oxygen uptake, VO2max. Successful aerobic performance is associated with a high VO2max and with the ability to sustain a high percentage of VO2max for the duration of the event. It is the most widely used index of cardiovascular and respiratory fitness.
tags: type:definition sport:general system:aerobic subtopic:vo2max
src: Ch 2 sidebar What Is VO2max? | p. 31

### [TPOP-C05-035] Detraining

Partial or complete loss of training-induced anatomical, physiological and performance adaptations, caused by an insufficient training stimulus (Mujika and Padilla 2000). In the taper it is the risk on the other side of fatigue removal: reducing load too much (in volume, frequency or especially intensity) or for too long causes detraining instead of peaking.

### [TPOP-C06-010] Taper duration: evidence and recommendation

- Positive adaptations reported with tapers of: 4-14 days (cyclists, triathletes); 6-7 days (middle- and long-distance runners); 10 days (strength-trained); 10-35 days (swimmers). Overall, beneficial effects from 4 days to 5 weeks (4-28 days in summary). The window between a successful taper and detraining is not clearly established; complete inactivity has clear negative effects.
- Kenitzer 1998 (female swimmers): ~2 weeks was the limit of recovery/compensation before detraining became evident.
- Kubukeli 2002: athletes who trained harder and longer need ~2 weeks; those who reduced high-intensity training need a shorter taper.
- No study shows the duration or intensity of the target event determines taper duration.
- Default: when individual profile is unknown, 2 weeks is the "suit-all" duration; most athletes benefit from 2 weeks, but shorter or longer may be optimal for some. Individual profiles can be built with simple questionnaires of anxiety and perceived fatigue.
- Modeling (Mujika 1996a, swimmers): theoretical optimal duration between mean values of 12 and 32 days, large inter-subject variability, so duration must be individualized. Sources of variability: physiological and psychological responses to load reduction and use (or not) of overload before the taper (Bosquet 2007).
- Millet 2005 (4 professional triathletes): time for self-perceived fatigue to return to baseline was 15 days, close to modeled optimal taper times (Busso 1994, 2002; Fitz-Clarke 1991); a simple anxiety/perceived-fatigue questionnaire can adjust optimal taper duration.

### [TPOP-C06-012] Typical size of taper-induced performance gains

- Gains are usually 0.5% to 6.0% for competition performance measures.
- Gains can reach 22% in noncompetitive criterion measures (Table 6.1).
- Realistic goal for the final taper: about 3% improvement in competition performance (usual range 0.5-6.0%).
- International-level swimmers: the taper usually produces an average improvement of 1.5% to 2.5%.
- Bosquet et al. 2007 meta-analysis: mean performance improvement 1.96%. Effect sizes (Cohen 1988 scale) were most often small and occasionally moderate.
- Mechanisms the author cites for the gains: increased muscular force and power; neuromuscular, hematological and hormonal function; psychological status (chapters 2-4).
- Validity of performance tests, and their relationship to actual performance in a specific event, must be established.

### [TPOP-C08-002] Tapering models: linear, exponential, step (Fig 1.5)

Four designs described (Fig 1.5, % of normal training over 14 days): linear taper; exponential taper with slow decay; exponential taper with fast decay; step taper (also called reduced training).
- Linear taper: the training load is reduced in a systematic, linear fashion; usually implies a higher total training load than an exponential taper.
- Exponential taper: the training load is reduced in a systematic, exponential fashion, either slowly (slow decay) or suddenly (fast decay); total training load is usually higher in the slow-decay taper.
- Step taper: the training load is suddenly reduced by a constant amount (nonprogressive standardized reduction); often maintains but may even improve many physiological and performance adaptations (Mujika and Padilla 2003a; Banister et al. 1999; Mujika 1998; Zarkadas et al. 1995).
Planning a taper implicitly requires reducing training load.

src: Ch 1 | pp. 22-24
evidence: study

### [TPOP-C08-003] Alternative taper: reduce then increase load before competition (two-phase taper)

Bosquet et al. 2007 noted alternative strategies: an advanced reduction in training load followed by an increase in the lead-up to competition. Rationale: athlete uses reduced fatigue to enhance training tolerance and respond to taper training. Anecdotal support: progressive performance improvement from first round to final in multi-round competitions. Mathematical modeling simulation suggests it may be suitable (see chapter 7).
Thomas et al. (in press) model study: for each swimmer, optimal progressive (linear) taper after a simulated 28-day overload segment compared with a two-phase taper of same duration and same load reduction except the last 3 days, where load varied linearly for best individual performance.
- Highest performance came after a moderate increase in load in the last 3 days. Optimal variation: increase from 35% ± 32% to 49% ± 46% of normal training in swimmers (Fig 1.6).
- For one swimmer the optimal load kept decreasing in the last 3 days, but less quickly than in the first phase and linear taper; emphasizes need to individualize tapers to each athlete's profile.
- Optimal linear taper raised simulated performance by 4.0% ± 2.6% from end of 4-week overload; two-phase was slightly better by 0.01% ± 0.01% (Fig 1.7), due to a bigger gain in PI; NI (fatigue) was completely removed in both.
- Conclusion: a moderate load increase in the last 3 days does not appear detrimental; yields additional adaptations without compromising fatigue removal. Relevant to multi-round competitions where qualifying rounds increase load before the final.
Summary: maximal gains with progressive reduction particularly in swimming and running; step tapers may suit cycling; reduce-then-increase needs experimental testing.

### [TPOP-C08-004] Periodized psychological skills training (Balague 2000)

- Concept: periodization of the annual plan into phases leading to peak performance for the main competition has been embraced by sport psychology. Prof. Gloria Balague (Univ. of Illinois) proposed a model (Balague 2000): psychological skills relevant to performance can be learned and improved by training like physical skills.
- Goals: help athletes reach their potential by maximizing learning and performance and, more specifically, increase consistency by increasing athletes' control over their performance.
- Skill classification in three groups:
 - Foundation skills: motivation, self-awareness, interpersonal skills.
 - Performance skills: self-efficacy, self-regulation of physical arousal, cognitive and emotional self-control, self-regulation of attention.
 - Facilitative skills: lifestyle management, media skills.
- Steps: (1) assess the requirements of the sport/event; (2) evaluate the athlete's psychological attributes, deficits, strengths and weaknesses via interviews, observation and psychometric testing; (3) introduce periodized psychological skills training that follows closely the demands and goals of each training phase.
- Example, long and triple jump (Table 4.3): the precompetitive phase main goal is to finalize the jump and gain consistency. The psychological goal is to help the athlete identify elements of good performance so they can be reproduced: optimal arousal, optimal focus of attention, cognitive and emotional control. Interventions: learning to focus attention, developing precompetitive routines, identifying the most adaptive behaviors between jumps, identifying self-dialogue, effective cue words and images evoking energy, rhythm and confidence.
- Main competitive phase: the athlete must perform independently and consistently and adjust to changing conditions; trust skills and be flexible to anticipate unexpected circumstances. Interventions: a solid competition plan, post-competition evaluation of psychological focus and performance (to allow minor adjustments), and media skills training if needed.
- Coaches and athletes should use psychological training techniques to optimize psychological status before major competitions.

### [TPOP-C08-006] Optimal taper simulations in elite swimmers (Thomas et al. 2008)

Computer simulations (variable dose-response parameters from 8 elite swimmers' real training):
- Optimal step taper without prior overload: mean training reduction 65% over 16 days.
- Preceded by 4 weeks of intensified training: reduction not significantly different (67%) but maintained longer (22 days).
- Highest mean performance with optimal step taper: 101.4% of personal record with prior overload vs. 101.1% without (significantly greater with overload).
- Performance gain from start of taper: 3.60% after overload vs. 1.99% without overload.
- Progressive tapers (linear and exponential), with or without overload, required a significantly smaller reduction over a longer duration than the step taper.
- Optimal taper completely eliminates the negative influence (accumulated fatigue), even after overload. Taper acts essentially by fatigue dissipation; the benefit of prior overload comes from further adaptations during the taper. Overload hinders pretaper performance but a bigger gain follows an optimal taper.
- Summary of author: overload before taper is essential to maximize performance but imposes requirements. A 20% increase in normal training during 28 days before the taper requires a step reduction of around 65% during 3 weeks, instead of 2 weeks without overload. A progressive (linear or exponential) taper is preferable after overload but should last nearly twice as long as the step taper.
- Earlier simulation (Thomas and Busso 2005, nonathletes, 6 participants of Busso 2003): optimization needs the best compromise between extent and duration of reduction; optimal taper depends on prior training; intensified training before the taper allowed better performance but needed greater reduction over longer time; optimal progressive reduction beat optimal step reduction after overload. These model-derived characteristics did not match the literature well (Mujika and Padilla 2003a), likely because parameters came from a nonathletic lab population.
- Two-phase taper (Thomas et al. in press): the last 3 days optimized with a 20% to 30% increase in training load. Benefits versus a traditional linear taper were very small, explained by additional adaptations, with fatigue removal not compromised.
- Caveat: theoretical results must be tested experimentally before conclusive recommendations. Models are far from a tool to prescribe training for individuals; new modeling strategies are needed.

### [TPOP-C08-007] Single and multiple peaking in team sports

No study has directly examined the taper in the context of multiple peaking. In individual sports the major competition involves heats, semifinals and finals over several days or weeks (e.g., grand tours). In team sports, most national and regional competitions involve one or more games per week over a 4- to 8-month season. Because of this lack of research, it is not known how often an athlete or team can obtain the benefits of an efficient taper, so no sound recommendations can be made (Pyne et al. 2009). Team-sport tapers include variants for regular season peaking and major tournaments (Mujika 2007b). Physical preparation in these settings has evolved through trial and error rather than experimental research. Scarcity of studies implies a potential competitive advantage for teams that develop effective tapering strategies.

### [TPOP-C08-008] Peaking for a major tournament: recovery, rebuild, taper

Football players competing for clubs before major tournaments such as the World Cup, with reduced opportunities to taper, are among those most likely to underperform (Ekstrand et al. 2004). Tournaments come at the end of a long club season. Options: advance the domestic calendar so players rest and rebuild, or delay the end of the domestic season so players remain in competitive shape. Both have pros and cons; the literature is not conclusive.
Ideal approach suggested by the reports: start several weeks before the first game, with initial recovery after the club season, then rebuilding, and a pretournament taper with low training volume and high-intensity activities. The team taper should feature no reduction in intensity but a low training volume, as with individuals. The basic structure (relative rest after the regular season, serious training, taper) is reasonable for team coaches.
Choice between (a) recovering then rebuilding or (b) maintaining intensive training and capitalizing on domestic-season adaptations should depend on players' fatigue after the domestic season and the time between the end of domestic competition and the start of national team competition. Both can be valid.

### [TPOP-C08-009] Bob Bowman: planning from general to specific (macrocycles and microcycles)

- Principle: "Failing to plan is planning to fail." The coach inventories the elements of the quadrennial plan, then shapes shorter-term objectives within it.
- Athlete factors to assess first: experience in international competition; technical strengths and weaknesses; strength, power and endurance; overall health and ability to withstand heavy workloads; mental strength (or need for more seasoning under pressure).
- Planning progresses from general (long term) to specific (short term) and from simple (macrocycle) to complicated (microcycle).
- Macrocycles: after a broad goal is set (e.g. win gold at the Olympic Games), each year of the 4-year term has a major international meet and perhaps another important national competition. Each training year is divided into two macrocycles: one culminating in a national championship (or trials) and one in an international competition (World Championships). Each macrocycle contains transition, building, intensity, competition and specific periods, and includes a taper or peaking period. The international-competition macrocycle has the more extensive fine-tuning stage.
- Microcycles: the general outline of training stays consistent within each training week. The emphasis on qualities (aerobic endurance, muscle endurance, anaerobic power, mixed training) sets the theme of each period. Emphasis and volume change as the season progresses, but each type of training is included in every microcycle; this is the foundation of the taper plan and ensures every important energy system is hit in each block.

### [TPOP-C08-010] Bob Bowman: components of the taper (volume first, intensity maintained)

- Main challenge: keep control of the interacting elements of the taper. It is unwise to reduce volume and intensity simultaneously. First reduce volume while keeping intensity at the same levels.
- Formal start: approximately 21 days before the start of the first event at the major competition. One extra step is taken approximately 6 weeks prior.
- Managing volume by reducing sessions per week: about 6 weeks before the peak competition, swimmers go from four to three morning practices per microcycle. This guarantees consistent volume reduction, particularly early in the peaking period when demand for intensity (speed) is high, and gives a more uniform practice pattern through the taper.
- Intensity is the critical element and is maintained through the bulk of the taper. Only in the final days, when volume is at its lowest, is intensity reduced to maintenance levels.
- Phelps 2007 plan: gradual volume reduction in 1,000 m increments. Average in-season volume 6,000 m a.m. and 7,000 m p.m. Formal taper starts 20 days out and follows a 2-week period in which volume was gradually reduced by about 20% from peak levels. Intensity stays constant: threshold and quality training paces are the same; only the number of repetitions or time spent changes. The ratio of each type of work to total volume is unchanged.
- The plan accounted for a taxing trip to Australia and time-zone adaptation; the experienced traveler took precautions.
- Outcome cited: 7 gold medals and 5 world records at the 2007 World Championships.

### [TPOP-C08-011] Martin Fiz: marathon preparation structure (4 months, specific phase, volumes)

### [TPOP-C08-012] Martin Fiz: marathon taper (endurance-pace work, shorter recoveries, 21 days)

- Fiz's characteristics: 55.5 kg, 169 cm, maximum HR 188 beats/min, peak lactate 8 mM after progressive treadmill test (1% incline, 3 min increments), peak velocity 22 km/hr. Conclusion: hard to beat opponents in final meters regardless of anaerobic training; extra anaerobic work in the final phase only adds muscle trauma.
- Experience: tapering for 5,000 m caused more injuries (hamstrings, Achilles, calves) than marathon prep; in marathon taper minor muscle problems can be managed with massage/physiotherapy without stopping preparation. Main marathon risk: physiological deficiencies (below-reference micronutrient, blood and immunological values) from high volumes.
- Recommendation for runners training at continuous fast pace: taper to enhance strength/endurance. Example choice: (A) 4 x 2,000 m at 2:40 min/km with 6 min recovery vs (B) 8 x 2,000 m at 2:55 min/km with 2:30 min recovery. He chooses B: pace similar to competition, memorizes race pace, builds endurance. A is 15 s/km faster, higher stride cadence, makes athlete feel fast, but accumulates lactic acid never reached in a marathon and may lead to overpacing.
- Do not race short distances (15 km to half marathon) at high level 2 weeks before the marathon: higher lactate, longer recovery. Race at the same pace intended for marathon day. Taper with endurance-pace sessions.
- As cycles progress, pace may increase (not too much) and recoveries shorten. Early months: slower pace, longer recoveries.
- Perfect taper duration: 21 days. One week before, include a race of 15 to 21 km. Thereafter continuous running and pace intervals increase while recovery between repetitions decreases.
- Rationale: faster than months before with shorter recoveries ("recovery times are key") produces fitness peak, arriving fresh and "hungry", with race pace set in brain and legs.
- Longevity: career extended to age 37 (sixth at Sydney 2000); attributes to aerobic-focused programs. At 46 still performs the same taper with one session per day.
- Fiz would repeat his 1997 plan and tactics (second to Antón; no more anaerobic work), changing only to find an ally to set pace.

### [TPOP-C08-016] Winckler: final taper rules for sprinting and hurdling

- Psychological fitness: help athletes gain confidence in their physical skills and know how to use them; confident athletes are more focused and not overwhelmed by competition stress.
- During the early and middle competitive cycle, observe competition performance, determine the most critical errors and why, select coping mechanisms (physical or psychological) and develop them in training and competition.
- Approaching the peak event: continually practice familiar tasks believed critical; gradual decline in training volume while maintaining the high-intensity work used all year.
- In sprinting and hurdling use high-intensity, competition-specific runs at least once every 3 to 4 days to maintain fitness and technical rhythm and competence.
- Do not abandon strength training: continue short, intense bouts of strength work right up to the day before competition.
- Do not introduce new exercises or protocols; stay with the familiar and proven, seeking better timing and execution.
- Summary: slightly reduce volume, keep similar intensity, and perform sessions at the time of day of the main event.

## C09 Decision Rules & Heuristics

### [TPOP-C09-001] Iron status risk during the taper

Enhanced erythropoietic activity in bone marrow during the taper could jeopardize iron status. A prelatent-latent iron deficiency profile (normal red cell count and hemoglobin but lowered ferritin, serum iron and transferrin saturation, increased transferrin) was found in middle-distance runners at the end of 6-day tapers, without apparent negative effect on competition performance (Mujika 2002a, 2000). Lowered posttaper ferritin also in male cross-country runners after a 3-week taper (Pizza 1997) and triathletes tapering 2 weeks (Rudzki 1995), but not in swimmers (Mujika 1997). Erythropoiesis requires significant iron.

### [TPOP-C09-002] Rules of thumb from Chapter 2 for interpreting taper data

- If VO2max drops during the taper in an endurance athlete, suspect a poorly planned taper.
- Keep training intensity while reducing volume; reducing intensity while keeping volume gave smaller, nonsignificant gains.
- Volume reduction of 41-60% is suggested as required for optimal results in most athletes (chapter 5); 25% (Harber 2004; Smith 2000) was insufficient for economy/lactate changes.
- In the 7-day cycling study, -50% volume worked; -30% and -80% did not (Neary 2003a).
- Abnormal HR changes (high resting, reduced maximal, raised submaximal) after taper suggest pretaper overreaching/catecholamine depletion.
- Expect unchanged or lower submaximal lactate and higher peak lactate after an efficient taper.
- Rising ferritin loss/iron profile: monitor iron.

### [TPOP-C09-004] Immune health management in the taper (at a glance)

- Athletes should be mindful of excessive loads during peak training but can train with confidence during the taper prior to competition in the knowledge they are unlikely to compromise overall immunological protection.

### [TPOP-C09-005] Using psychological markers to read taper response

- Expect mood disturbance, RPE, stress and somatic complaints to fall and vigor/recovery to rise when the load is reduced after intensive training; sleep movements fall.
- Tension may stay high before a major event: do not read it as taper failure.
- Individual responses vary (some athletes did not respond; POMS predicted taper response poorly in Martin et al. 2000): monitor each athlete individually and do not rely on POMS alone.
- Mood improvements are not always correlated with performance changes (Berger et al. 1997, 1999).
- A worsening mood state (Taylor et al. 1997, female swimmers) was associated with only small gains (1.3%) and can be a warning sign.
- HR:RPE change during the preceding high-intensity block predicts the taper performance response (r = .72).

### [TPOP-C09-006] Rule: do not decrease training intensity during the taper

Research clearly shows training intensity should be maintained during the taper: it is key to retaining aerobic power, circulating anabolic hormones and feelings of speed and power; high-quality training in the taper can further enhance adaptations. If intensity is diminished, some adaptations may be lost, leading to suboptimal performance. Bosquet et al. 2007 concluded the training load should not be reduced at the expense of intensity. In every case (overall and per sport), not decreasing intensity was preferable (see Tables 5.1, 5.2).

### [TPOP-C09-007] Effect of pretaper overload on taper duration (Thomas 2008 simulations)

Mathematical modeling simulations (Thomas et al. 2008):
- A 20% increase over normal training during 28 days before the taper requires a step reduction in training of around 65% during 3 weeks, instead of 2 weeks when no overload training is performed.
- A progressive taper requires a smaller reduction of training over a longer duration than a step taper, whatever the pretaper training.
- Rationale: overload training before the taper causes greater stress requiring a longer time to recover. Furthermore, higher training loads could make adaptations peak at a higher level but take longer to produce.
- Practical rule: overreaching an athlete before the taper requires not a bigger percentage training reduction during the taper, but rather a longer taper.

### [TPOP-C09-008] Using the data to set realistic performance goals

The data give a quantitative framework for setting realistic goals from the athlete's pre-taper performance. Expected improvement: about 3%, range 0.5% (inefficient taper) to 6% (particularly efficient taper).
Examples:
- Elite 100 m breaststroker, pre-taper 62.00 s: 3% gain gives 60.14 s; 0.5% gives 61.69 s; 6% gives 58.28 s (world record–smashing).
- Highly trained 5K runner, pre-taper 14 min 10 s: 12 min 45 s would be unrealistic (about a 10% improvement). Likely range 14 min 6 s (0.5% gain) to 13 min 19 s (6% gain).
Caution: unrealistic expectations may harm the athlete's psychological state and motivation; avoid them. Assess taper effectiveness after the major competition against realistic targets.

### [TPOP-C09-009] Individual differences: why some athletes do not improve

- Eight swimmers in Mujika et al. 2002b did not improve; unchanged performances occur in various sports (Table 6.1).

- Placebo/expectancy effect (Vollaard et al. 2006): subjects cannot be blinded to training changes, and well-trained athletes know a taper is beneficial, so expectancy may account for part of the gain and may relate to individual variability. This is plausible but still to be investigated.

### [TPOP-C09-012] Luc Van Lierde: principles of tapering for triathlon

- Tapering is the final critical stage of training, essential to obtain maximal benefit from intensive training. There are no rules: responses vary widely; some prefer long tapers, others short. Each athlete must develop an individual procedure.
- Mistake: using the final sessions as a test, which may cause excess energy expenditure.
- His experience: low-intensity sessions in the taper are very fatiguing, high-intensity ones less so; constant tiredness and empty legs during taper, which he interprets as readiness; once he gets this feeling he is ready to perform.
- Nutrition: a carbohydrate-rich diet during the 4 days before competition is pivotal, since muscles need fully repleted glycogen stores for maximal endurance capacity.
- Short vs long: for long-distance events he reduces volume and intensity much more than for short-distance triathlon. For short-distance races he needs high-intensity stimuli until the day before; muscle tone must remain higher than for an Ironman. Several short stimuli in the days before a short event are recommended to obtain speed and explosiveness.
- "There are no secrets in training or tapering, rather some logical rules to which each individual must adhere."
Background credentials: 4th Olympic-distance Worlds after 18 months of triathlon; Ironman Hawaii wins 1996 and 1999; world long-distance record 7:50:27 (1997); 8th at Ironman Hawaii 2007 aged 38.

### [TPOP-C09-013] McFarlane: key idea (At a Glance)

### [TPOP-C09-019] Water polo (McFadden): taper and peaking principles

- Team of 13 (6 field players plus goalkeeper in water). Unequal playing time: players with little time detrain unless given extra work; players with most pool time need recovery or lighter sessions. Goal: all 13 as close as possible to physical peak.
- Break: 2 to 3 days off training (except a light strength session) 5 to 10 days before the major competition, so minimal muscle soreness on return; mentally as important as physically (refresh, forget what lies ahead). Timing/length depend on the prior 6 to 10 weeks.
- Intensity/duration: lead-up tournaments or camps with opposing countries give near-competition intensity; winning matters for confidence and referee expectations. Camps make equal pool time easier to control than tournaments. Pool time tracked with a daily athlete monitoring sheet filled online. With a good uninterrupted base, maintaining fitness can be one or two fitness (swim or game simulation) sessions per week.
- As competition nears, session duration decreases but intensity stays high (e.g. two quarters instead of four, played at 100% quality and intensity).
- Skill/tactics: preparatory phase seven 2- to 3-hr sessions per week (basic skills, individual position skills, tactics); about 75% of tactical work can be at full game conditions. Rest days: two 1-hr sessions; game day: single 1-hr session (not always used; weigh travel). Session structure: first 40 min in two groups, 20 min sprints and recovery vs passing and shooting, then swap for 20 min; last 20 min tactical work on next opponent.

### [TPOP-C09-022] Sport-specific reading of the meta-analysis (cycling, running, swimming)

The authors' own interpretation of the per-sport data (Table 2 of the article; same data as C15-005, C15-011, C15-013, C15-014, C15-015). Samples per sport were too small for firm sport-specific rules, but the trends are:
- **All sports:** maintain training intensity. This is the one indisputable result.
- **Volume cut:** 41-60% is optimal in swimming. In cycling and running no clear cutoff was found; the optimal cut lies somewhere between 21% and 60%.
- **Duration:** 8-14 days looks optimal in cycling and running. Swimming also shows significant gains with longer tapers (15-21 days). Longer tapers could not be tested in cycling (N = 0 for 15-21 and ≥22 days) or running (N = 10).
- **Cycling open questions:** which pattern (step vs progressive) and whether frequency may be reduced are both still controversial.
- **Rowing:** excluded (2 studies, N = 23).
Data base: swimming 8 studies (N = 249), running 9 studies (N = 110), cycling 6 studies (N = 80).
Default generic taper: 2 weeks, volume reduced exponentially by 41-60%, intensity and frequency unchanged. Alternative designs may work for individuals, with larger interindividual differences.

### [TPOP-C09-023] Cut volume by shortening sessions, not by dropping sessions

Training volume can be reduced by shortening each session or by reducing the number of sessions. The meta-analysis favors shortening sessions, because reducing frequency did not produce a significant performance improvement (C15-014). Frequency interacts with intensity, pattern and duration, so its own effect is hard to isolate; the conservative rule is to keep frequency at ≥80% of pretaper value.

### [TPOP-C10-001] Energy balance and body composition during the taper

Margaritis 2003: 20 male long-distance triathletes, 4 weeks overload then 2 weeks taper. Energy intake unchanged (13.8-15.0 vs 13.2-15.0 MJ/day); energy expenditure fell from 16.8-17.0 to 12.1-12.7 MJ/day; body mass unchanged; body fat rose slightly from 11.4-11.5% to 11.8-12.1%. No significant change in body mass/fat: female swimmers 2- and 4-week tapers (D'Acquisto 1992); strength-trained athletes 4-week taper (Izquierdo 2007); runners 3-week step taper (Houmard 1990a,b); collegiate runners and swimmers 3 weeks with 20-33% weekly volume reduction (Flynn 1994); 9 male cyclists 10 days resting every other day (Dressendorfer 2002a); collegiate swimmers (Van Handel 1988). These did not report fat/muscle mass changes.

### [TPOP-C10-005] Taper nutrition: energy intake down, carbohydrate up

- **Energy:** Margaritis et al. 2003 (20 male long-distance triathletes, 4 weeks overload then 2 weeks taper): energy intake unchanged (15.0 ± 3.2 vs 15.0 ± 3.3 MJ/day) while expenditure fell from 17.0 ± 1.9 to 12.1 ± 1.2 MJ/day; body mass unchanged (67.9 vs 67.8 kg) but body fat rose 11.4% → 11.8%. Match intake to the lower expenditure.
- **Glycogen:** muscle glycogen rises during the taper, and the size of glycogen supercompensation was associated with the size of the 40 km TT improvement (Neary 2003b). Use nutrition strategies that maximize glycogen replenishment.
- **Women:** Walker et al. 2000 (6 eumenorrheic women): a 7-day taper with 78% carbohydrate in the final 4 days gave more glycogen and better performance at 80% VO2max than 48% carbohydrate for 7 days.
- Conclusion of the authors: a carbohydrate-rich diet is an important component of a successful taper.

### [TPOP-C11-002] Sleep quality and the taper

- Sleep is a compensatory mechanism following catabolic processes of daytime activity. Sleep disturbance is often associated with excessive training loads and overtraining, defined as "an imbalance between training and recovery, exercise and exercise capacity, stress and stress tolerance" (Lehmann et al. 1993, p. 854).
- With reduced exercise load, slow-wave sleep pressure is reduced, resulting in lower levels of slow-wave sleep and increased rapid eye movement (REM) sleep (Hague et al. 2003).
- Taylor et al. 1997 (elite female swimmers): sleep-onset latency, time awake after sleep onset, total sleep time, and REM sleep time were unchanged during the taper. However:
  - Slow-wave sleep, which represented 31% of total sleep time during peak training, fell to 16% following the taper, suggesting that the need for restorative slow-wave sleep is reduced with reduced physical demand.
  - The number of movements during sleep was reduced by 37% after the taper, indicating less sleep disruption compared with periods of higher training loads.
- Hooper et al. 1999: slightly improved quality of sleep in 7 highly competitive female swimmers after 2 weeks of tapering for the Australian national championships (measured at onset of training, peak, and taper, ~3 months apart).
- Table 4.2 (sleep measures, mean ± SD, seven athletes; Taylor et al. 1997) was removed from the source due to rights limitations: [MISSING].
- At a Glance / Summary: Sleep is the most important recovery mechanism for an athlete, but its quality is often altered during intensive training. During tapering, athletes tend to sleep better (fewer movements/disruptions), which together with reduced physical demand facilitates recovery and optimizes competition performance.

### [TPOP-C11-009] Measuring fatigue before the taper

## C12 Adaptations by Population / Context

### [TPOP-C12-001] Taper-induced economy adaptations by athlete caliber

Gains in economy of movement (reduced oxygen cost at a given submaximal exercise intensity) during a taper appear to be inversely related to the initial caliber of the athlete:
- Lower-caliber / less skilled athletes: High school swimmers tapering 2 or 4 weeks improved economy curves by 4.9% to 15.6% and 8.5% to 16.7% across swimming velocities (D'Acquisto et al. 1992), likely mediated by volume reduction allowing improvements in stroke biomechanics and mechanics. Intercollegiate swimmers showed 5% to 8% declines in oxygen cost after 10-14 days of taper (Johns et al. 1992).
- Elite / high-caliber athletes: In Olympic-caliber and national championship swimmers, who are already highly economical, taper did not elicit significant changes in economy curves (Van Handel et al. 1988). Similarly, highly trained runners and cyclists often show minor or unchanged submaximal oxygen costs during taper unless substantial biomechanical refinement or recovery from severe prior fatigue occurs.

### [TPOP-C12-002] Muscle glycogen supercompensation in female athletes during taper

In eumenorrheic female endurance athletes tested during the luteal phase of their menstrual cycle, a 6-day taper combined with a high-carbohydrate diet (78% CHO) consumed during the final 3-4 days elicited a 13% higher muscle glycogen concentration compared to a moderate-carbohydrate diet (48% CHO) (Walker et al. 2000). Concomitantly, cycling time to exhaustion at 80% to 82% VO2max increased by 8%.
Key sex distinction: While women successfully supercompensate muscle glycogen during the taper, the magnitude of supercompensation is smaller than that generally reported in male athletes under comparable glycogen-loading protocols.

### [TPOP-C12-003] Environmental factors: jet lag and the taper

- Long-haul flights cause travel fatigue (transient, overcome by rest and sleep). Crossing meridians desynchronizes circadian rhythms (jet lag; body clock in hypothalamus); lasts days depending on zones crossed, direction, departure/arrival times, individual factors. Impairs muscle strength, reaction times, arousal.
- Lower training load while desynchronized (compensates reduced capability, avoids injury risk especially with complex practices). This acts as a temporary taper and should be integrated into the overall program and taper.
- Allow time to adjust completely to the new time zone before competing; readjustment can be part of the lowered taper volume.
- Do not train in the morning after traveling eastward (promotes phase delay instead of desired advance).
- Little point training hard at home before departure (arriving tired slows adjustment); shifting body clock phase for days before departure is counterproductive (disrupts training quality).
- Taper should proceed as planned despite jet lag. Napping at an inappropriate time may delay resynchronization; a short nap of ~30 min can be restorative in some circumstances. Immune suppression is more linked to sleep disruption than jet lag itself. Jet lag displaces appetite rather than reducing energy intake.
- Staff should apply strategies to minimize travel stress before departure, in flight and on arrival.

### [TPOP-C12-004] Environmental factors: heat and altitude and the taper

- Interactions of taper with heat, cold, altitude are little studied; additive effects of altitude with climatic stress/jet lag lacking (Armstrong 2006). Still, environmental effects must be considered systematically in the annual plan.
- Camps (altitude or heat) rely on adaptations transferring to competition during the taper; absolute intensity is lowered by necessity, uncoupling physiological and biomechanical loading, with unknown consequences.
- Heat: tapering in heat is compatible with the reduced volume advocated for heat stress; increased glycogen use in heat should be offset by reduced intensity and duration. Athletes must be heat-acclimatized or performance may suffer. Winter sports: no equivalent problem (clothing, shelter, rehydration).
- Altitude: VO2max falls with ambient pressure, so intensity/power at a given relative load is lower. First days: respiratory alkalosis (self-limiting via renal compensation). Reducing load is imperative at altitude before increasing it during acclimatization. Extra hydration need (dry air, initial diuresis, plasma volume changes), increased carbohydrate use, propensity to sleep apnea run counter to taper support; here the reduced load does not substitute for a taper. Added illness risk (decreased immunoreactivity). Maximal cardiac output may fall over a typical 14-21-day sojourn due to impaired training quality. Place altitude camps strategically in the annual plan.
- Altitude training is accepted at elite level (swimming, rowing) despite lack of compelling evidence; optimal timing of return to sea level unknown; athletes advised to reduce training before competing after altitude (a form of taper); extent and individual variation not explored.

### [TPOP-C12-005] Sex effect on taper response

Evidence is inconclusive; there is no evidence that tapers should be designed differently for males and females.
- Mujika et al. 2002b: mean improvement higher in males (2.57% ± 1.45%) than females (1.78% ± 1.45%); 200 m events 2.91% ± 1.38% vs 1.80% ± 1.35% (p<.05); freestyle 2.54% ± 1.50% vs 1.60% ± 1.33% (p<0.05). The study was observational and uncontrolled for motivation, shaving, suits or diet, so there is no apparent explanation.
- Mujika et al. 1996a: females improved 2.58% ± 1.96%, 2.95% ± 0.93% and 2.56% ± 2.19% after tapers of 3, 4 and 6 weeks, not different from males (3.12% ± 1.15%, 3.42% ± 2.19%, 1.60% ± 0.92%). Female event range from 0.47% (100 m breaststroke, n=1) to 5.42% (100 m butterfly, n=1); 2002b: loss of 0.53% in 400 m freestyle (n=2), maximum gain 3.04% in 200 m butterfly (n=4).
- Hooper et al. 1993, 1998, 1999: small nonsignificant gains of 0.1-0.7% in both sexes, not reported separately.
- Smith 2000: no sex difference after a 1-week taper in 6 female and 10 male elite rowers.
- Duration: Kenitzer 1998 recommended 2 weeks for female swimmers, since performance indexes began to deteriorate with longer tapers. Taylor et al. 1997 linked the 1.3% female gain to mood deterioration. Contrary: Mujika 1996a found no sex difference in optimal duration by mathematical modeling; Morgan 1987 and Raglin 1991 found similar changes in perception of effort, global mood and total mood disturbance.

### [TPOP-C12-006] Regular season: preseason periodization and in-season maintenance

Coaches understand that having all players at peak fitness for a whole season is not possible. It is important to prescribe periodized conditioning programs in the preseason so physical capacities are optimal at the onset of the competitive season. Intensive preseason programs should finish with a taper to facilitate supercompensation and near-optimal values in strength, power, endurance and repeated sprint ability.
Once the season starts, maintaining the preseason peak depends on: time between games, travel, competitiveness of the opposition, injury, minutes of match play, physiological adaptations to competition, recovery, and training of individual players. All must be integrated into the in-season plan to retain or further improve early-season fitness. A team is also a group of individual athletes: basic training principles effective in individual sports also apply in team sports.

### [TPOP-C12-007] Bob Bowman: event- and athlete-specific taper considerations

- Men and women differ, particularly in dry-land training: men need much more rest from intense land work; women must work to maintain strength throughout the taper.
- Volumes are higher for long-distance swimmers than for middle-distance or sprint athletes.
- Older swimmers generally need more rest and less volume than younger athletes.
- Phelps, Vanderkaay and Keller followed the same general plan, with slight deviations. Phelps divided time among the four strokes (individual medley swimmer). Vanderkaay and Keller trained exclusively for 400 m and 200 m freestyle. Keller (not particularly muscled, distance background) was given more aerobic mileage. Vanderkaay was given slightly less volume because of his muscular build and the residual fatigue that can occur during sharpening training.

### [TPOP-C12-008] Rollason: psychology in the final week and during a meet

- Consider each swimmer's personality, especially in the last 5 to 7 days and for rooming. Two highly excitable swimmers are not a good match.
- In an 8-day meet, match roommates by when they finish competing (e.g., one racing days 1, 2, 4 with another racing days 4, 7, 8 is undesirable).
- Mind the emotional cost, especially for 50 m swimmers racing the last 2 to 3 days; performance can be compromised if overstimulated by peers' fast swimming.
- Jodie Henry 2004: kept her name out of the press; after her 52.95 s relay split she was talked of as the winner and got off track; coach reminded her to stay in the moment. Race instructions covered only the 10 to 15 min before the gun (what to do on pool deck), not the race itself; same tactic used with Alice Mills at the 2003 Worlds. Too many swimmers swim their race before the gun.
- Did not talk about what the opposition would do. What you do not say is as important as what you say; timing is everything; do not say the right thing at the wrong time.

### [TPOP-C12-009] Vatkin: imperfect preparation, lessons from cases

- 1994 Worlds Brisbane: after nearly missing the competition (public train, lost), relief removed pressure; Ivan Ivankov had his best competition, won all-around despite the weakest group (strongest compete later, a major advantage).

### [TPOP-C12-010] Olazabal: golf has no taper; mental timing and long season

- Annual structure vs. traditional taper: Golf does not feature a traditional training taper because professional golfers face a continuous competitive season extending from mid-January through late December. Athletes cannot peak "in crescendo" for a single annual peak; rather, the challenge is maintaining baseline physical competitiveness and achieving precise mental timing across scattered major championships.
- Mental timing over technical obsession: Peak performance depends on achieving "the mind factor" rather than physical or technical over-analysis.
- Competitive milestones and mental lessons:
  - Ryder Cup 1987 (Columbus, Ohio): Entered with low self-esteem after a winless season. Irish pro Des Smyth advised enjoying good shots equally with lamenting bad ones. Relying on team cohesion and partner Seve Ballesteros, Olazabal experienced his first victory driven by the "mind factor" rather than physical aggression.
  - World Series 1990 (Firestone, Akron): Arrived dissatisfied with his form; entered "the zone" (described as an inner bubble of stillness, absence of external distraction, perception of slow motion) from the opening shot. Shot 61 (tournament record), opened with birdie-eagle-birdie-birdie (first-round 62), won by 12 strokes at 18 under par.
  - Masters 1994 (Augusta): Prior form was poor for two years until February 1994. Took 1 week of complete rest, followed immediately by two U.S. tune-up tournaments (placing 8th and 2nd), then entered the Masters maintaining a low mental profile without overthinking, resulting in victory over Tom Lehman.
  - Masters 1999: Returning from an 18-month debilitating injury; lacked self-belief until veteran Gary Player reprimanded his defeatist attitude during the Champions Dinner. Shifting immediately into self-belief, he defeated Greg Norman to claim his second Masters jacket.
  - Ryder Cup 2006 (The K Club, Ireland): Illustrated collective psychology ("collective catharsis"), where 12 players plus captain united as an unstoppable single unit ("13 souls pulling in the same direction represent a mighty locomotive").

### [TPOP-C12-011] Team sport adaptations: equalizing load among squad members

In team sports not all players receive equal playing time. Those who play less risk detraining and need extra work before major tournaments; those who play most need recovery or lighter sessions. ## C13 Contraindications, Cautions & Safety Limits

### [TPOP-C13-001] Taper cautions: iron status and energy intake

- Iron: increased red cell production during the taper may compromise iron status (lower ferritin, serum iron, transferrin saturation seen after 6-day to 3-week tapers). WHO: tapering endurance athletes. WHY: erythropoiesis requires iron. No numeric limit given.
- Energy intake: reduced training lowers energy expenditure; athletes must match energy intake to reduced expenditure to avoid energy imbalance and undesirable body composition change. No exact amount given.
- Pre-taper overreaching: abnormally high resting/submaximal HR or reduced maximal HR may reflect overreached status, not taper effect.

evidence: author-experience
src: Ch 2 | pp. 26, 28, 33

### [TPOP-C13-002] Cautions from Chapter 3 (training load and taper content)

- WHAT: excessive loads during peak training; LIMIT: avoid sustained uncompensated overload; WHO: athletes in heavy training phases; WHY: short intensified segments suppress resting immune cell counts; repeated fluctuations without baseline recovery can cause chronic immunosuppression and elevated illness risk over years (Gleeson 2000; Smith and Pyne 1997); EXCEPTION: the taper itself is safe and unlikely to compromise immunity.
- WHAT: complete rest during taper week (no training for 7 days); LIMIT: avoid zero-training taper; WHO: competitive runners/athletes; WHY: Shepley et al. (1992) found 1 week of no training caused a 3% performance decline despite positive muscular strength gains.
- WHAT: insufficient volume reduction paired with excessive interval increases; LIMIT: 25% volume reduction with 67% increase in interval training was inadequate; WHO: runners; WHY: Harber et al. (2004) showed this load profile failed to elicit single-fiber power gains or running performance improvements.
- WHAT: mucosal immune depression (salivary IgA); LIMIT: values below 40 mg/L; WHO: elite swimmers / endurance athletes; WHY: associated with increased incidence of upper-respiratory tract infections (Gleeson et al. 1999).
- WHAT: excessive low-intensity continuous running volume during the taper; LIMIT: reduce continuous distance in taper; WHO: middle-distance runners; WHY: plasma CK rose with increasing continuous running distance during taper, indicating ongoing muscle damage; high continuous running volume also correlated inversely with testosterone (Mujika et al. 2000).
tags: type:caution level:elite subtopic:immune-function subtopic:overload phase:taper
src: Ch 3 | pp. 52, 55, 63, 67, 69-70
evidence: author-experience

### [TPOP-C13-003] Cautions from Chapter 5: detraining, altitude, jet lag

- WHAT: reducing intensity in the taper. LIMIT: avoid (do not cut intensity). WHO: all, esp. highly trained. WHY: loss of VO2max, endurance, cardiac growth (21%/30% performance loss when intensity cut by one/two thirds in Hickson 1985); suboptimal competition performance.
- WHAT: cutting duration by two thirds (to 13 min/day). WHY: ~10% loss of long-term endurance (moderately trained).
- WHAT: reducing frequency in highly trained athletes. LIMIT: keep >80% of pretaper. WHY: loss of feel, especially technique-dependent sports.
- WHAT: complete inactivity. WHY: negative effects readily apparent.
- WHAT: altitude camp near competition. WHY: dehydration, carbohydrate use, sleep apnea, reduced immunity/illness risk, possible reduced cardiac output; reduced load there is not a taper.
- WHAT: morning training after eastward travel; badly timed naps; hard training before departure; pre-shifting body clock. WHY: delay adjustment.
- WHAT: competing in heat without acclimatization. WHY: performance compromised.
tags: type:caution sport:general phase:taper level:all subtopic:detraining subtopic:altitude subtopic:jet-lag
src: Ch 5 | pp. 76-86
evidence: review

### [TPOP-C15-003] Banister 1999 / Zarkadas 1995: exponential vs step taper in triathletes

Only one intervention compared progressive vs nonprogressive tapers in highly trained athletes (triathletes). After 1 month of intensive training:
- 10-day exponential taper: +4.0% all-out 5K criterion run; +5.4% peak power in ramp cycling test to exhaustion.
- 10-day step taper: nonsignificant +1.2% and +1.5% respectively.
After six more weeks of intensive training, 13-day exponential taper with time constant of decay of training volume fast (τ = 4 days) or slow (τ = 8 days) [τ symbol missing in text, shown as blank; values per author's wording]:
- Fast: +6.3% (5K run) and +7.9% (peak power).
- Slow: +2.4% and +3.8%.
Conclusion: exponential taper better than step taper; fast-decay (low-volume) protocol more beneficial than slow decay.
Summary: progressive tapers seem to have a more pronounced positive impact than step tapers, although step tapers can also improve performance.

### [TPOP-C15-016] Mujika et al. 2002b: Sydney 2000 Olympic swimmers, final 3 weeks of training

Observational study of 99 individual swimming events in the final 3 weeks of training (generally coincident with the taper) before the Sydney 2000 Olympics.
- Overall change for all swimmers: 2.18% ± 1.50%; maximum loss -1.14%; maximum gain 6.02%.
- 91 of 99 performances were faster; 8 were slower.
- Improvements did not differ significantly among events: from 0.64 ± 1.48% in 400 m freestyle (smallest mean) to 2.96 ± 1.08% in 200 m butterfly (largest) (Table 6.2).
- Males improved 2.6% (2.57% ± 1.45%), somewhat lower than some earlier values in the literature; the likely cause is the higher performance level of these swimmers.
- No significant difference between distances (Fig 6.1) or between freestyle and form events (backstroke, breaststroke, butterfly, IM) (Fig 6.2). Implications: the metabolic contribution that varies with race distance does not affect the potential taper gain; technical and biomechanical aspects do not necessarily affect the taper outcome.
- No significant difference among swimmers of the 14 countries, from 0.13% ± 0.28% (Nigeria) to 3.98% ± 2.18% (Swaziland).
- Table 6.2 (performance times before and after F3T) was removed from the source for rights reasons: [MISSING].

### [TPOP-C15-017] Other swimming taper gains cited for comparison

- Costill et al. 1985: mean 3.1% after a 2-week taper, 17 collegiate male swimmers. By event: 2.2% in 100-yard freestyle (n=2) and 200-yard butterfly (n=3); 4.6% in 200-yard IM (n=5).
- Costill et al. 1991: 3.2% in 24 college male swimmers, 2-3 week taper.
- Johns et al. 1992: 2.8% ± 0.3% with a 10-14 day taper. Range: minimum 2.0% in 100-yard breaststroke (n=1); maximum 3.7% in 100-yard (n=5) and 200-yard (n=2) freestyle.

### [TPOP-C15-018] Linear mixed model and residual effects in swimmers (Avalos 2003, Hellard 2005)

- Avalos et al. (2003) used a linear mixed model as an alternative to Banister's model, in highly trained swimmers over three consecutive seasons. Instead of a personal model per athlete, it builds a population model whose parameters vary between individuals (subject heterogeneity).
- Findings: short-, mid- and long-term training adaptations are individual; an athlete's reaction to a given stimulus is variable. Individual adaptation is not stable over time, so an identical reaction to an identical load repeatedly applied should not be expected.
- Hellard et al. (2005), Olympic-caliber swimmers: multiple regression of performance on training variables in the short term (weeks 0, 1, 2 before competition), intermediate term (weeks 3, 4, 5) and long term (weeks 6, 7, 8). A modified model included threshold saturation, above which training elicits no further adaptation.
- Main taper outcome: low-intensity training load during the taper related to performance by a parabolic relationship (figure 7.5; performance as % of personal record vs. training load as % of each athlete's maximal load). Low-intensity training should decrease to 40% to 50% of the maximal training load during the taper; smaller or larger decreases may jeopardize optimal performance. This agrees with observations in various sports (see chapter 5).

### [TPOP-C15-019] Coutts et al. 2007a: overreaching and taper in rugby league players

Semiprofessional rugby league players did 6 weeks of progressive overload training with limited recovery, then a 7-day progressive taper.
After overload: multistage fitness test performance significantly reduced (12.3%); most strength, power and speed measures tended to decrease (range -13.8% to -3.7%). Significant changes in plasma testosterone to cortisol ratio, creatine kinase, glutamate, and glutamine to glutamate ratio (p < .05).
After taper: significant increase in peak hamstring torque and isokinetic work; minimum clinically important increases in multistage fitness test, vertical jump, 3RM squat, 3RM bench press, chin-up and 10 m sprint. Biochemical markers tended to return to baseline.
Conclusion: overload produced overreaching, most likely explained by increased muscle damage via decreased anabolic-catabolic balance; a progressive taper may induce supercompensation in strength, power and endurance related to increased anabolism and decreased muscle damage.

### [TPOP-C15-024] Bosquet et al. 2007 meta-analysis: design and headline results (supplementary source)

Supplementary source added to this KB; Mujika is a co-author and the book reproduces its tables.
- **Search:** Embase, Kinpubs, Physical Education Index, PubMed, SportDiscus, Web of Science; 182 potential studies.
- **Inclusion:** competitive athletes; taper procedure fully described; actual competition or field-based criterion performance; data to compute effect sizes. Duplicate datasets excluded. 27 studies included (10 used competition data, 17 reliable field tests).
- **Analysis:** pre-post standardized mean differences weighted by within-group heterogeneity; Cohen scale (0.2 small, 0.5 moderate, 0.8 large); volume reduction computed as area under the volume-time curve. Two independent coders, agreement 0.927.
- **Headline:** 2-week taper (ES 0.59 ± 0.33), volume reduced exponentially by 41-60% (ES 0.72 ± 0.36), intensity unchanged (ES 0.33 ± 0.14), frequency unchanged (ES 0.35 ± 0.17), all P < 0.001. Expected gains are small to moderate (ES 0.35-0.72).
- **Performance change:** range -2.28% to +8.91%, mean +1.96%.
- **Meaningfulness:** the smallest change that matters for a top athlete's medal chances is about one third of the typical competition variation, which is about 0.5-1% in swimming and running. So a ~2% taper gain is large in practice (Sydney 2000: 2.2% gain vs 1.62% gap gold-4th and 2.02% gap 3rd-8th).
- **Robustness:** repeating the analysis without the 4 studies that cut intensity did not change any effect size or the dose-response curves.
- **Sex:** only 9 of 27 studies had mixed samples and 3 reported sexes separately; apart from a possible smaller gain in women's freestyle (Sydney 2000), no evidence against applying the results to women.
- **Two-phase taper** (reduce then increase before competition): no experimental data in highly trained athletes at the time.

## C17 Validity Notes

### [TPOP-C17-001] Step vs progressive taper: conclusion firmer than the data

Table 1.1 (Bosquet 2007) gives a larger mean effect for step tapers (0.42, n = 98, p = .12) than for progressive tapers (0.30, n = 380, p = .0001). The book concludes progressive is better; the difference in significance comes mainly from the unequal samples, which the meta-analysis authors flag. Also, a Ch 1 At a Glance describes progressive tapering as decreasing "in a stepwise manner", which blurs its own distinction. Use: prefer progressive, but do not treat step as wrong.
tags: type:caution level:all subtopic:validity subtopic:taper-pattern
src: Ch 1 | pp. 10-11

### [TPOP-C17-002] Cycling may favor step tapers

By sport (Table 1.2), cyclists showed a large mean effect with step tapers (2.16, wide CI, trend) and a non-significant one with progressive tapers (0.28), the opposite of swimming and running. The author acknowledges it ("step tapers may suit cycling") but keeps the general recommendation. Small, unequal samples. Use: for cyclists, both patterns are defensible; individualize.
tags: type:caution sport:cycling level:all subtopic:validity subtopic:taper-pattern
src: Ch 1 | p. 11

### [TPOP-C17-003] Optimal taper duration is stated several ways

- Meta-analysis: 8-14 days best (Bosquet 2007).
- Default recommendation: 2 weeks.
- Modeling: 12-32 days (Mujika 1996a); 2-4 weeks in swimmers (Ch 7).
- Simulations: 16 days without overload, 22 days with overload (Ch 7), stated elsewhere as "3 weeks instead of 2" (Ch 5, 7); 65% vs 67% reduction for the same scenario.
- Ranges of benefit: "4 to 28 days" (Ch 5 summary) vs "4 days to 5 weeks" (At a Glance).
- Practitioners: about 21 days (Bowman, Fiz, Rollason, Winckler), 4 weeks (Queally).
Use: 2 weeks when the profile is unknown, longer after overload, then individualize from experience.
tags: type:caution level:all subtopic:validity subtopic:taper-duration
src: Ch 5, 7, 9-10 | pp. 81-83, 118-125

### [TPOP-C17-004] Frequency recommendation rests on indirect evidence

The meta-analysis found no significant harm from reducing frequency (Yes 0.24, p = .08 vs No 0.35, p = .0001). The >80% rule for highly trained athletes rests mainly on one 6-day study in 800 m runners (1.93% vs 0.39% gain) with no physiological difference between groups ("loss of feel", not measured). Use: keep frequency high in elite and technique-dependent athletes, but the evidence is weaker than for intensity and volume.
tags: type:caution level:all subtopic:validity subtopic:frequency
src: Ch 5 | pp. 78-80

### [TPOP-C17-005] Sex differences: conclusion vs evidence

The book concludes there is no evidence that tapers should differ by sex. But it also reports: bigger gains in males at Sydney 2000 (2.57% vs 1.78%), Kenitzer's 2-week limit for female swimmers, and Taylor's mood deterioration with a small gain (1.3%) in females. Other studies (Mujika 1996a modeling; Morgan 1987; Raglin 1991; Smith 2000) found no difference. Bowman (Ch 9) adjusts dry-land work by sex from experience. Use: same design by default; watch individual response.
tags: type:caution level:all subtopic:validity subtopic:sex-differences
src: Ch 4, 6, 9 | pp. 72-76, 96, 125-128

### [TPOP-C17-006] Typical gain is stated three ways

About 3% (range 0.5-6.0%) for goal setting; 1.5-2.5% in international swimmers; 1.96% mean in the meta-analysis; 2.18% in Sydney 2000 swimmers. Use: for elite athletes, 2% is a more conservative central estimate than 3%.

### [TPOP-C17-007] Mechanisms not fully explained; expectancy effect

Performance improves with or without VO2max change, and the author states the mechanisms are not completely understood. Most markers correlate with performance in some studies and not in others. Expectancy (placebo) cannot be excluded because athletes cannot be blinded to a taper (Vollaard 2006). Use: judge a taper by performance in key sets and competition, not by single markers.

### [TPOP-C17-009] Limits of the meta-analysis (Bosquet 2007, stated by its authors)

- Results assume pre- and post-taper tests were maximal efforts; few studies controlled this (Neary used end-test RPE; Houmard reported ICC 0.99). A placebo effect plus extra effort in the final test may overestimate gains.
- Unequal subjects per category: duration (N = 164, 176, 84, 54 for 1-4 weeks), pattern (98 step vs 380 progressive) and frequency. Only the volume categories are balanced, so 8-14 days and "progressive is better" are less certain than the 41-60% volume result.
- Volume reduction had to be estimated in some studies, adding measurement error (wide categories used to limit it).
- Selection bias: researchers mostly test tapers already known to work, which can lead to circular thinking; innovative designs remain untested.
- The type of progressive taper (linear vs fast or slow exponential) could not be analyzed; the "exponential" recommendation rests on Banister et al. 1999.
- Smallest worthwhile change: the article uses one third of typical competition variation; the book (C04-045) uses about one half. Both lead to the same practical conclusion.
tags: type:caution level:all subtopic:validity subtopic:meta-analysis

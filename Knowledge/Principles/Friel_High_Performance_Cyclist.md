## C01 Overview & Scope

### [HPC-C01-001] Overview & Scope

**What the book is.** Joe Friel's High-Performance Cyclist: The Complete Training Manual (Bloomsbury Sport, 2025) is a self-coaching manual for serious cyclists. Friel is an exercise-science MSc, coach of novices through Olympians for 40+ years, and co-founder of TrainingPeaks. Nine chapters each open with a story about an athlete he coached, include expert sidebars, and close with Key Takeaways. An Appendix holds the workouts and field tests, followed by a Glossary and References.

**For whom.** Riders aiming at a goal at the edge of their own ability. "High performance" is personal and does not require winning or elite status. Events covered: road races, criteriums, stage races, time trials, gravel, XC mountain bike, cyclocross, virtual races. Less sport-specific but helpful for ultra-distance, track, gran fondo, cyclosportive, randonneuring and century rides. Triathletes may use it, but it has no swim or run guidance.

**Problem it solves.** Self-coached riders tend to:
- ride too hard on easy days (the "middle zone" trap);
- skip the aerobic Base;
- under-rest and under-sleep;
- fail to identify their limiter;
- overtrain.

The book supplies a complete, flexible season design and the decision rules to run it.

**Central thesis.**
- High performance comes from consistent, individualized, periodized training.
- The progression runs frequency, then duration, then intensity.
- It rests on a very large base of easy aerobic riding in zones 1-2, with only about two hard days a week once intensity starts.
- Lifestyle (sleep, R&R, nutrition, mindset, stress) matters as much as workouts.
- Training targets the rider's limiter (aerobic capacity, stamina or sprint power) while maintaining strengths.
- The plan is flexible, not carved in stone.

**Organization.**
- Prologue: principles.
- Ch 1 Getting Started: team, goals, equipment, physical assessment and bike fit.
- Ch 2 Training Basics: lifestyle determinants and the three fundamentals.
- Ch 3 Exertion: zones (Table 3.1), Training Triad, testing, HR vs power.
- Ch 4 Planning: polarized vs pyramidal, periods, weekly routines, volume.
- Ch 5 Training Details: strength, basic and advanced abilities, season table.
- Ch 6 Stress and Rest: overtraining, R&R, HRV, PMC.
- Ch 7 Mindset: self-assessment and mental tools.
- Ch 8 Common Questions: nutrition, weight, limited time, missed workouts, age.
- Ch 9 Race Day: heat, altitude, race week, race day, post-race.
- Epilogue: simplicity, obsession, enjoyment.
- Appendix: workouts and tests T1-T4.

**What it does NOT cover.**
- Swim and run training.
- Detailed sports nutrition, micronutrients and individual dietary needs (vegetarian, allergic, diabetic). The author refers these to a dietitian and recommends sport-nutrition books.
- Other periodization models (see his Cyclist's Training Bible); this book uses linear periodization only.
- Bike fit specifics beyond the sidebar.
- Clinical diagnosis of overtraining.
- A guaranteed formula: "There is no secret, perfect way of training."

### [HPC-C01-004] Chapter 3 Exertion: scope and workout elements

Chapter 3 covers intensity (how great exertion is during a workout or race), which the author describes as the most complex and confusing topic in endurance sports.
Workout elements determining race performance:
1. Frequency: how often you ride. High-performance cyclists typically get about six rides weekly.
2. Duration: how long (in hours) each weekly ride is. Workouts typically range from about an hour to several hours depending on priority race, time until race, available time, workout purpose, and current capacity. (Detailed in Chapters 4 and 5).
3. Intensity: how easy, moderate, or hard workouts are. Very intricate, and the element riders are most likely to get wrong intentionally, upsetting training and race performance.

Chapter content includes: options for setting training zones (Table 3.1), the Training Triad of six abilities (basic and advanced), Ratings of Perceived Exertion (Borg 1–10 scale), time to exhaustion per zone, tests to measure high-performance progress, and when to use heart rate versus power.

### [HPC-C01-005] Ch 6 Stress and Rest: purpose and central thesis

Balancing stress and rest is described as the hardest part of self-coaching. Getting this balance right week after week is often the difference between a good race and a poor one, and it is the most likely culprit for a lackluster season. If stress and rest are not balanced, the athlete is either overtrained or undertrained, and both have dire consequences. The author calls this the single most important reason for having a training plan. The equilibrium must be reflected in the plan and followed up daily. It will seldom be perfect, but there must never be an exceptionally prolonged shift toward either side ("yin and yang"). Upsetting a well-balanced program for a few weeks may cost the entire season or more.

## C02 Philosophy & Core Principles

### [HPC-C02-001] Core principles (summary)

**What the author optimizes for**
- Reaching a personally challenging goal and improving relative to your past self, not beating others.
- Long-term health and a long cycling career, including enjoyment of the ride.
- Adaptation (becoming fitter), not just feeling recovered.

**Principles of training**
1. Individuality. Athletes differ in genes, lifestyle and response time. Zones, R&R frequency and plan timing must be personalized, and "if you are sure there is a better way for you, you win."
2. Specificity. Train for the goal event. The season starts non-specific (cross-training, weights, easy riding) and becomes race-like as the A race nears.
3. Progression. Start from a manageable load and raise it only at a rate the body adapts to.
4. Reversibility. Fitness is lost if load falls. This is why A races are limited and tapers are kept short.

**Non-negotiables**
- Consistency above all. Better to do the wrong workouts consistently than the right ones inconsistently. A single missed workout is not a big deal.
- Mostly easy riding in zones 1-2. Easy builds the engine; hard fine-tunes it. The scales tilt "significantly toward the easy side."
- Sleep of at least 7 hours of actual sleep, 8 better. Fitness happens during sleep, and alarm clocks are discouraged.
- R&R days are not optional.
- Power meter plus heart rate monitor, with RPE as the primary intensity marker.
- Base before Build. Base is the most important period. The duration stage can be extended, the intensity stage must not be.
- One A race per season, two at most (12+ weeks apart), never more than three.
- Identify and train the limiter, and maintain strengths.
- A flexible written plan.
- Listen to your body. Abandon or shorten a workout rather than miss several days.
- Safety over data. Health over fitness.

**What the author rejects**
- "Every workout must be grueling." Also "intensity addicts" and zone 3 creep.
- Beating others as the measure of success.
- Fast group "races" during Base.
- Long high-intensity blocks (the Build period is 8-9 weeks at most).
- Unrealistic or conflicting goals.
- Unsupportive people.
- Obsession dressed up as consistency ("exercise is strong medicine" with a right dose).
- Ultraprocessed food and "training is a ticket to eat junk."
- Rapid weight loss.
- Trying anything new on race day.
- Treating recovery gadgets as more important than sleep, food and fluids.

**Why (author's reasoning)**
- Physiology: low intensity builds stroke volume, blood volume, capillaries, mitochondria, EPO and fat oxidation, which underpin high-intensity work.
- Hard work creates only potential; rest converts it into fitness.
- The brain is the most basic performance tool, so mindset is trained like the body.
- Commitment and patience are required: "Training is thought of in years, not weeks."

### [HPC-C02-002] Definition of high performance

- High performance = the level of performance that achieves YOUR challenging goal this season (high for you), and then building on it in future seasons. It is not limited to elite racing.
- Performance is about you and your goal, not about beating others. A podium goal implies beating others, but the measure remains your own goal.
- Finishing an event can be a high-performance achievement depending on the rider's current level.
- Reason to avoid "who you beat" as the measure: it is narrow and egotistical, and the result often depends on who showed up and in what shape.
- Better outcomes to measure: performing higher than before, getting faster under similar conditions, regardless of others.
- Being high-performance is challenging but not complicated: have a dream and pursue it persistently and patiently.
- Riding must have a purpose accepted and pursued every day, week and month. Fun is allowed, but serious rides require focus.

### [HPC-C02-003] No guaranteed formula; athlete-coach collaboration

- The author has no guaranteed formula and there is no secret perfect way to train. Each athlete is unique.
- The rider must pay close attention to training details to find the best way for them. Questions to answer about yourself:
  - How do you respond to frequent workouts?
  - How often do you need a day off?
  - When should extended rest and recovery (R&R) breaks be?
  - Do long rides cost you heavily?
  - After high-intensity intervals, are you wiped out for days or do you bounce back quickly?
  - What is the best diet leading up to the race?
  - How quickly do you respond to training?

- Keep a very close eye on progress and change the plan as needed.
- If a past approach made you a high-performance rider, continuing it is acceptable. If you have not achieved what you sense you are capable of, work through the whole book.

### [HPC-C02-004] Four principles of training: individuality, specificity, progression, reversibility

1. Individuality: you have unique genes, talents, needs, lifestyle and habits. What works for a pro or a training partner is not necessarily right for you.
2. Specificity: train for your goal event. The closer to the event, the more training must resemble the race.
   - The season starts with non-specific work, e.g. cross-training and weight lifting in the very early season, with only a little bike riding at first.
   - Non-bike work is gradually cut back and bike focus increases. The change starts several weeks before the first high-priority race.
   - In the last few weeks, training matches the expected demands of the race.
3. Progression: start at a manageable load and gradually increase it over the season at a rate the body can easily adapt to. Adaptation is the key to progression. Do not overwhelm with workouts that leave you wasted.
4. Reversibility: stopping or greatly reducing training for a long time loses fitness.
   - Caveat: training load is reduced in the last few weeks before the most important race, so you "come into form".
   - Purpose of coming into form: shed fatigue while keeping fitness as high as possible, with only a very small loss. Result: you feel great on race day.

### [HPC-C02-005] Coach-athlete relationship and the team approach

- The coach-athlete bond is at the heart of training. You must like and trust each other because you face problems and make decisions together. The author credits this relationship for much of Steve's success.
- Method: a "team" approach with coach and athlete at the core. If the athlete is sure there is a better way for themselves, the athlete's way wins; the athlete knows themselves better and the coach respects how they respond to training.
- Honesty: the author never lies to or exaggerates expectations to an athlete, because it comes back to haunt both. This requires a flexible plan. Unanticipated change is the norm: no athlete has ever finished a season without significant alterations to the annual plan.
- Typical coaching conversation topics (Steve example, weekly phone call plus daily workout-file review): how the athlete is feeling and thinking, feelings about recent training, what is needed going forward, possible plan changes, upcoming lifestyle, and next week's workouts.
- Because this book is a distant coach without conversations, the reader is directed to JoeFrielTraining.com (blog search, then "About" contact) if confused.
- Guest view (Dr. Andrew Kirkland, University of Stirling, Chartered Scientist, triathlon coach):
  - Athletes' beliefs and values differ from the coach's, and humans do not always act rationally or truthfully with themselves.
  - Maladaptive behaviors (exercise addiction, overtraining, eating disorders, Relative Energy Deficit in Sport, REDs) can emerge; he says they are prevalent in high-performance cycling, more than 30 percent in some instances.
  - The coach's job is to help athletes be honest with themselves and with the coach: understand what drives behavior and support more adaptive behaviors.
  - Relationships fail with mismatched expectations and unclear roles (e.g., rider wants only workouts, coach is holistic) or when imposed, like an arranged marriage.
  - Athlete should be able to answer "Why me?" when choosing a coach. A good relationship is intimate, long-lasting, built on openness, respect, trust.
  - Distinguish "want" from "need": address wants short-term, and over time help the athlete understand needs. Coaching only to wants may succeed in business but be less sustainable for performance.

### [HPC-C02-006] Your high-performance team (support system)

- A truly high-performance goal requires a team; more supportive people means greater chance of success.
- Core: family and friends. They must know the goal, why it matters, and that it will stretch you to your limits. Offer to help them in return; if a parent, this models a great lesson for kids.
- Cycling team: tell them the goal. They can help in the race and give event knowledge (terrain, surfaces, weather, typical competitors). Help teammates with their goals in return.
- No team: treat regular training partners as a team; share your goal and return the favor.
- Experts to recruit locally (or follow on social media and contact if stymied): sport science, health, bike fit, equipment, mechanics, training, sport psychology, data analysis, nutrition. Do not overstay your welcome.
- Key requirement for every team member: happy, positive people who never doubt or mock you or your goal. Avoid people lacking these qualities; they drag you down.
- Takeaways: success depends largely on the support team; share goals with family and friends; include cycling team or training partners; a team of experts makes the goal more achievable.

### [HPC-C02-007] SMART goal criteria and goal-setting rules

- Specific: goal includes an action verb (win, finish, improve, qualify, achieve) and a specific event (Steve: win the USA 20km National Time Trial Championship, 60+ category).
- Measurable: how will you know you succeeded? (Steve: top step of podium, partly dependent on who shows up; still set the bar high.) Running-type finish time also works.
- Achievable: knowing yourself, can you reasonably expect it, given focused preparation? Nobody knows months ahead; that is what sport is about.
- Relevant: why is this goal important to you (mental side). Steve: time trialing was his strength and he wanted to test himself nationally.
- Timed: a date by which it will be achieved (the date of your A event).
- Goal must meet all five criteria, excite you, and feel really challenging ("Can I really do that?"), yet not be impossible. An unrealistic goal equals no goal. A goal already done and easy has no excitement.
- Multiple goals must be complementary, not contradictory. Example: if Steve had added a goal to lose weight to climb better at another A road race, the muscle loss would have reduced power and hurt the flat time trial (on a flat course weight matters little; wind resistance/drag dominates and drag differences between large and small riders in aero position are inconsequential).
- A goal without a plan is a wish; the plan is built in Chapters 4 and 5.
- Takeaways: action verb; measurable part; achievable; appropriate; timeline.

### [HPC-C02-009] Predictors of goal success: five mental predictors

- Predictors fall into physical (e.g., aerobic capacity), historical (cycling experience: more experience means greater chance of success and better goal definition), lifestyle (time to prepare, physically/mentally demanding career, psychological stress), and mental.
- Author considers mental dedication most important. Five mental predictors (the more present, the greater the chance of success):
  1. An unquenchable curiosity for the sport.
  2. Strong bonds with other cyclists.
  3. The support of family and friends.
  4. Riding is seen as enjoyable and fun, not laborious.
  5. Training is thought of in years, not weeks or months.
- Steve had all five. Athletes with all five very likely perform at a high level (author's coaching experience).
- Athletes focus mostly on physical preparation, but research shows the psychological side is just as important (mental readiness: Chapter 7).
- Takeaways: mental determiners as important as physical; experience matters; lifestyle must allow training without frequent interruptions or undue stress; complete dedication; mindset/self-belief is often the key.

### [HPC-C02-010] Commitment, passion and the hard-easy balance

- Success is about commitment; the bigger the goal, the harder to stay committed. Commitment plus dedication produce passion, which takes time to develop (Steve became passionate after starting and it carried him through setbacks).

### [HPC-C02-011] Training defined broadly: lifestyle plus workouts

Friel uses "training" to mean everything done in preparing to race, not only workouts: riding and lifting plus eating, sleeping, resting, recovering, planning and even thinking. The higher the goal, the more is included. Many athletes treat lifestyle matters as inconsequential compared with riding; Friel calls this too narrow a view to produce a successful athlete. Without the lifestyle factors, fitness does not fully develop even if physiological training is done. Combining on-the-bike fundamentals with lifestyle determiners leads to an exceptional season. He starts with lifestyle matters (consistency, mindset, listening to the body, R&R, sleep, intensity, fuel, plan) and then the physiological fundamentals. Individually these small things are not big, but combined they can make or break an A race. High performance requires doing the demanding, tedious, disliked things others will not do.

### [HPC-C02-012] Consistency is the foundation of high performance

- Consistency means persistent devotion to purpose and training steadily, "showing up for work every day", not missing workouts. It does NOT mean repeating the same workout (that is repetitiveness).
- Occasionally skipping a workout for good reason is fine (even the best do); just don't miss too many. A single missed session is not a big deal; missing several is problematic.
- Fitness changes are slow: a felt improvement takes about six to eight weeks of consistent riding ("weeks, not days"). Losses from missed workouts are also not felt right away, but several missed workouts are noticeable and the gains slide back.
- Claim: a rider would likely perform better doing the wrong workouts consistently than the right workouts inconsistently. Nothing is more important than consistency.
- Example lesson (Dirk): success came from rarely missing a day of riding for years, not exceptional talent or hard workouts; talent built one workout at a time.
- Consistency does not mean pushing beyond limits. Missed-workout handling is covered in Chapter 8.

### [HPC-C02-014] Most riding should be easy: intensity principle

- Most self-coached riders ride too hard; most of the time you should ride slower. Riding slower most of the time makes you faster. Always pushing slightly harder (zone 3 when zone 2 is prescribed) gives mediocre workouts and poor racing.
- Two overarching goals: (1) build aerobic (low-intensity) fitness; (2) increase high-intensity ("anaerobic") fitness. Aerobic endurance must be built before the truly hard work and then constantly maintained.
- Cycling, especially mass-start, is largely aerobic: outcomes are decided by brief high-intensity efforts (sprint, climb, staying off the front, closing gaps), but the aerobic side gets you there and makes up well over 90 percent of the race.
- Self-coached riders spend too much time in the middle of the intensity curve: not fast enough to improve racing, not slow enough to build aerobic fitness; it just feels tiring.
- Most rides should be at the easy end with only a few pushing limits (Chapter 3). Low-intensity = zones 1 and 2. Friel still uses all zones; each has a purpose. Emphasis on easy rides is because self-coached riders underappreciate them.
- Low-intensity rides build the engine; high-intensity rides fine-tune it.

### [HPC-C02-016] Low-intensity riding is not a throwaway; individuality of zones

### [HPC-C02-017] Success = healthy consistency, not epic workouts (Seiler foreword)

- Great endurance coaches know success is not about epic workouts or ever-increasing intensity. Success is achieved when athletes stay healthy and execute most sessions with intent, discipline and quality.
- Seiler: polarized training is a day-to-day management system balancing adaptive signal against systemic stress so training is sustainable. "No pain, no gain" is a catchy slogan but a terrible training philosophy. What is polarized is stress, not heart rate, power or pace.
- Low-intensity long sessions, threshold sessions (about 75 min) and HIIT sessions (about 45 min) all generate molecular adaptation signals (mitochondrial protein synthesis, capillary growth). Threshold and HIIT generate much more systemic stress than well-executed longer low-intensity sessions. The 80 percent stays under the stress radar while inducing lots of adaptive signaling.
- Intensity can only be understood in the context of duration. Threshold (lower intensity, longer accumulated work) and high-intensity (higher intensity, shorter total) sessions are both HARD, need longer recovery, and form the 20 percent of the 80-20 distribution.
- Threshold sessions are not bad, but the most common mistake is letting intensity distribution "regress toward the mean": easy days become too hard, so athletes are not recovered for hard days. Result: too much threshold training, a recipe for stagnation and burnout (elite or recreational).

### [HPC-C02-018] Limiter: definition, examples and how to use it

- A limiter is what stands between you and your goal: your weakness relative to the demands of the race. Fixing (or greatly improving) it is key; otherwise training is largely a waste and results will not improve.
- Examples: course terrain (climbing on hilly course, sprinting on flat course), lacking stamina, too little saddle time, poor aerobic endurance, skill such as descending, insufficient strength, poor heat performance (Ch 9), insufficient fueling (Ch 8), inconsistent training, mindset (minor compared with psychological challenges for many; Ch 7). Option: pick races matching strengths.
- Determining the limiter is the most important decision of the season. Keep it front and center daily. It may be an advanced ability: aerobic capacity, stamina or sprint power. Decide by start of Build (about three months before A race).
- Train the limiter every week of the season; by Peak it should be greatly improved. Maintain strengths when not working the limiter.

### [HPC-C02-020] Why lift weights (muscular force rationale)

- Performance rationale: Research shows weightlifting improves cycling performance by increasing leg power, time-trial power, and sprinting power. The author suspects (though has seen no research) that it also benefits climbing. Several studies have shown improvements in aerobic capacity and stamina from strength training.
- Economy: Strength training improves economy (energy expended at a given power output), meaning it requires less effort and fuel to drive the pedals across a wide range of intensities.
- Health rationale (bone density): Pedaling a bicycle provides no impact stress or bone-loading benefit, regardless of how hard one pushes. Many serious cyclists in their 30s show early stages of osteoporosis; risk increases with age. Weightlifting strengthens bones and prevents fractures. Cross-training endurance sports (running, hiking, walking) also provide impact benefits for bone health.
- Demographic differences in response: Riders over 50 stand to make the greatest improvements from weightlifting; riders in their 20s likely see the smallest performance gains.
- Setting: Health club/gym or home gym (garage, spare room, basement, patio) outfitted gradually over several years (rubberized flooring, free weights, bench, leg-press machine, heavy elastic bands, step stools) to remove travel time and excuses.
- Guiding principle: Keep gym sessions brief and focused—cyclists are not gym-goers or bodybuilders. Leg exercises must be multi-joint extensions (hip, knee, ankle) that simulate pedaling; no single-joint exercises.

### [HPC-C02-021] Eustress, distress and the three situations of distress

- Hans Selye (Hungarian-born Canadian endocrinologist, "father of stress research") introduced stress in a biological setting. He distinguished eustress ("good stress") and distress ("bad stress").
- Today "stress" usually means distress. The author wants the rider to focus on eustress: the beneficial stress of training that makes the body adapt and become stronger.
- The workout does not make you fit; it only creates the potential for fitness. Fitness occurs with rest, above all deep and prolonged sleep.
- Distress occurs in only three situations: (1) a race at full gas; (2) very hard race-like group rides or very high-intensity intervals; (3) field tests of stamina or FTP.
- Each requires a lot of R&R, especially sleep, and usually more than one easy or off day. Such rides must be rare, with broad gaps between them, to avoid injury, a weakened immune system and overtraining. Riders who make them common, thinking it makes them faster or tougher, only become more fatigued.
- Tiredness after a hard workout should be fleeting: gone within two days at most, usually recovered after 24 hours. That is eustress. Frequent exhaustion from pushing beyond limits is distress and sets up overtraining syndrome.
- Key takeaway: beneficial stress comes from widely spacing hard rides and separating them with easy ones, seldom pushing to the limit.

### [HPC-C02-028] Consistency and its dark side (obsession)

- Consistency is defined as not missing workouts, but it goes further. Short-term fitness comes from high intensity; long-lasting fitness comes from consistency. Train consistently and patiently and improvement is certain.
- Dark side: a narrow divide separates consistency from obsession, and the step is small and slow enough to go unnoticed.
- Signs of obsessive behavior: seldom taking a day off; refusing to ride easy when the plan calls for it; frequently pushing limits; denying fatigue when obvious to everyone; putting training above health and life in general.
- Consequences: likely overtraining, possible ill health, burnout, eventual abandonment of the sport. High performance may not result.
- Example: Dave Scott (six-time Ironman World Champion), at 70 facing open heart surgery, said he "exercised like a maniac" and learned "more does not mean better"; he links it to four serious heart conditions. Wes's overtraining (Ch 6) ended his racing career early.
- Exercise is strong medicine: respect it like any drug. Frequency, duration and intensity are the three "pills"; each has a right dosage for you (see Tables 4.1, 4.2, 5.2). Taking more than prescribed may be only a short-term problem, but doing so weekly for months and years will cause health problems.
- Rule: keep training consistent but avoid obsession. It is fine to ride very slowly, miss an occasional workout, and take a day off. Riding should be one part of life, not the reason for life.

## C03 Integral Model

### [HPC-C03-001] Integral Model

## 1. Chain: beliefs → mechanisms → method → decisions

**Beliefs**
- Success is self-referenced, and the athlete is an individual.
- Adaptation takes weeks to months and happens during rest.
- Consistency and patience beat intensity.

**Mechanisms** (see Aerobic capacity (VO2 max): determinants and trainability; Stamina (durability); Sleep and anabolic hormones; Recovery versus adaptation)
- Easy zone 1-2 riding enlarges stroke volume, blood volume, capillary beds, mitochondria, natural EPO and fat use. This is the foundation of aerobic capacity and stamina.
- High-intensity work adds only a few percent of VO2 max ("10 percent would be a lot") and produces much more systemic stress.
- Fatigue is masked by constant training. Only reducing duration (taper) reveals "form".
- Sleep delivers anabolic hormone waves roughly every 90 minutes.
- Lactate is a fuel, not the cause of fatigue or soreness.

**Method**
- Linear periodization: frequency (Preparation), duration (Base), intensity (Build, Peak, Race), then Transition (Table 4.1).
- Intensity distribution is pyramidal in Base and polarized (80-20 by session intent) in Build and Peak. Pyramidal throughout if the event is contested in zone 3 (gran fondo, century).
- Weekly routine is 4+3 in Base and 5+2 in Build and Peak.
- The limiter gets the focus every week; strengths are maintained.

**Decisions:** workout type, duration, intensity, R&R timing, taper, fueling, all adjusted daily by feel, EF, HRV and life stress.

## 2. Variables and priority order
1. Consistency (showing up).
2. Sleep and R&R (adaptation).
3. Frequency, then duration, then intensity (in that seasonal order).
4. Intensity placed only on fresh days.
5. Limiter focus.
6. Nutrition matched to training.
7. Mindset.
8. Gadgets and recovery tools, which are minor.

## 3. How the tools fit together

| Tool | Use |
|---|---|
| Table 3.1 zones (set via MHR, FTHR, LTHR/LTP; talk test confirms) | Prescribe and police intensity. HR for zones 1-2, power for zones 3-5c, RPE always. |
| Efficiency factor (EF) | Daily or weekly on AE1/AE2 rides; compare with a similar ride ~8 weeks earlier. A falling EF over two back-to-back AE rides is an R&R trigger. After illness, EF must return to normal before hard work. |
| Field tests T1-T4 | See the test schedule in Table 5.6. Repeat every 6-8 weeks, after rest. |
| Table 4.1 / Table 4.2 | Period lengths and weekly hours, anchored to Base 3 (up to +10% over past). |
| Table 5.1 | Weightlifting phases: Anatomical Adaptation, Max Transition, Max Strength, Strength Maintenance. |
| Table 5.6 | The season at a glance. |
| Appendix workouts (AE, MF, SS, AC, ST, SP) | The session library for each ability. |

| PMC / TSB | TSB at -30 or below is high risk; needs an accurate FTP. |
| Mindset self-assessment | Find weak skills, then apply tools in B/C races and hard rides. |

## 4. Decision flow for a new athlete
1. Clarify the goal with SMART criteria. Check it is challenging but realistic, that goals don't conflict, and that there is one A race. Set the A/B/C race calendar.
2. Screen lifestyle: sleep (is it under 7 hours?), commitments (family, career, training only), stress, time available, age and experience. Decide the 3-week or 4-week block (50+ or novice = 3; under 50 = 4; novices under 50 get 3 for the first 2-3 years).
3. Physical therapist assessment, then professional bike fit.
4. Equipment: power meter and HR monitor.
5. Set zones (lactate testing is best, else the 20-minute field test plus talk test), then run baseline tests.
6. Identify the limiter from race demands vs weaknesses, and decide it by the start of Build.
7. Choose Base 3 weekly hours from Table 4.2, fill in Table 4.3, and set period lengths (Preparation ≥26 weeks out, Base ≥23 weeks out, Build 8-9 weeks, Peak 10 days-2 weeks, Race 5-7 days).
8. Execute with the weekly routines, EF monitoring and gym phases.
9. Daily: body check, then train, shorten or rest (see listen-to-your-body and readiness rules).
10. Adjust for missed workouts, illness, heat or altitude using the specific rules.
11. Taper, then run race week and race day (plan B, try nothing new).
12. Post-race: celebrate, analyze the next day across seven categories, then take Transition rest.

## 5. Tensions and exceptions the author acknowledges
- Taper loses a little fitness but gains much form, so it is a net gain.
- Zone 3 is avoided yet used for stamina work and for zone-3 events.
- Zones are approximations: they overlap, and the athlete may go slightly above or below.
- Consistency vs listening to the body: skipping is fine, missing many is not.
- Consistency vs obsession.
- Recovery on demand is better in theory but riskier when the athlete is self-coached. When unsure, rest.
- Fast recovery can tempt an extra hard day, so adaptation matters more than feeling fresh.
- FTP is placed inside zone 4 because research on FTP vs lactate markers is contradictory.
- Time-crunched riders (~8 h/week) cannot follow the full plan and should favour short races.
- Heat and altitude preparation are health risks, not just performance issues.
- Quality vs quantity in multiple A races: if you must, allow 12+ weeks.
- Mistakes should be made on the conservative side: too much easy, too much recovery, too much Base.

## C04 Mechanisms

### [HPC-C04-001] Fat vs carbohydrate use and low-intensity riding

Fat is the energy source Friel most wants to harness, especially early in season. Long steady low-intensity rides train the body to use stored fat. Beyond zone 2 muscles shift significantly to carbohydrate as lactate production increases; lactate has been shown to inhibit muscles' use of fat. Low-intensity riding builds capillaries, burns fat and ultimately makes you faster at higher intensities too. Body carbohydrate stores are small while fat supplies hours of fuel regardless of leanness; the goal is that in racing, stamina is fueled by the largest energy source. Excellent stamina means riding more than one hour at relatively high intensity while still using fat.

### [HPC-C04-002] Determinants of VO2 max and how easy riding builds them

1. Stroke volume (blood per beat): low resting HR signals high stroke volume. Bulk of season's improvement comes from zone 1-2 riding; later high-intensity intervals raise it slightly more.
2. Blood volume: top cyclists have about twice the circulating blood of sedentary people of same age and size. Also improves heat dissipation via sweating and quick heart refill. Biggest contributor: low-intensity aerobic riding.
3. Capillary beds in leg muscles: final stop delivering oxygen and nutrients; a few weeks of aerobic exercise significantly increases them, largely from easy rides.
4. Mitochondria: take up fat and carbohydrate and produce energy for contraction; more mitochondria = higher aerobic capacity. Low-intensity exercise builds them; a few weeks of consistent riding gives significant increases.
5. Natural EPO: raises red blood cells (oxygen carriers) over weeks of exercise; low-intensity easy exercise is the intensity range most responsible (contrast with EPO doping).
Bottom line: low-intensity riding is the starting place; high-intensity also helps but most gains come from easy riding. ### [HPC-C04-003] Lactate myth and fatigue causes

- Lactate does not cause muscle fatigue during exercise nor next-day soreness (misconception since the 1920s). Heavy breathing, leg fatigue and next-day feel are unrelated to lactate/"lactic acid".
- Lactate is an efficient fuel, preferred even over glucose by heart and brain; working muscles also use it.
- Causes of fatigue are not precisely known. Big-picture factors: intensity, duration, fitness, heat adaptation, nutritional status, hydration, sleep, mental stress, muscle fiber composition. Cellular possibilities: hydrogen ions, muscle glycogen depletion, central nervous system, sodium-potassium pump, others.

### [HPC-C04-004] How stamina is built: mitochondria and MCT-1

### [HPC-C04-005] Sprint power and muscle fiber types

### [HPC-C04-006] Low- vs high-intensity adaptations (Alan Couzens sidebar)

Low-intensity training (at or below the first rise in lactate) is fueled primarily by fat, can be sustained for long periods, recovers quickly (minimal nervous-system stress). High-intensity training relies heavily on glycogen/carbohydrate, causes quick fatigue, stresses the system significantly. They produce different adaptations; the key question before any session: "What physiological adaptations am I seeking with this training?"

Low-intensity adaptations:
- Increased mitochondrial density, particularly in type 1 (slow twitch) and type 2a fibers; seen as lower lactate at a given power.
- Enhanced cardiac capacity: changes in heart size and structure, increased end-diastolic volume; seen as higher power for a given heart rate, i.e., higher efficiency factor (EF).
- Fat utilization: increased liberation of free fatty acids; allows going further at a given power before glycogen is emptied; aids body composition.

High-intensity adaptations:
- Increased mitochondrial density mainly in type 2x fibers, but limited.
- Improved cardiac output at maximal efforts (ejection fraction).
- Enhanced glycolytic power.
- Acidosis buffering.

Reasoning: fast-twitch fibers produce pyruvate that mitochondria-rich slow-twitch fibers can process, preventing premature acidosis; a developed slow-twitch base enhances high-intensity performance. Oxygen delivery at high intensity depends on stroke volume; the absolute heart size built in the low-intensity Base period matters more than the percentage of capacity used per beat. Fat-burning conserves glycogen for high-intensity work in training and racing; athletes who fail to build fat-burning in Base struggle with time to exhaustion in long races. Low-intensity adaptations keep improving over long periods; high-intensity adaptations plateau quickly. Type 2x fibers can improve fatigue resistance but endurance capacity is limited. Elite cyclists training over 1000 hours or 30,000+ km annually fuel this primarily from fat.

Common mistake: time-crunched cyclists over-rely on high-intensity workouts to substitute for missed low-intensity training. Bottom line: low-intensity training underpins the high-intensity adaptations; reducing overall intensity and prioritizing low-intensity systems prepares for peak performance.

### [HPC-C04-008] Aerobic capacity (VO2 max): determinants and trainability

- Shuttling of oxygen from lungs to muscles; close to half of potential is genetic, the largest portion is training; highly trainable; one of best indicators of race readiness, especially shorter races.
- Women's aerobic capacity is about 10 percent lower than men's on average: VO2 max is relative to body weight and women have more body fat; women also have somewhat less hemoglobin.
- Most improvement occurs in Base (AE1 and AE2 work, plus years of training; early-season gains from bike and cross-training). Build adds only a few percentage points with high-intensity work (10 percent would be a lot); still significant (last 1-2 percent may decide hanging on a climb, closing a gap, riding into crosswind). See Figure 2.1.
- Primary determiner: stroke volume (how much blood the heart pumps per beat), i.e. left ventricle. The heart does not know the activity, hence cross-training in Prep/early Base builds it while giving a break from bike.
- Next determiner: capillary density in working muscles: requires riding (cross-training won't do); built by easy riding in zone 2 in Base.

### [HPC-C04-009] Stamina (durability): definition, matches burned, capillary density

- Stamina = maintaining a high percentage of aerobic capacity for more than an hour (also called durability). Because VO2 max is usually unknown, use current FTP as proxy; requires an accurate FTP (Appendix Field Tests; T1 FTP/FTHR).
- Even more trainable than aerobic capacity. Goal: solid aerobic capacity AND ability to ride at a high percentage of it for over an hour.
- Aerobic capacity does not always decide a race: in very long races a rider with high VO2 max may not hold the wheel of one with lower VO2 max but great stamina (muscle types, mitochondrial density, capillary beds, aerobic enzymes). The longer the race, the more important stamina relative to aerobic capacity.
- Matches burned: a match is a brief, extremely high-power effort almost like a sprint. Each rider has a limited number per day; when gone, stamina is gone. Do not waste matches (big accelerations without clear advantage), especially early. A dropped rider returns to the group gradually without hard accelerations to save matches.
- Matches matter less in races of one hour or shorter, where aerobic capacity matters more; burning still an issue for poorly conditioned riders. Highest match consumption in short races: newcomers excited about the race, and highly fit experienced riders; only the latter benefit.
- Ability to burn many matches requires high capillary density (repeatedly call on energy and remove waste products that inhibit contraction). Capillary beds are developed by lots of riding just below 2 mmol/L lactate (LT1, Table 3.1), i.e. easy zones 1 and 2. Self-coached riders who push this aside for higher intensities are called intensity addicts.

### [HPC-C04-010] Sleep and anabolic hormones; why alarm clocks and lifestyle stress matter

- Sleep is when fitness happens. During sleep the body releases anabolic (tissue-building) hormones, such as growth hormone, insulin-like growth factor, androstenedione and testosterone, responsible for growth and repair of tissues.
- They are released in waves of roughly 90 minutes through the night. Cutting sleep short with an alarm clock may miss one or more waves, so the benefits of the previous day's workout are not fully reaped. The author is not a fan of alarm clocks; if you must be up early, go to bed earlier. If you cannot, you have too much going on.
- Lifestyle stress (hard day at work, pressing problem, inadequate sleep, argument, sore throat) reduces training capacity just as workouts do. Honor that fatigue; do not train through it, as nothing is gained.
- Coaching stance: more commitments means less sleep and lower workout quality. For a very challenging goal the author allows an athlete only three responsibilities: family, career and training. Athletes unwilling to accept this are not coached. Thriving on constant stress is unhealthy and reduces performance, health and lifespan.

### [HPC-C04-011] Recovery versus adaptation; the three key modalities

- Between hard rides in Build 5+2 there are usually two or three easy days; you should feel fresh by the third or fourth day.
- Recovery (feeling refreshed) is not adaptation (becoming fitter). Feeling ready while not yet adapted can lead to questionable decisions such as a third hard ride, which is how overtraining starts (Wes). Adaptation is far more important than recovery; recovery is a stage on the way to it. Hence the polarized 5+2 routine.
- Adaptation needs three modalities: sleeping, eating, rehydrating, in order of importance (sleep best). Other methods have minor or no physiological impact; benefits are mostly psychological, which is still valuable. Speeding recovery is fine so long as you do not add another race-like ride.

### [HPC-C04-012] Challenge and threat appraisal and self-efficacy (Rob Griffiths)

Before any task we want to do well in (race, hard session) we unconsciously appraise its demands (distance, intensity, competition) against perceived resources (fitness, skills, experience): "Can I handle this situation?"
- CHALLENGE state (resources judged sufficient): increased cardiac output, reduced total vascular resistance, release of adrenaline, noradrenaline, neuropeptide Y. Nerves are interpreted as excitement; supports performance.
- THREAT state (resources judged insufficient): raises cortisol, smaller increase in cardiac output, no change or increase in total peripheral vascular resistance. Emotions feel debilitating: unusually weak, distracted, demotivated, wanting to avoid the competition.
- Self-efficacy (self-confidence, self-belief) is a precursor to a challenge state (Marc Jones and colleagues). Bandura: self-efficacy is "how well one can execute courses of action required to deal with prospective situations"; belief in ability to succeed in any situation.
Bandura's four sources of self-efficacy, most influential first:
1. Mastery experiences/performance accomplishments (succeeding at a new challenge).
2. Vicarious learning (seeing others complete a similar task).
3. Social persuasion (positive verbal feedback during a complex task).
4. Emotional, physical, psychological wellbeing (feeling low, injured or ill lowers confidence).
Vealey (sport-specific) added four sources: successful preparation (mental and physical); physical self-perception (body weight, image); environmental comfort (familiar venue, equipment, routines and rituals); leadership (trust in coach or team manager's decisions).
Context: Bradley Wiggins has described imposter syndrome (BBC 2023): fear of being exposed as not good enough, fear of failure, guilt about success. Mark Cavendish quote: pros train legs but few train the mind, the only muscle used to make race decisions.
Author credential: Rob Griffiths, 30+ years with endurance athletes, MSc performance psychology.

### [HPC-C04-013] Fat and carbohydrate stores; crossover point (Figure 8.1)

- A fit, well-nourished athlete stores approximately 2,000 calories of carbohydrate; even the skinniest rider stores about 53,000 calories of fat.
- Zone 1 and 2 training taps fat, conserves carbohydrate for intense efforts, and is metabolically healthy.
- Figure 8.1: as intensity rises from zone 1 to zone 5, fat use falls (high to low) and carbohydrate use rises (low to high). The crossover is the intensity where the athlete goes from primarily fat to carbohydrate-dominant fueling. Slopes vary by individual but are highly trainable; the most metabolically fit have a crossover far to the right.
- Carbohydrate-dependent athletes show a gradual rise in heart rate at steady power; they waste the fuel they have least of and lack carbs for high-intensity efforts late in the race. A very high-carb diet tends to create a carb-burner with a low-intensity crossover.

### [HPC-C04-014] Bodyweight and performance: VO2max per kg

VO2max is oxygen delivery to working muscles per minute divided by bodyweight (kg). Example: 70 kg (154 lb) rider loses 2 kg (4.4 lb); oxygen delivery 4.2 L/min unchanged; aerobic capacity rises from 60 to 61.8 (about 3% for about 3% bodyweight loss). Rider would ride faster "maybe". Benefit mainly when climbing; no benefit on flat roads or all-out flat time trials, and possible disadvantage from muscle loss. Losing muscle when calories are inadequate: body takes calories first from stored fat, then muscle protein.

### [HPC-C05-054] Overreaching / Overtraining

### [HPC-C05-055] Overreaching, overtraining, under-recovering

- Overreaching: a normal result of focused training over several days or a couple of weeks, marked by tiredness. It must be accepted and relieved sooner rather than later.
- Overtraining: the product of distress, commonly tied to excessive workouts (too often, too long, too intense, or all three). It is serious, not merely being tired.
- Under-recovering: the other side of the same coin. A reduced capacity to work out can come from under-recovery rather than too much training, because both share two sources: workouts and lifestyle. Usually it is a mix: a little overtraining and a lot of under-recovering.

### [HPC-C05-058] Periods: Preparation, Base, Build, Peak, Race, Transition

Preparation (prep) period: training period with very "general" workouts (not the same as the demands of the targeted event). Purpose: return gradually to a structured training program following a break from focused training during the preceding Transition period.
Base period: workouts are "general," not exactly like the demands of the targeted event. Purpose: prepare the body for the training stresses of the Build period.
Build period: workouts are "specific," very much like the demands of the targeted event. Purpose: prepare the body for the training stresses of racing.
Peak period: workouts are "specific," and workout durations are decreased while intensity remains high. Typically follows the Build period and precedes the Race period. Purpose: produce form gradually by allowing the body to recover from the previous period of hard training while steadily becoming race-ready.
Race period: workouts are "specific," durations are very brief while intensity remains high. Typically follows the Peak period and culminates with the targeted race. Purpose: recover completely from the previous periods of hard training and become race-ready.
Transition period: workouts are quite easy, allowing full recovery, both physical and mental, in the days immediately following a targeted race. Purpose: recover completely from the stresses of recent training and racing.

## C06 Quantitative Reference

### [HPC-C06-001] VO2 max reference values and age decline

- Decline with age: serious riders typically drop around 7 to 8 percent per decade; sedentary people about twice that per decade. Also declines with reduced training load or added body weight.
- Males typically higher than females, all else equal. Elite male riders generally in the 70s; elite females in the 60s (ml/kg/min).
- Highest ever measured: male 97.5 (Norwegian cyclist Oskar Svendsen); female 78.6 (American runner Joan Benoit).
- Wearables (watch, ring, strap) give only rough estimates that could be way off, but may show trend. Lab testing is not necessary but can be an eye-opener and motivating. A field test for aerobic capacity power comes in later chapters (does not give VO2 max number).
- Resting HR: very fit riders typically 40s and 50s; untrained 60s, 70s, 80s (bpm).

### [HPC-C06-002] Stamina benchmark: 75% of FTP for equivalent minutes

Pro breakaway riders can typically hold at least 75 percent of FTP for an equivalent number of minutes. Example: FTP 400 W → 300 W for 300 minutes (five hours). Self-check: multiply FTP by 0.75 and hold that power for that many minutes. Example: FTP 300 W → 225 W for 225 minutes (3 h 45 min) is a challenging goal; achieving it is the starting point for boosting stamina and the threshold of high-performance cycling. Measurement and improvement in Chapter 3. If two riders have similar aerobic capacities, the one with more stamina (and motivation) will probably win.

### [HPC-C06-009] Macronutrient split and fueling targets

- Season-average diet, very roughly: 50 percent carbohydrate, 25 percent fat, 25 percent protein (fewer carb calories, more fat and protein than typical Western diet). Some endurance athletes go down to 40 percent carbohydrate and still race at a high level via nutrition periodization.
- Water only for rides shorter than 90 minutes to two hours. Long easy ride: water only for the first two hours. Intense ride: carbohydrate starting at about 90 minutes.
- Carbohydrate intake on long races: long-standing upper limit 60 g (240 calories) per hour (about one large bottle); after the first 90 minutes, a large bottle of sports drink with 240 calories every hour. Some pros take 90 g (360 cal) or up to 120 g (480 cal) per hour by gut training; author does not recommend it (nausea).
- Dehydration over a two-hour ride reduces cardiac output, and thereby aerobic capacity, by up to 8 percent.

## C07 Testing & Assessment

### [HPC-C07-001] Intensity measurement basics and required tools (power meter and heart rate monitor)

- Only two variables are measured in a ride: duration (watch) and intensity (more complex).
- The single most important marker of intensity is how hard the ride feels: rating of perceived exertion (RPE). Key: relate any measured intensity to how you feel at that moment (detail in Chapter 3).
- Author has required clients to have both a heart rate monitor and a power meter for over 20 years. Reasons: both give unbiased data and different information; comparing power and heart rate measures aerobic efficiency, an indicator of improving or fading fitness; shows how you respond to training and what to work on (Chapters 3 and 5).
- You can train without them but the quality of work is lower and you miss the finer points.
- Cost argument: author guarantees the combination makes you faster than new wheels; look for used power meters via local shops; heart rate monitors are comparatively inexpensive.
- Technology argument: compared to Luddites; derailleurs were allowed in the Tour de France 34 years after invention.
- Use both every ride. Novices should focus on workout frequency rather than heart rate or watts; this book assumes you are beyond that stage.

### [HPC-C07-002] Setting HR zones: three methods (MHR, FTHR, LTHR)

1. Maximal HR (MHR): only option not threshold-based. If unknown, do NOT go bust a gut to find it; instead search the past year of diary/online data for the highest HR (likely in a race, hard group ride or interval session); assume it is MHR until exceeded. Multiply MHR by the column percentages for each zone (write them on the table). Zone 5c has no MHR value.
2. Functional threshold HR (FTHR): use if no MHR or it seems too low. From the 20-minute field test (Appendix T1): average HR of the 20 min minus 5 percent = FTHR. Apply column percentages. The FTHR single threshold lies between zones 4 and 5a.
3. Lactate HR (LTHR): from finger-prick lactate samples. LT1 = HR at 2 mmol/L lactate (border of zones 2 and 3). LT2 = HR at 4 mmol/L (border of zones 3 and 4). Follow column 8 to set zones. Gold standard; can buy gear and self-test or pay a university, health club or coach.
Thresholds exist because reference points are needed since riders have unique HR and power. The functional threshold concept came from Andrew Coggan PhD and Hunter Allen (Training and Racing with a Power Meter) as a field alternative to lab tests.
The three methods will probably give different results, sometimes considerably; precision favors lactate testing, but repeated testing costs money (start of season and at intervals).

### [HPC-C07-003] Talk test (counting) to confirm HR zones

Supported by research (see References) as a rather accurate estimator of ventilatory thresholds. Procedure: after warm-up, ride with slowly increasing intensity over a few minutes.
- At VT1 (like LT1, border of zones 2/3): without taking a breath you should be able to count "One thousand one ... one thousand six." If you can reach one thousand seven, you have probably not reached VT1 (threshold HR is a bit higher). If you cannot reach one thousand six, threshold HR is lower.
- At VT2 (border of zones 3/4): you should only be able to say "one thousand one." If you can say more, VT2 is probably a bit higher.
Choose the HR method (max or functional) whose zones come closest to your talk-test thresholds. Less accurate than lactate testing but free. Also helps to adjust zones and confirm MHR-based zones.

### [HPC-C07-004] Setting power zones: FTP and lactate power (LTP)

- FTP: most common worldwide; 20-minute field test (Appendix T1) sets both HR and power zones. Free, easily repeated, but hard. Learning curve: first-timers start too high and fade, giving poor data. Start conservatively, easier than you think you can hold 20 min, and every five minutes decide whether to ride slightly harder or easier. Accuracy improves with repetition.
- Test every six to eight weeks because power changes through the season; HR changes much less (desirable: power rises, HR stays stable).
- FTP is not placed on a zone border but inside zone 4 because research comparing FTP to maximum lactate steady state, critical power, ventilatory threshold and lactate threshold has been contradictory.
- FTP test variables affecting results: bike, course, weather, wind, temperature, warm-up, tire pressure, motivation, traffic.
- Lactate power (LTP): more accurate than FTP test if tester is skilled; best on an indoor trainer with your own bike (not a standard indoor cycling machine); control heat with a couple of fans; graded test (effort increased slightly every two or three minutes until failure); have an experienced tester (university lab, health club, local coach) so you can concentrate on riding; repeat every six to eight weeks.
- Percentages are estimates; no perfect system, lactate testing comes closest.
- Recommendation if not doing lactate testing: the functional threshold test, confirmed with the counting talk test.

### [HPC-C07-007] T3 Stamina Test (summary)

Hard effort for 90 minutes, best in the latter part of a workout (e.g., after one hour of aerobic endurance riding). Measure as percentage of FTP or LTP (whichever sets your power zones); in TrainingPeaks this is intensity factor (IF), shown on the post-workout analysis page. First test at end of Base to set a standard; repeat every few weeks in Build when rested. Stamina matters for staying away from a chasing field, closing gaps, holding position, long climbs.

### [HPC-C07-008] T4 Sprint Power Test (summary) and sprint technique

Measures top-end power over a five-second jump and a 20-second sprint; expect improvement in both by the last few weeks before the A-priority race (Appendix T4). Every rider needs a good sprint (finish, close gaps, jump out of corners, hold position). Positions to master in Base: most powerful sprints are standing with butt over the saddle, hands in the drops, elbows bent (lowers torso, reduces drag), head up and eyes forward for safety and maneuvering. Cadence: pure sprinters' cadence probably well over 100 RPM; time trialists and climbers may find slightly higher gears and lower cadences more effective. Aerodynamics and group-sprint skill cannot be measured, only power.

### [HPC-C07-012] T1 Functional Threshold Power (FTP) and Heart Rate (FTHR) test

- Purpose: determine FTP and FTHR (as in Chapter 3) and set power and HR training zones. Needs a power meter for FTP and a heart rate monitor for FTHR.
- Conditions:
  - Do it after a few days of R&R, and warm up well (see the Appendix intro: 20 to 30 minutes for high-intensity efforts).
  - Road mostly flat to slightly uphill (3 percent grade or less), with a wide bike lane, light traffic, no stop signs, few intersections or corners. It must be safe for high-speed testing.
  - You need roughly 5 to 10 miles (8 to 16 kilometers) of road, depending on speed and gradient.
  - May also be done on an indoor trainer.
  - Use the same course or indoor trainer every time, and the same bike you train on.
  - Keep your head up throughout.
- Protocol:
  - Treat it as a 20-minute time trial.
  - Hold back slightly for the first 5 minutes (most riders start too fast).
  - Every 5 minutes, decide whether to ride slightly harder or easier for the next 5 minutes.
- Calculation:
  - FTHR estimate = average HR of the 20-minute portion minus 5 percent.
  - FTP estimate = average power (not normalized power) of the 20-minute portion minus 5 percent.
  - Then use Table 3.1 to compute HR and power zones.

### [HPC-C07-013] T2 Functional Aerobic Capacity test (VO2max power)

- Purpose: determine Functional Aerobic Capacity (VO2 max) power. A power meter is required.
- Conditions:
  - Best after 3 to 5 days of R&R.
  - Flat or slightly uphill course (3 percent grade or less), wide bike lane, light traffic, no stop streets, few intersections or corners. Most of all it should be safe.
  - Use the same course every time.
  - Look straight ahead throughout; do not ride looking down at the power meter.
  - Best done on the road, not on an indoor trainer.
- Protocol: after the warm-up, ride steadily at an effort you can sustain for 5 minutes.
- Result: average power for the 5-minute portion is a good predictor of power at aerobic capacity. You want a steady increase over time.
- Caution: some tests may show no improvement or a loss of power. That is expected given the many variables on test day, most importantly your readiness to test at a very high intensity.
- Context: best young riders typically have a VO2 max in the 70s (men) and 60s (women) or higher, but knowing the number is not necessary for training.

### [HPC-C07-014] T3 Stamina test

- Purpose: feedback on stamina, the ability to hold a relatively high power for a relatively long time.
- Conditions:
  - A long test needing a significant stretch of mostly flat road, with a wide bike lane, light traffic, no stop streets, few intersections or corners.
  - Option: a repeatable loop taking 15 to 30 minutes per lap, with several laps to reach the test duration.
  - Precede the test with a long warm-up, such as an AE1 and AE2 combined workout.
- Protocol and goal:
  - 90-minute ride at a goal Intensity Factor (IF) of 88 to 92 percent.
  - IF = normalized power for the test ride divided by FTP (from T1).
  - If you can ride at a higher IF for that duration, you have excellent stamina. That is the ultimate goal.
- Recovery: the test is quite stressful, usually requiring at least two days of recovery before the next hard ride.

### [HPC-C07-015] T4 Sprint Power test

- Purpose: gauge sprint power progress and prepare to sprint in various race situations. A power meter is required.
- Conditions:
  - After 3 to 5 days of R&R and a thorough warm-up.
  - A road section roughly 50 to 200 yards/meters long, with light traffic, a wide bike lane, no intersections, no stop streets.
  - Not on an indoor trainer.
- Protocol:
  - From a rolling start, pedal as powerfully and quickly as you can.
  - Do two sprints: one lasting 5 seconds and one lasting 20 seconds.
- Result: peak and average power for the two durations indicate short- and long-duration sprint power. Keep a record of your best sprint powers for both.
- Interpretation: at which duration are you better? Does one need improving? Improvement results from increased training.

## C08 Methodology & Progression

### [HPC-C08-001] A/B/C race priorities and season race-count limits

- Limit A-priority races to no more than three per year; two is better; one is great if it is late-season. Multiple A races should be separated by several weeks (e.g., early spring, midsummer, late fall is probably fine).
- Reason: each A race gets a 2-3 week taper that cuts volume (fewer hours per week, intensity kept high). By the reversibility principle, reducing training load makes fitness begin to decline. Closely spaced tapers risk losing too much fitness and a poor follow-up race.
- A races: taper 2-3 weeks prior (details in Chapters 4 and 5).
- B races: reduce workout volume for only 3 days prior, mostly rest/recovery; about 6 per year.
- C races: no taper or rest; done as a workout.
- Steve's single fall A race made for a highly focused season.

### [HPC-C08-003] Basic vs advanced abilities: role in the season

- Basic abilities (aerobic endurance, muscular force, speed skills): focus of Preparation and Base periods; maintained in Build, Peak and Race periods. They make up the bulk of training all season and general fitness.
- Advanced abilities (aerobic capacity, stamina, sprint power): focus of Build, Peak and Race periods ("icing on the cake that gets you ready to race"), while basics are maintained.
- Aerobic endurance (low intensity, zones 1–2): single most important early-season workout type; starting point for most abilities; lots of time early, maintenance later.
- Muscular force: strength to drive pedals with more force; weight room, mainly Base, maintenance in Build (details Ch 5).
- Speed skills: drills for pedaling, descending, climbing, sprinting, cornering; improves economy (less wasted energy, e.g., novices pedaling in squares); Base-period focus.
- Stamina training progression: long easy endurance rides early; near end of Base, once endurance is established, intensity of long rides is gradually increased; by peak period stamina must be high.
- Sprint progression: muscular force (weights) early, then aerodynamic/bike-handling skills in Base, then group sprint work.
- HIIT is not begun until the Build period (last couple of months of race preparation); LIT in Build is maintenance.

### [HPC-C08-004] Polarized vs pyramidal training and when to use each

- Polarized: about 80 percent of sessions easy, 20 percent hard (Seiler, early 2000s, observed in elite endurance athletes, later found effective at all levels). Avoid the middle, threshold zones 3 and 4 ("somewhat hard" and "hard"); easy workouts truly easy, hard truly hard.
- Classification is by session intent/type, not measured zone time. A hard interval workout is "hard" even though warm-up and recoveries pass through low and moderate heart rates. Polarized refers to distribution of sessions (very hard or easy), not of intensities within a workout.
- Pyramidal: three broad workout types (easy, moderate, hard), using all intensities; intensity distributed by time spent in training zones, not workout purpose.
- Friel's use: pyramidal in early season (Base) while building general/aerobic fitness, then moderate zone 3-4 rides after the aerobic system is developed (many middle-zone rides for stamina). Polarized from Build (and Peak): easy or hard with little moderate.
- Exception: athletes performing on event day in moderate intensity (gran fondo, century) stay pyramidal all season. Road cyclists, mountain bikers, gravel racers and others whose outcomes depend on very high intensities train polarized in Build.

### [HPC-C08-005] General vs specific training

- General training (Preparation, Base periods, and Transition): workouts unlike the race: low intensity, weightlifting, cross-training, riding slow and easy, improving weak skills, many solo workouts.
- Specific training (Build, Peak, Race): workouts increasingly mimic the event: high intensity, limiter work, race-specific terrain, race-like conditions, group rides, refining strategy and tactics.
- Transition is general and is the physical/mental shift between seasons (off season).
- Purpose of the concept: know what the training purpose is at each time.

### [HPC-C08-006] Three-stage seasonal progression: frequency, duration, intensity

Order matters so the body adapts gradually. Skipping early stages (jumping into high intensity and hard group rides months before the A race) precludes fully absorbing aerobic benefits; high performance is the accumulation of many components, not only high intensity. No shortcuts; leaving out the basics leads to disappointment.

Stage 1 Preparing to train (frequency): raise volume without long rides by increasing workout count: two short rides daily, or (preferred) one ride plus one cross-training session daily. Rides about half typical summer duration or less (e.g., commuting). Cross-training: running, hiking, rowing, Nordic skiing, snowshoeing, stair climber, brisk walking. Walking with weighted backpack of 10 percent of bodyweight or less for those with aerobic capacity greater than about 50 (likely under age 50); older/lower-capacity riders just walk briskly. No heavy breathing. Reasons for cross-training: winter weather; avoid boredom/burnout over a long season; variety aids motivation. Indoor trainer/virtual apps: do not compete with others; stay in zones 1 and 2. All workouts in HR zones 1-2; for cross-training use the talk test (Ch 3), staying just a few heartbeats below the lower threshold at the upper end of zone 2. Also begin muscular force work (weights, elastic bands, bodyweight).

Stage 2 Training to train (duration): keep daily/weekly volume about the same as stage 1 but shift to doing it all riding; progress from short two-a-days to one longer ride. After three or four weeks cross-training phased out (continue if weather poor, or ride indoors). Pyramidal: some zone 3 and occasional zone 4 rides (early stamina work). Stamina tested by end of stage to guide next stage. In first few weeks strength peaks (low resistance/high reps progressing to high resistance/low reps), then strength maintenance.

Stage 3 Preparing to race (intensity): race-like workouts introduced while volume cut slightly to free energy. Hard workouts devoted to aerobic capacity, stamina and sprint power, prioritized by race type, goal, limiter. Taper at end: reduce durations further, intensity race-like. Polarized: key workouts high-intensity or low-intensity (maintain aerobic endurance and speed skills). Events in lower zones (gran fondo, century): pyramidal with greater stamina emphasis. Strength training only maintenance.
- Stage 3 is much shorter than stage 2; stage 2 can be extended several weeks, and should be if starting preparation more than 48 weeks before race day. Extending stage 3 causes loss of fitness (body handles this stress only a few weeks). If in error, err toward too much duration stage and too little intensity stage. Do not extend stage 3 beyond eight to 11 weeks of race-like workouts.

### [HPC-C08-008] Plan flexibility and individual response time

- The plan is a strong suggestion to be constantly rethought. Not deviating when change is needed is the biggest reason periodization fails. On days when unmotivated, tired, sore or near burnout, make a significant change (how: Ch 6).
- The body cannot be forced into fitness. Individuals respond at different speeds (quick, moderate, slow; appears built into DNA, lifestyle may affect). Know your response to high volume, long durations and high intensity, and design the plan to your likely response time. Forcing an overly quick peak leads to frustration and a poor race.
- Other causes of failure: aiming too high, overtraining, injury, low motivation, inconsistent training, poor diet, inadequate sleep. Self-assess what typically gets in the way.

### [HPC-C08-009] Goal, A/B/C races and number of A races

- Goal should be SMART (Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Timed). Write it where seen daily (diary cover, stem tape).
- Strongly recommended: only one A race per season. Each A race requires a taper (Peak) that loses fitness; with multiple A races there is often not enough time to regain it.

- B races: important but no peak; cut back training workload for three days prior; perhaps three or four per season.
- C races: no taper or peak; like group rides, treated as hard workouts.

### [HPC-C08-012] Base period: start, purpose, weekly templates Base 1 and Base 2-3

### [HPC-C08-013] Build period: content, length and weekly template

- Workouts become specific: expected intensities and duration, plus terrain, weather, group dynamics, strategy, tactics, refueling, equipment (Ch 8, 9). Stay focused on limiter. Polarized.
- Length matters: no more than eight or nine weeks, with frequent R&R breaks (Ch 6). More is not better.
- Stage race: Saturday and Sunday rides could both be races, or one race and one aerobic capacity/stamina/sprint power ride. If both weekend days are hard, the following Tuesday is an easy AE1 ride (zone 1).
- Standard 5+2 week (Build 1 and 2): two hard rides, four easy, a day off bike, one gym session:
  - Gym: one session (Strength Maintenance).
  - Limiter: one hard ride on limiter (Aerobic Capacity, Stamina or Sprint Power) or group ride focused on limiter.
  - Strengths: one hard ride on strengths, or B or C race, or group ride.
  - Long AE: one long easy ride.
  - Short AE: three short easy rides.
  - Off: one day off from riding.

### [HPC-C08-014] Peak period: taper, fitness-fatigue-form and weekly template

- Durations reduced (taper); ride frequency stays as in Build; hard-day intensity stays race-like.
- Fitness = physical race readiness. Fatigue lingers in the background through weeks of training, never fully cleared by brief breaks; athletes become insensitive to it. Tapering volume removes most fatigue, producing "form" (freshness, absence of fatigue). Reduced load loses a tiny amount of fitness, more than made up by the gain in form. You cannot improve fitness by training less.
- Weekly 5+2 in Peak (two race-like rides, four easy, a day off bike, gym):
  - Gym: one session (Strength Maintenance).
  - AE and Sprint Power: one AE session finishing with a few sprints.
  - Limiter: two race-like group rides (or B or C races); or Aerobic Capacity intervals, a Stamina session, or Sprint Power repeats, whichever is a limiter.
  - AE: three AE rides.
  - Off: one day off the bike.
  - Taper volume: Peak week 1 reduce by 30 percent, Peak week 2 by 40 percent of Base 3 weekly volume (see Table 4.2).

### [HPC-C08-015] Race period (race week) and Transition period

Race week (event Saturday or Sunday): workouts short, half or less of longest Build rides; emphasis on R&R and freshness; brief workouts with intermittent high-intensity intervals and repeats for aerobic capacity and sprint power maintenance; very race-specific; avoid Stamina workouts (too fatiguing); no weightlifting this week (first time all season). If taking another day off, make it two days before race day.
  - Gym: none.
  - Hard ride: one short race-like workout early or midweek (group ride, Aerobic Capacity or Sprint Power intervals).
  - AE: four short AE rides including sprints near the end.
  - Taper volume: reduce weekly volume by 50 percent of Base 3 volume (see Table 4.2).
  - Race; Off: Monday.

Transition (off season): starts immediately after an A race; could be one week if another A race is planned, four weeks or more after the last race. Purpose: physical and mental rest, regain health, lifestyle normalcy, relationships. Fitness loss is good: you cannot be race-ready always (leads to sickness, injury, burnout, overtraining). Stress is like a bank account: Transition makes deposits. Don't work out unless you can't stand it; then brief and embarrassingly slow and easy; best not to ride the bike (cross-training okay). No standard weekly plan.

### [HPC-C08-016] Rules for setting weekly volume

- Highest-volume weeks are Base 3 (and Base 4 for older riders, Ch 6); these are the reference; all other periods have fewer hours. Base 2 fewer than Base 3; Build 1 and 2 (and Build 3 for some) about the same hours as each other; Peak and Race least.
- Why: when weekly intensity rises duration must drop; high intensity and high duration together could be a disaster.
- Choose the Base 3 figure realistically; may add up to 10 percent more weekly hours than previously done, but that is the limit. Better too few hours with energy to spare than pushing beyond limits (fatigue, falling fitness, health risk).
- Base 4 and Build 3 are typically used by riders over 50, slow recoverers and new cyclists (Ch 6).
- Daily workout durations must add up to the weekly volume in Table 4.2; the author leaves daily durations to the rider. Period length in weeks is completed after Ch 6.

### [HPC-C08-018] Seasonal progression transitions: frequency, duration, intensity

Seasonal order every season: workout frequency first, then duration, then race-like intensity in the last weeks before the A race. The progressions must BLEND gradually, not be inserted suddenly. Prep and early Base: smoothly mix aerobic endurance time on the bike and cross-training with gym workouts while improving bike-handling skills. Build: advanced-ability workouts are gradually blended in as basic abilities go to maintenance mode.

Transition 1 - FREQUENCY (Preparation and early Base):
- Key factors: cross-training and frequency. Cross-training options (must be aerobic): running, hiking, walking, rowing, Nordic skiing, snowshoeing, others.
- Frequency = two somewhat brief workouts daily: two cross-training sessions, or one cross-training + one cycling. Two days per week one of the two-a-day workouts is a gym session.
- Brief = each workout less than half of the single daily bike workout duration at the height of seasonal volume. Example: average daily ride in Base 3 is 2 hours, so two-a-days in Prep/early Base are each less than 1 hour. Lots of latitude on exact length.
- It is an in-between period: more about staying aerobically active than building great fitness, but early aerobic endurance is developed, leading to higher aerobic capacity.
- In Base 1 begin replacing cross-training sessions with increased time on the bike.

Transition 2 - DURATION:
- Begins in Base 2, no less than eight weeks before starting Build. Focus shifts from frequency to duration: move from cross-training to only riding, by gradually replacing cross-training with riding; may take a week or two. Winter weather may mean cross-training more and riding only when weather permits.
- In Base 3, single daily rides should be about as long as, or longer than, the combined times of the earlier cross-training days (Prep and Base 1). Weekly long-ride durations are roughly based on expected DURATION (not distance) of the A race and history of typical training loads. Cut back to one gym session a week.
- Group rides only if aerobic. Most groups, even if billed as easy, are unsanctioned races; avoid in Base. They do not help race speed later; choosing fun over Base fitness leaves you lacking aerobic fitness entering Build.

Transition 3 - INTENSITY:
- Begins moving up to Build, 11 or 12 weeks before the A race (Table 4.1). Ride durations become a bit shorter than late Base as energy shifts from long rides to race-like intensities.
- Now is the time for groups that cannot limit intensity below race-like; such rides may become a central focus of weekly workouts.
- Include some C races and occasional B races. Go easy on B races: training load must be reduced for three days before a race, which costs saddle time/fitness. Best time for a B race is the end of an R&R week (Chapter 6). This implies two or three B races at most in Build and Peak. They serve as tests of the season so far.

### [HPC-C08-021] Gym session structure, routine, and scheduling rules

Gym session structure:
- Exercise stations can be performed in whatever order is practical, but two leg weightlifting exercises should be separated by a few minutes, during which planks and mobility exercises are completed.
- Warm-up must begin with Cossack squats.
- Suggested routine: Warm-up (Cossack squats) -> Weightlifting (leg exercise 1) -> Core strength -> Weightlifting (leg exercise 2) -> Hip mobility -> Repeat.
- Do not lift to excess or cause fatigue that compromises upcoming cycling or cross-training sessions.

Scheduling rules:
- In Preparation and Base 1: two gym sessions weekly, separated by two to three days (e.g., Monday and Thursday). Scheduling sessions only one day apart increases injury risk and diminishes strength adaptations.
- In Base 2, Base 3, Build, and Peak: reduce weightlifting to one Strength Maintenance (SM) session weekly.
- In Base 1: begin gradually phasing out cross-training to increase time on the bike.

### [HPC-C08-022] Speed skills training: when and how

Speed skills (pedaling, sprinting, cornering, group riding, descending); detailed workouts are in Appendix, Speed Skills Workouts. Riders in first three years of racing have most room to improve; even top pros work on weak skills.
- Purpose: conserve energy and increase speed; poor skills slow you and raise energy cost. Better skills make riding safer (descending, cornering, tight fast groups are risky).
- If you are skilled and another rider in a group lacks skills, keep some distance.
- Focus in the Base period: work on sprinting, cornering and descending every week, built into daily workouts whatever the ride's purpose. Best done at the start of a ride when fresh. Each Base day pick one skill from the Appendix that fits the workout. Be aware of handling; refining movement often involves relaxing. Eventually able to ride skillfully when tired. In risky situations (high-speed descent) stay focused and within skill limits.

### [HPC-C08-024] Building and training stamina across Base and Build

- Base: increasingly long workouts in zones 1 and 2. Late Base and Build: add zone 3 stamina workouts. Field-test stamina at end of Base (T3 Stamina Test; see Appendix Field Tests). If you meet the excellent standards, stamina is a strength, not a limiter: maintenance only in Build.
- If still a limiter in Build: stamina rides such as St3 Cruise Intervals (Appendix, Stamina Workouts), fast group rides, B and C races.
- Best time for a Build stamina ride: end of a workout that began with another purpose (aerobic capacity or long aerobic endurance ride), simulating late-race demands and showing how sustainable power is when fatigued. Also: sit in on group ride or C race, then late move to the front or break away, riding hard within stamina limits (rehearsal for A race).
- Use late-stamina sessions to work out fuel and fluid needs; refuel and hydrate or be significantly slowed.
- Monitor: when heart rate rises rapidly while power is steady, you approach your stamina limit. Causes: need for fuel or hydration, general training fatigue, or current stamina status. Note it for comparison with similar rides; consider more endurance training.
- Combined workouts (aerobic capacity + stamina, then sprint or series of sprints) closely simulate a race; making workouts like portions of the race is the fundamental purpose of Build. Ride group workouts with a purpose; usually the purpose is your limiter.

### [HPC-C08-025] Sprint power training in Build

- If sprint power is the limiter, focus on it throughout Build. Races shorter than an hour: combine aerobic capacity and sprint power work in the two weekly hard rides. A race longer than an hour: combine stamina and sprint power. Make combination similar to the anticipated race.
- Prerequisites by Build: gym strength, refined sprint technique, big-gear muscular force sessions (Appendix, Muscular Force Workouts), aerobic endurance incl. long easy rides.
- If sprint power is the primary limiter: two workouts weekly that include sprint training along with aerobic capacity, stamina or aerobic endurance. Practice sprints in varied situations: uphill, downhill, straightaways, out of corners, alone, with other riders (latter especially late in Build).
- If you are not a pure sprinter (better at climbing or time trialing) and weak at short accelerations (gap opens, coming out of a corner): include random sprints in workouts and especially group rides; also do the Base period preliminary sprint power work (weightlifting, skill development, muscular force on bike).

### [HPC-C08-026] Key takeaways: advanced abilities

- High-intensity training in Base harms race preparation; save for Build.
- Aerobic capacity is mostly a product of Base; Build increase is small but necessary.
- Cross-training in Prep and Base 1 boosts aerobic capacity by enlarging the left ventricle.
- Carbohydrate stores and hydration status affect aerobic capacity on race day.
- Frequent heat training has been shown to increase aerobic capacity.
- Stamina = riding over one hour at a high percentage of FTP.
- High aerobic capacity plus great stamina define the best endurance cyclists.
- Limit matches burned in races.
- Best time to train stamina: last hour and longer of a Build workout at high percentage of FTP.
- HR rising late in a stamina workout with constant power may signal need for more endurance training.
- Build hard rides should imitate the upcoming race.
- Combine sprint power after aerobic capacity or stamina work.
- Base sprint power work = weightlifting, skills, muscular force on the bike.

### [HPC-C08-027] Peak and Race periods: taper rules

Peak (relatively short; Table 4.1): gradually reduce workout durations to taper volume and improve form, still including race-intensity workouts on the limiter, but shorter than in Build. With a 10-day to two-week taper: reduce durations by 10 to 20 percent each day compared with similar Build workouts; by end of week two durations about 50 percent of Build. Expect growing impatience/readiness (coming into form); keep easy workout intensities under control.
Race week: if all went well you feel fresh, fit, eager. Several race-like rides focused on aerobic capacity and/or sprint power (per limiter), but quite short: less than half of Build durations for similar rides. Warm up, a few high-intensity efforts, cool down. Avoid using up stamina until race day (long efforts, much fatigue). A week without stamina training is no problem if aerobic endurance is maintained. Basic abilities remain strong, no maintenance needed, if refreshed weekly in Build and Peak.

### [HPC-C08-031] R&R breaks: when needed, duration and block length

- R&R breaks shed accumulated fatigue; without them the rider becomes a "zombie". Typically three to five days of greatly reduced workout durations and intensities. Young/high-capacity athletes may need only three or four days; older riders longer. Age is only a quick, inaccurate proxy (some 30s need long breaks; some 50+ get by on short ones).
- Preparation and Base 1: need for R&R is low or none (training is very easy except weightlifting). If you cannot get through them without an R&R break you are training much too hard.
- Base 2, 3 and Build: R&R imperative as volume (late Base) and intensity (Build) rise.
- Block lengths: a young rider trains in four-week blocks (three weeks moderate to high load, then a few days of R&R followed by testing). An older rider may do two weeks of high load then R&R and testing.
- Two methods: planned recovery and recovery on demand.

### [HPC-C08-032] Planned recovery: rule by age and experience

- Schedule R&R weeks in the calendar during Base 2, Base 3 and Build. Weakness: fatigue may accumulate well before the planned week; even so, overtraining risk is greatly diminished since reduced work follows soon.
- Frequency depends on how quickly you recover. If fatigue is common and hard to shed: R&R every third week (two weeks high load then rest, recovery and testing). If fatigue-resistant (or load reduced): R&R after three weeks of training. Self-coached riders often misjudge borderline fatigue, which a coach, spouse or training partners may see.
- Simple rule used by the author with new athletes: riders 50 and older take a break every third week; riders under 50 every fourth week. Exception: inexperienced riders under 50 recover every third week for the first two or three years in the sport.
- Three-week blocks change the period count: four Base periods of three weeks each (Base 4 added) and three Build periods of three weeks each (Build 3 added). Older/slow-recovering riders do one more week in Build (nine weeks instead of eight). Base remains 12 weeks for both.

### [HPC-C08-033] Hard and easy days; 5+2 vs 4+3 weekly routine

- Easy days are in zones 1 or 2; hard days in zones 3, 4, 5 (5a, 5b hard; 5c max effort for very short periods). Easy days must be easy so hard days can truly be hard. (See Table 3.1.)
- 5+2: five easy days (including a day off the bike) and two hard ride days; strongly suggested in Build and Peak. Ensures you enter each hard day rested. 4+3 has four easy and three hard days, so two of three hard rides come ~48 hours after a hard ride and you are a bit tired. Two fresh hard rides beat one fresh and two tired. Training fatigued is wasted time or worse.
- 5+2 riders train more regularly than 4+3 riders in Build: fatigue accumulates with three hard rides weekly, and 4+3 riders often skip a day. Consistency is key.
- 4+3 is used in Base because the three hardest days are long rides and moderately high intensity, not high intensity.
- Easy days are somewhat boring: ride steady and easy, preferably flat, zones 1-2; this is where most aerobic fitness is built.
- Typical hard rides: steady stamina rides, aerobic capacity intervals, sprint power repeats, fast group rides, B and C races (see Appendix).
- Day off the bike is good for mental health and gives life beyond the saddle. The Monday strength maintenance (SM) gym session is very brief and can be moved (e.g., Thursday post-ride) for a fully off day.

### [HPC-C08-035] Heat adaptation training protocol

- Only way to prepare for a hot race: train in the heat. Acclimatization takes 10 to 14 days of near-continuous exposure. Core temperature needs to rise on most rides over a 14-day period; sweat massively and keep a high sweat rate 60 to 90 minutes on most days for up to two weeks. Best time: the two Peak-period weeks.
- Workouts must be AE1, truly easy and "very slow" (heart rate rises in heat); no moderate speeds, no zone 2 or higher. Adaptation depends more on duration than intensity.
- Two hard days weekly in Peak (Table 5.5) are done in a cooler part of the day, not hot. Over 14 days that gives eight heat rides; goal is minimum 10, so add two hot rides at end of Build/start of Race period, or one just before and one just after Peak.
- Option 1 (moderately warm climate): ride easy AE1 in the hottest part of the day, 95 to 100 F (35 to 37 C) for an hour or more over two weeks.
- Option 2 (not hot enough): layers (cycling raincoat, or winter kit: long sleeves, jacket, leg covers, booties, full-finger gloves). Use caution: initially no longer than 1 hour, at most 90 minutes in week 2; increase duration in small steps; AE1 only, no exceptions; hard rides done without extra clothing.
- Option 3 (less beneficial): sauna or steam room 1 hour daily for 10 to 14 days, before or after rides; avoid air conditioning in car, workplace and home to boost effect.
- Option 4: simulate hot environment: indoor trainer plus space heater in a small room (laundry room, walk-in closet) pre-heated to 95 to 100 F (35 to 37 C), AE1 ride about an hour; or a tent tall enough to sit on bike and trainer (basement, garage, patio). If not hot enough add clothing. Expect heavy sweating.
- Hard rides may be followed by a "cool down" in the hot environment (on bike or sauna) limited to 30 minutes or less.
- Training in heat also boosts aerobic capacity, stamina and economy by increasing blood volume (see Ch 5).

### [HPC-C08-036] High-altitude race preparation

- Best preparation: exposure to altitude; preparation depends mostly on total daily time at altitude, not workouts there. For common race altitudes of 6,500 to 8,200 ft (2,000 to 2,500 m) may need 3 to 4 weeks living, possibly training, there. Adaptation over about 3 to 4 weeks "live high" lets you breathe easier and lowers altitude-sickness risk; you still won't be as fast as at sea level (same for everyone).
- "Live high, train low" is the standard: workouts at altitude are always low intensity and fitness may be lost. Example: Flagstaff runners live at 7,500 ft (2,280 m), drive 45 min to intervals at 4,300 ft (1,300 m) in Sedona, getting ~22 h/day at altitude.
- Why intensity is low: lower air pressure makes it hard to take in oxygen and deliver it; you breathe deeper and faster; thinner atmosphere at higher elevation.
- At camp: first few days keep intensity low, zones 1 and 2 only; sleep may be difficult, afternoon naps help; excessive training/intensity can block adaptation. After 3 to 5 days increase duration and intensity; everything stays slower. If you can't descend for intervals, make hard portions shorter and recoveries longer.
- Altitude tents: legal per WADA but banned in some countries (Norway, Italy); need most hours daily for at least 3 weeks; 8 to 10 h sleeping in tent not enough; less effective than a camp.
- Living at 5,500 to 6,500 ft (1,600 to 2,000 m) and racing below 7,500 ft (2,300 m) usually needs little adaptation. Sea-level riders at 7,500 ft can be disastrous to performance and health; must adapt.
- If no 3-week camp (sea-level resident) arrive: race at 1,600 to 6,500 ft (500 to 2,000 m): 3 to 5 days before; 6,500 to 9,800 ft (2,000 to 3,000 m): 1 to 2 weeks before; above 9,800 ft (3,000 m): at least 2 weeks before. These are minimal.
- If can't adapt: most athletes feel worst 1 to 3 days after arrival (typical range 24 to 72 h). Arrive 4 or more days early, or no more than 24 hours before start (night before or just before start, leaving time to settle and warm up).

### [HPC-C08-037] Race-week form and training

- Form = absence of fatigue plus physical and mental readiness to race (Ch 5). With a proper taper you feel fresh and itching to race; it may be hard to hold back on easy days. Rare (a couple of times a season); do not waste it on long, high-intensity group rides. Save it for race day.
- Include some short high-intensity race-like efforts in Race week to maintain fitness (Table 5.6). Doing only easy rides with no intensity can also ruin race day (form lost). Blend short, hard and easy rides; do not make high-intensity rides long. Endurance is established and won't fade unless you stray.

### [HPC-C09-001] Handling early-season setbacks and preparing for a goal race

- Setbacks (head cold, crash, missed workouts from family matters) are normal; when many weeks from the goal race they are not major obstacles. Become more cautious with such matters as the goal race approaches.

### [HPC-C09-002] Listen to your body: back off rather than push through

Many motivated athletes ignore body signals during workouts. Friel's rules:
- If a knee feels tender, back is tight, or a cold may be coming on, do not press on with the planned workout. Continuing hill repeats with a tender knee is a good way to get injured; continuing a hard workout when a cold may be coming on is a big mistake.
- When the body says something is wrong: back off the intensity, shorten, or abandon the ride. Replacing a hard workout with an easy one or a day off is smarter than missing several days due to breakdown.
- The closer to the race and the more race fit, the greater the risk of breakdown (body pushed to limits for weeks/months), so the more critical this advice.
- A good coach asks how you felt during hard workouts, intervals, climbs, group rides and races; if no coach, ask yourself.
Rationale: injury or illness means missed workouts, which undermines consistency.

### [HPC-C09-003] Why zones, and how to treat their imprecision

- Heart rate and power are stochastic (change constantly with terrain, surface, wind, perception, focus), so a single value cannot be held; ranges are needed.
- Zone percentages are estimates based on what works for most riders and zones overlap; outliers exist (e.g., some athletes found the top of zone 1 too hard for recovery). Adjust slightly to fit you.
- Daily adjustment: if leg-weary, ride the lower part of the called-for zone; if feeling good, the higher end. Zones vary day to day with freshness and fatigue.
- Common self-coached mistake: doing most rides in the middle of the range, zone 3. Zone 3 (if zones are correct) is too hard for aerobic benefits of LIT and does little for aerobic capacity. Save zone 3 for stamina focus, and for the final weeks before an event conducted largely in zone 3 (gran fondo, century ride). Otherwise avoid it, especially when concentrating on aerobic endurance. (Also noted: an exception in the Base period.)
- Use HR or power method choice: decide one method for zones after reviewing options.

### [HPC-C09-005] Choosing the Build-period limiter and allocating time

- Build has four goals: increase aerobic capacity, improve stamina, boost sprint power, and MAINTAIN aerobic endurance, muscular force and speed skills (use it or lose it; maintenance takes much less time than development).
- In Base the three basic abilities were nearly equal; in Build prioritize the advanced abilities: which is strongest, which weakest, compared with race demands.
- Examples: hilly course decided by several-minute high-intensity climbs and this is a weakness: limiter = aerobic capacity. Flat or long gradual climbs, long breakaways, long time trial or off-road race and weakness: limiter = stamina. Aerobic capacity and stamina good but race likely a sprint and sprint weak: limiter = sprint power. Limiter may be a combination.
- The more important an ability is to goal success and the more it is a limiter, the more Build time it gets. This is the most difficult planning decision of the season.
- Strengths are treated as basic abilities: maintain for the whole Build with much less time; maintain within a limiter ride (e.g. stamina limiter: include some sprints or aerobic capacity intervals). Once-weekly focus on a strength is usually enough; twice is possible on hard-ride days.

### [HPC-C09-006] Recognizing overtraining / under-recovering: symptoms and response

- No sure biological indicator exists and no doctor's test can confirm overtraining. Lyme disease and chronic fatigue syndrome have nearly the same symptoms, so get checked by a doctor when symptoms occur (chronic fatigue syndrome is also hard to diagnose).
- Symptoms (a few, not all; the first two are most common): unrelenting fatigue; reduced performance in training; moody, grumpy, emotional; depression; high perceived exertion for a common power output; low motivation; colds, flu, allergies; no improvement with a short recovery; low heart rate variability; elevated resting heart rate; unusual changes in appetite; restless sleep, insomnia; menstrual cycle dysfunction.
- Fatigue is the single best indicator. Fatigue after very long or intense workouts (heavy legs, low motivation for the rest of the day) is expected. If it lingers despite backing off for a few days, especially with more riding than usual or infrequent recovery, overtraining is quite likely.
- It sneaks up: a long streak of hard workouts with limited R&R. The only cure is a prolonged break from training; length depends on how advanced it is (maybe a week off and symptoms persist; quick if caught early). A couple of days of rest is not enough; expect weeks.
- Prevention: train conservatively, emphasize regular R&R, use the 5+2 routine once high intensity starts. If you err, err toward too little hard riding and a little too much easy riding.
- Wes case lesson: an eight-week plan of up to 24 hours per week with no rest or easy days, including hard group rides on Tue/Thu/Sat, a Saturday 5 km race, track intervals and high-intensity swims, led to severe overtraining. Three weeks off were needed before he slept normally, then a slow return with frequent R&R weeks and far fewer hard days separated by easy ones. Weeks of chronic fatigue affected the entire season and his later career.

### [HPC-C09-007] Undertraining / detraining effects and cross-training

- Reducing weekly workouts from six to three rides for two to four weeks ("detraining") may cost about 10 percent of fitness at most.
- Changes: lower left ventricle stroke volume with higher heart rate, less dense muscle capillary beds, reduced blood volume, more carbohydrate and less fat used as fuel.
- Beyond four weeks, further performance loss, largely from decreased aerobic capacity and lowered lactate thresholds (LT1 and LT2, Table 3.1), though still fitter than a sedentary person.
- When off the bike temporarily (e.g., foul weather), any aerobic cross-training (running, hiking, walking, Nordic skiing, snowshoeing, rowing) keeps losses small. Hence cross-training is suggested in Preparation and Base 1. Illness is covered in Chapter 8.

### [HPC-C09-008] Recovery on demand: triggers to take an R&R break

Recovery on demand ("periodization on demand"): take R&R when fatigue warrants, then test and resume; periods become fluid and tailored. Requires excellent self-assessment; a coach may do better. Indicators that you need R&R:
- Tired and leg-weary on the second recovery day after a hard workout.
- Efficiency factor (EF) down for two back-to-back daily aerobic endurance workouts.
- Unmotivated to ride two days in a row and must force yourself out the door.
- Trained steadily three weeks or more: R&R regardless of how good you feel (objective).
- HRV outside your normal range two days in a row.
- Unsure whether recovery is needed: take R&R (objective).
Most indicators are subjective; many riders wait until they are nearly ill. If you mistime, err on the side of too much recovery; resting unneeded is preferable to the opposite. In late Base or Build, three consecutive serious weeks means a break regardless of feel.

### [HPC-C09-009] Return-to-training readiness checklist after R&R

- My HRV is in the normal baseline range.
- My waking heart rate is in my normal range.
- I have no muscle soreness or fatigue-like feelings.
- My appetite is normal.
- I feel happy, not moody.
- I am healthy.
- My lifestyle stress is low.
- I was not traveling yesterday.

### [HPC-C09-011] How to lose weight: rules and causes of weight gain

- Calories in vs out; calories out alone usually inadequate; most riders must restrict calories in, with continuous self-monitoring.
- Greatest contributor to gain: ultraprocessed foods (central grocery aisles, hard to resist). Start by eating only natural foods.
- Mental distress: stress raises cortisol, which triggers ghrelin (hunger), leading to ultraprocessed eating. Losing weight under overwhelming stress is next to impossible; simplify life, say "no" more.
- Do not add more high-intensity workouts in place of easy rides: upsets the 5+2 ratio and won't dent weight. Do not greatly restrict calories or fast frequently.
- Timing: slight calorie reduction for a few weeks early in season (Transition, Preparation, Base 1): 8-12 weeks for a small amount of excess fat. Loss >5% bodyweight: devote most of the season; better spread over two seasons (assume done at start of Base 2, maintain, restart at season end).
- Hold muscle: increase protein, ride easily, lift weights especially legs.
- Daily weight varies by ~1 kg or a couple of pounds, mostly water; each gram of glycogen stored holds about 3 g water. If weight only rises over several days, adjust diet/lifestyle.

### [HPC-C09-012] Missed workouts: decision rules by amount missed

General: everyone misses some; consistency is the best predictor of performance (Ch 2). Do not cram make-up workouts.
- 3 or fewer missed: return as if nothing happened; do not make up.
- 4-7 missed, due to illness: body chemistry changed; may show as high HR or high RPE at low power. Treat as more than seven days. Following week or longer: zones 1-2 only, no higher intensities. Monitor EF on these rides (probably lower). Only when EF is back to what it was for AE1 and AE2 rides before illness, resume normal training; ease back into two hard rides weekly conservatively.
- 4-7 missed, not illness, ready to start: consider lost time a rest week; A-race peak synchronization is thrown off. Option 1 (Base/Build): shorten current 3-4 week period by one week; if still unsynchronized do the same to next period. Option 2: reduce Peak from two weeks to one. Both yield less fitness. Then make up two or three key workouts most important given limiters; reschedule, pushing others ahead; something eventually must give.
- Up to 2 weeks missed due to illness: if in Base 3 or either Build period, restart with Base 3; if in Base 2, go back to Base 1 or even Preparation training. Stay until HR and RPE match power as before (EF normal vs last time in that period); if in doubt add a few easy days. Then repeat last week of hard training before interruption; if it goes well, move forward; if not, repeat it. Leave out 1-3+ weeks of planned training (e.g., couple weeks of Build 2 or first week of Peak). Priority: make sure Base fitness is back before pressing into Build.
- More than 2 weeks: if in Build, return to the last Base period completed and start over; if in Base, go back one 3-4 week period. Omission priority: first one or two weeks of Peak, portions of Build 2, then Build 1, in that order.
- Late season (last week of Build 2 or Peak): continue as if nothing happened; if illness, be conservative, zones 1-2 until EF normal. If EF not normal quickly and still run down/weak, consider skipping the A race; if racing, race conservatively as a hard workout and avoid pushing to limits (risk of relapse).
- EF in easy rides is the key readiness indicator after missed training.

### [HPC-C09-014] Race strategy, tactics and Plan B

- Define strategy by starting from the desired outcome (matching your goal) and working backwards from finish to start.
- Team race: discuss expected events and tactics with teammates in the last days; primary team goal is to assist the leader; know your responsibilities; your role must not conflict with your personal goal; know your strengths/weaknesses vs course and competition.
- Have Plan B (maybe C) agreed before race day (e.g., loss of key teammate, missed breakaway); a last-minute Plan B during the race is next to impossible.
- Before race day (several weeks before) research past editions: dominant teams, sprint or not, winners, how they won, weather effects.
- Individual events (TT, MTB, CX, gravel, gran fondo, cyclosportive): decide outcome (win, podium, finish with leaders, time range, just finish); review tactics for terrain (climbs, descents, corners, wind, conditions) and competition strengths/weaknesses; be realistic.
- Mass-start reading: observe other riders, especially in the second half: breathing (labored or light), pedaling (circles or squares), drinking/eating, fuel and hydration state, who wastes energy, fatigue on climbs and out of corners; decide who looks weak or strong; anticipate what smart riders will do.
- Time trial: use a rehearsed pacing plan (HR, power); first minutes feel unbelievably powerful but fade; going out too hard leads to fading; hold back at start regardless of feel, even with new fitness; remind yourself during warm-up.
- Cyclocross/MTB: no time for numbers; pace by perceived exertion; early sensations are misleading; a skill from experience.

### [HPC-C09-015] Post-race protocol and analysis

- Immediately: greet/praise teammates, then congratulate or console main competition; never be a poor loser. Complaints about illegal/questionable acts: calm, concise, to proper officials; otherwise be kind to officials and competitors.
- Share initial thoughts with team: what went well, obvious correctable mistakes, ask opinions; learn something from every race and hard group ride.
- Cool-down: optional. Roots in the old belief that lactate causes exhaustion and soreness and must be cleared; no sport scientist believes this now. Friel: unlikely to harm; may help relax now, may do nothing for tomorrow.
- Celebrate (meal or brief meeting) with the team; find positives no matter the outcome; save deep analysis.
- In-depth analysis the next day, never on race day (emotions, confusion); avoid changes right away; be reluctant to draw immediate conclusions. Seven categories for good or poor performance: knowledge of racecourse and conditions, race plan, physical readiness, mental readiness, strategy and tactics, nutrition and hydration, equipment. Key question: why did the race turn out as it did? Same process for individual events.

### [HPC-C09-016] Training balance: easy most of the time, hard only occasionally

- Most common problem in determined athletes: overtrain and under-rest (workouts too hard, rest too infrequent), usually focused on intensity. Result: frequent fatigue and possibly a short cycling career. Quoting Socrates: "All things in moderation" – avoid extremes, choose the mean.
- Go easy most of the time, hard only occasionally. Easy rides give R&R while building aerobic fitness and make hard rides truly hard and beneficial.
- Easy = zones 1 and 2; most training should be there. A small share of training prepares for race speed. Speed is easier to develop than aerobic endurance; two or three fast rides a week suffice.
- Base period: three weekly moderate-intensity rides. Build and Peak: only two weekly high-intensity hard rides (see Table 3.1). This brings you fresh to hard days and gives adequate adaptation time afterward.
- Riding in zones 3 and 4 when the workout calls for zones 1 and 2 does not make you faster; it makes you tired, so on hard days you lack the legs and power numbers fall short (though it feels hard).
- Pros do not go moderately hard on easy days; they ride faster than amateurs at the same low RPE. Hold back; do not half-wheel training partners or try to beat riders on the road. Riding more slowly on most days makes you ultimately faster.

### [HPC-C10-003] Bike fit (Paraic McGlynn sidebar)

- Bike fit customizes the bike to the rider's physical traits for comfort, longevity, performance; done on own bike or adjustable fit bike. Session 90 to 180 minutes; bring bike, clothing, shoes, proprietary parts, helmet/glasses for tri/TT. Good fitters are booked weeks ahead. Saddles are the most replaced item.
- Process: interview (lifestyle, goals, injury history, discomfort); foot evaluation (size, width, volume, arch length/height, forefoot orientation → shoes, footbed, pedals, cleats); muscle, skeletal symmetry and core stability assessment (e.g., hamstring flexibility limits saddle height; referral to medical professional in rare cases); on-bike warm-up then static (goniometer, plumb bob, laser) or dynamic (2D/3D video with joint markers) angle measurement; saddle pressure mapping (saddle sores, low back pain, groin discomfort); handlebar width/shape by shoulder width and riding type; shifters positioned so wrist nerves avoid pain/numbness.
- Saddle height: ideal knee angle 30 to 40 degrees at leg extension; poor flexibility → near 40 degrees (leg less extended); excellent flexibility → near 30 degrees (more extended).
- Outcomes: recorded fit dimensions; posture/off-bike recommendations (exercises, stretches, PT); bike recommendations.
- Choose an experienced, thorough, well-reviewed, certified fitter who assesses both sides of the body while riding.

### [HPC-C10-004] Heart rate vs power: when to use each; efficiency factor

- Zones 1 and 2 (aerobic endurance): use heart rate — measures physiological input, internal aerobic progress. Much of training is here.
- Zones 3, 4, 5a, 5b, 5c: power preferred — measures output/performance, tied to advanced abilities.
- Use both at once on low-intensity rides (Aerobic Endurance 1 and 2) to track efficiency factor.
- EF = power (normalized power in TrainingPeaks) divided by heart rate for a workout or portion = watts per heartbeat (output/input). Want EF to rise over time (typically six weeks or more) for similar aerobic rides; compare with similar rides from eight weeks before. Key in Base period. EF is not useful for high-intensity workouts (zones 3–5c); use only for strictly aerobic rides below LT1.

### [HPC-C10-006] Heat training to raise aerobic capacity

- Evidence: training in the heat increased aerobic capacity in well-trained cyclists by 5 to 8 percent when done persistently for several weeks; benefits similar to altitude training. Small number of studies: riders 30 to 60 minutes in 90 to 100 Fahrenheit (32 to 38 Celsius), indoors on trainers.
- Options: finish ride in hottest time of day two times per week for eight weeks before A race (if temperatures allow), then once weekly for a few weeks for maintenance; or finish by riding indoors in a warm room on a trainer in zone 1 without fans or cooling; portable heater if cool.
- All hot rides in zone 1; going harder adds no benefit and risks heat exhaustion or worse. Stay well hydrated.
- Do hot rides only after low-intensity rides (AE1, AE2), not after hard workouts (author had heat exhaustion and an emergency room visit). See Chapter 9 for heat adaptation.

### [HPC-C10-007] Nutrition and hydration for recovery and adaptation

### [HPC-C10-009] Confidence tools: supporters, Success Savings Account, Act As If

### [HPC-C10-012] Self-talk tools: positive talk, affirmations, critical questions

- Self-talk influences endurance performance. Always talk positively; never self-flagellate; accept failure while staying positive; believe unconditionally in yourself and decisions; execute as planned; accept results gracefully. Identify when confidence usually breaks down (day before, morning of, start line, a gap opening) and prepare to interrupt negative thoughts (also a time to make a Success Savings Account withdrawal).
- Research: positive self-talk reduces perceived exertion at high power output. Facial expressions (big smile when tough, tongue out) reduce feeling of effort. Key words: relax, focus, rhythm, calm.
- Affirmations: more personal and detailed than a key word (e.g. "Pedal smooth and steady"). Make them stronger in second person as an outside observer: "Tom, keep your pedaling smooth and steady." Examples: "Throw your knees over the bars, Elizabeth" (smooth pedaling); "Relax your toes, Bill"; "Kate, you've done this before, you can do it again, stay focused and relaxed"; "Close the gap just like you've done before, Steve"; instead of "My legs hurt": "Mary, your legs are doing the work they need to do so hang in there"; "Jane, this is exactly how it's supposed to feel this far into a fast race"; "Alex, this is tough, but you've done it before." Keep effective affirmations in a separate savings account. Practice on group/hard rides.
- Critical questions at your limit: ask "Can I do this?" If yes, ask "How?" (the key to that day's performance). Happens in a split second because prepared in hard rides and low-priority races. If the answer is "No," you have found a weakness to address in training.

### [HPC-C10-015] Workout fuel: sports products only on the bike after 90 min to 2 h

### [HPC-C10-017] Supplements: the good, the bad, the ugly (Louise Burke sidebar)

### [HPC-C10-018] Snacks and the four-point weight-loss bottom line

Snacks: too little intake after a ride likely reduces resting metabolic rate. Avoid sugary snacks. Best: protein-based with some fat (satiating): unsalted nuts, hard-boiled eggs, unsweetened Greek yogurt, Cheddar cheese, cottage cheese.

2. Increase aerobic endurance training volume by 10 percent in Preparation and Base 1 (about eight weeks).
3. Eat more protein in meals and snacks (e.g., a cup of nuts on hand).
4. Shift mindset: food as fuel for the tank, not enjoyment.

### [HPC-C10-019] Science of the warm-up (Trevor Connor)

- Reasons for warming up: raise muscle temperature; believed injury prevention; VO2 priming (aerobic system revved up); post-activation potentiation (PAP: muscles need a few explosive efforts to fully activate; critical for track starts).
- Research: the traditional warm-up accomplishes all four but often does not help performance. VO2 priming lasts only ~10 minutes and has no performance effect; PAP is questioned; benefits are more anaerobic and help only the first few minutes of an event that starts hard. If an event is long or starts easy, warming up hardly matters.
- Side effects of a traditional warm-up: raised lactate, lower pH, other fatigue markers; these can outweigh benefits. The traditional 45-minute warm-up with several ramp-ups and 6 to 8 sprints is too long and too hard.
- Structured warm-up only for events explosive or hard from the start (criteriums, time trials, track): 15 to 20 minutes moderate riding, then three or four 6- to 10-second sprints.
- Three-hour road race: rolling around the parking lot is enough; use the time to assess other riders. Experienced riders on trainers with a concentrated look probably plan to blow up the race at the gun.
- Timing: fatigue markers (lactate, pH) take 30 min to recover; PAP and muscle temperature benefits last 20 min; VO2 priming ~10 min. Best balance: finish the last effort 10 to 12 minutes before event start.
- Avoid fatiguing yourself with too much intensity. Individuals differ: start with a shorter warm-up and experiment.
- Trainer gives control and focus, especially on unknown roads; for crit or time trial, warming up on the course is best.
- Hot/cold weather: its fatigue/damage can outweigh warm-up benefits. In cold, heated clothing on core and legs after the warm-up helps; sometimes skipping the warm-up and staying in a warm car or air-conditioned room is best.

### [HPC-C10-022] Hot-race hydration, fueling and cooling strategies

- Drink cold liquids throughout hot training rides: avoids breakdown and keeps blood from thickening (concentrated blood lowers aerobic capacity and power, raises heat-exhaustion risk).
- Fueling in heat: carbohydrate need rises but the body processes consumed carbs poorly in heat, relying heavily on muscle glycogen; lightheadedness late in a hot ride may be glycogen depletion (bonking). With adaptation the body handles fluid carbohydrate better. Start hot rides after a high-carbohydrate meal.
- Race-day cooling: avoid sun, stay shaded; before start use ice vest or frozen jersey, cold drinks to thirst, fan during warm-up. During race pour cold water on skin (more effective than drinking cold water; do both), place small ice packs at base of back of neck/upper back. Aim to lower core temperature about one degree.
- Rising core temperature is the basic problem: a rise of only about one degree above normal can cause a catastrophic problem; in unadapted riders extreme fatigue may occur in the first 20 to 30 minutes; positive thinking or sodium-laced drink will not fix it.

### [HPC-C10-023] The day before the race checklist

- Most important: check the course (part on bike, rest by car): hill grades and lengths, corners especially series of tight turns, off-camber turns, roundabouts, street furniture, road surface/terrain.
- Check weather: wind speed, direction, gusts, temperature, precipitation. Note distance to start and plan travel to the start; arrive earlier than necessary. Even if you've done the race, recheck the course for changes.
- Team meeting: course, weather, strong teams, fastest/smartest riders, team plan; understand your role.

- Evening: lay out race kit; check bike (loose connections, tire wear, chain lubrication, shifting smoothness); bed at normal time considering time zone and sleep conditions.

## C11 Recovery, Monitoring & Lifestyle

### [HPC-C11-001] R&R days are not optional

- R&R days are as important as any long or hard ride and are a requirement of high-performance training.
- Not every ride can be all-out; the body must unload accumulated stress, otherwise overreaching and possibly overtraining follow (Chapter 6).

Apps such as TrainingPeaks that estimate fitness will show a fitness "loss" after even one day off, but this is only an algorithmic calculation from a couple of markers, not a measure of actual physiological loss. Even if accurate, workouts must periodically be reduced for a few days, or you dig a hole that only an extremely long break fixes.

### [HPC-C11-002] Sleep: minimum 7 hours, 8 better

- Sleep is when fitness happens: workouts only provide the potential for gains. Training = quality workouts + deep sleep. Without sleep fitness (and health) go backward. Second in importance only to consistency.
- Target: average at least seven hours of actual sleep (not time in bed) every night; eight is better. Weekend catch-up does not compensate; the average must exceed seven hours every night.
- Why: during sleep anabolic hormones (growth hormone, testosterone, estrogen, insulin-like growth factor, etc.) are released in waves through the night; they improve muscle strength, repair cells, increase bone density, clean the nervous system, increase heart stroke volume, improve brain adaptation and clarity, and improve immunity. Too little sleep misses some waves. Short sleep is like not doing the entire workout; some of the ride is wasted.
- Friel asks new athletes their typical sleep; if under seven hours he investigates why. For athletes short on sleep: in the months before a key race keep only three time-consuming things: family, career, training. More than three usually cuts sleep (later bedtime, alarm wake-up).
- If riding is not going well, check sleep first. Do not artificially shorten sleep with an alarm clock.

### [HPC-C11-003] Training data analysis (Dirk Friel): time, NP, TSS, HR, decoupling, EF

Sidebar by Dirk Friel (former pro road cyclist and coach; co-founder of Peakware Holdings and TrainingPeaks).
- Franz Stampfl (coach of Roger Bannister): Training is an act of faith. Indicators now exist for feedback, but faith remains; measuring the right data at the right times makes training more predictable. Data is pointless without context (goal date, event limiters, prescribed training). Most valuable channels: time, power, heart rate and how they relate.
- Time: daily and weekly hours; more relevant than distance for adaptations.
- Normalized Power (NP): better expresses physiological demand than average power when a ride is stochastic/variable (criterium, MTB race with many 0 W coasting periods that drag average down while HR and lactate stay high). NP is the intensity the body would feel if the ride were at constant power. In a steady-state time trial, average power is very close or equal to NP (variability index, also in TrainingPeaks).
- Training Stress Score (TSS): from NP and duration, assigns a workload value to a session. Needs the athlete's threshold so Intensity Factor (IF) can be calculated; IF is a percentage of threshold, combined with duration to give TSS.
- Average heart rate: indicator of aerobic fitness, more valuable as average HR within intervals. Caution: intervals shorter than about five minutes are too short for HR to respond to the work performed.
- Decoupling (Pw:HR): as fitness improves you see more output (watts, speed) for same input (HR). Related to heart rate drift. Decoupling reflects the change over time between HR and power within a session or interval; aim is to lower the value as aerobic fitness improves. Greater value = larger spread between HR and power in the segment.
- Efficiency Factor (EF): another view of the HR-power relationship, like miles per gallon. EF = normalized power of a steady-state segment / average heart rate of the same segment. Unlike decoupling, EF does not measure change over time.

### [HPC-C11-004] Heart rate variability (HRV) interpretation (Friel)

- HRV is a measure of the variation in time between heartbeats; discovered in the 19th century, accurately measured from the 1960s; use in sport is still being learned.
- Low HRV (beats closer to even spacing) relative to your normal baseline: sympathetic ("fight or flight") dominance; possibly distress from lifestyle, accumulated fatigue or early illness, perhaps unnoticed. Physiological capacity to respond to training is low; forcing hard riding is mostly a waste or worse; good day for a recovery ride or day off.
- High HRV (beats vary widely), above baseline: parasympathetic ("rest and digest") prevailing; calm, perhaps too much so to train well.
- Measure every morning soon after waking with a phone app. HRV is a good but imperfect, objective indicator; consider it along with the other factors; it is a strong suggestion only.

### [HPC-C11-005] Using HRV to monitor stress: Marco Altini protocol

- HRV quantifies variation between beats; measured under proper conditions it reflects the autonomic nervous system's response to stress. A decline typically indicates reduced parasympathetic activity, i.e. incomplete recovery or heightened stress.
- Use accurate sensors: chest straps (e.g., Polar H10), optical sensors (e.g., Scosche Rhythm24), validated apps (e.g., HRV4Training).
- Assess underlying stress (acute: hard workouts, travel; chronic: work) by measuring far from stressors, in a resting state, after several hours; do not monitor continuously.
- Use a morning spot check or full-night measurement. Late stressors (alcohol, late food, evening exercise) affect night data more, so morning is better then. Morning allows orthostatic measurement (sitting or standing), more sensitive to stress; morning shows readiness for the day, night shows the prior evening's behavior.
- Morning protocol: after waking, measure with phone camera, chest strap or reliable optical sensor for about one minute; limit movement, breathe naturally, avoid yawning and swallowing; use the bathroom first; recommended sitting, especially with a low heart rate or for endurance athletes.
- Stay consistent in protocol. Interpret only against your own historical data; there is no universal reference and your normal differs from a friend's.

### [HPC-C11-006] Recovery tools and their value

Use any tool you like provided it does not interfere with sleep, nutrition or fluids:
- Cold-water immersion: reduces inflammation short term (e.g., between same-day racing stages); over several days ice-cold water inhibits muscular adaptation. Alternating hot and cold may be more beneficial.
- Compression boots: no direct physiological benefit found; athletes feel more recovered; no downside.
- Active recovery (walk or swim): may relieve fatigue sensations; running less likely beneficial, may be harmful; be conservative.
- Gentle stretching (legs, lower back): may reduce stiffness/soreness.
- Massage: most used in pro cycling; benefits appear mostly psychological.
- Foam rollers: relieve tightness sensations.
- Percussion devices: no known downside unless overly aggressive.

### [HPC-C11-007] Performance Management Chart: TSB (form) and the -30 high-risk threshold

### [HPC-C11-008] Monitoring heat adaptation and duration of adaptation

- Sign of adaptation: in an AE1 heat ride, HR rises within minutes to 20 or more beats above zone 1, and RPE goes very high. As you adapt both come down at the same power (still high, less so).
- Do not ride more than 1 hour with HR elevated by 20+ bpm. When it falls to less than 20 bpm above, you may extend time in heat (expected within 10 days); upper limit 90 minutes; workout stays slow, low-power AE1.
- Duration of adaptation: lasts at most about 14 days after heat training ends, with rapid loss in the last few days. Options: heat-adapt in first two Peak weeks then train normally in Race week; or start 10-14 days of heat training in the last Build period, then do easy heat-simulation rides three times weekly in Peak and Race periods to maintain.

### [HPC-C11-009] Race-week sleep and travel

- Sleep is often poor near race day; travel adds to it, especially eastward. Arrive at least two days before the event and get comfortable with sleeping quarters; arriving the day before stacks the cards against you.
- One night of poor sleep has been shown to affect next-day performance in several endurance sports, including cycling time trial, and to impair decision-making.
- Eastward travel of 3 or more time zones is hard. Pre-adapt: shift daily routines (sleep, eating) by 30 minutes per day for 3 or 4 days before travel: earlier bedtime, waking at race-venue time, eating earlier. Even a 1-hour shift helps. Westward is easier (days get longer; eastward makes them shorter, harder to adapt).
- Without time-zone change, a new sleep environment still matters; arrive 2+ days early and get used to sleeping and eating.

### [HPC-C11-010] Enjoyment factor (the other EF) and burnout

- Efficiency factor (EF; watts per heartbeat at low intensity) is tracked methodically in Preparation and Base; EF rising over several weeks in similar rides indicates improving aerobic fitness (carbohydrate-fat crossover point moving right, Fig 8.1). Author's general approach: identify limiters and measure progress in that area from daily workout data.
- More important EF, monitored all season: "enjoyment factor", hard to measure precisely. Declining when you stop enjoying riding and look for reasons not to train (weather, not feeling like it, "more important" things). Indicates a psychological problem and a low-motivation athlete (Johnny, Ch 9); enthusiasm is in decline.
- Possible causes: pushing too hard without expected gains, lifestyle stress, poor diet, lack of sleep, failure to achieve a goal, unclear goals, burnout; also pressure of an unrealistic goal (requiring a monk-like life), or simply the wrong time for this goal. Deep commitment is needed for high performance but the goal must be realistic.
- Remedies: take a break (length unknowable; may be long); change routines (more sleep, healthier food, different company, altered workouts, unplanned vacation); listen to your feelings. Let the goal go or put it on the backburner and ride for fun if needed.
- Restarting: do it differently; find a training partner who will not half-wheel, change the routine entirely, eat healthier and stay well hydrated; hiring a coach may be best (author admits bias) – choose one who is sympathetic/empathic to your situation.

### [HPC-C12-004] Time-limited rider (about 8 h/week)

- About eight hours weekly is about the lowest volume that still allows competitive racing. Every workout needs a defined purpose, followed precisely. Focus on short races: criteriums, cyclocross, time trials. Under 10 hours weekly: focus on short races.

Start every ride in zones 1-2; two days per week in Build finish with about 30 minutes higher intensity (stamina, aerobic capacity or sprint power). - Best time: morning before the day's activities; mostly indoors, needs a good bike ergometer.

### [HPC-C12-005] Older riders: aging physiology and countermeasures

- From a mid-20s peak, VO2max falls roughly 6-7% per decade in a consistently trained high performer (<1%/year); often unnoticed in 30s, compensated by experience. By 40s a further ~7%: about 14% below mid-20s; FTP, sprint power and stamina decline. By 50s racing young riders is not viable; racing mostly among peers ("super agers" are exceptions).
- 50s: early osteoporosis (bone density loss) and sarcopenia (muscle mass loss). Cycling lacks bone-loading (unlike running); riders in late 20s can have bones of a 70-year-old. Crashes at 50 can be very serious.
- Countermeasure: weightlifting. Legs (Ch 4/5 strength work) plus from 50s upper body: one push and one pull exercise weekly in gym workouts (bench press, tricep extensions = push; bicep curls, lat pull downs, weighted rows = pull). Vary loads seasonally.
- Sarcopenia rarely in legs of serious riders; upper body declines. Higher protein diet plus weightlifting helps; trim carbohydrate (starches, sugars) rather than adding calories.

### [HPC-C12-006] Young riders and parents

- Parent involvement is often the worst part of coaching juniors (overly demanding plans; teen skipped workouts). Do not force a child; be a role model who visibly enjoys a sport for life; treat sport as lifetime activity, not a bid to be the next pro; ride together and have fun.
- Research supports participation in a variety of sports as a junior over early specialization for later success.
- Read biographies of champions (Merckx, Fignon, LeMond, Hinault) to learn how they developed.

### [HPC-C13-005] Training and testing cautions: MHR testing, Zone 3 avoidance, sprint safety

- Finding Maximal Heart Rate:
  - What: Field testing to exhaustion for MHR.
  - Limit: Avoid completely. Do NOT go out on the bike and "bust a gut" trying to get heart rate as high as possible.
  - Who: Any cyclist needing an MHR value to set zones.
  - Why: Unnecessary strain/risk.
  - Exception/Alternative: Look back in training diary or online data over the past year for the highest HR recorded (typically from a race, hard group ride, or interval session) and assume that number is MHR until exceeded.

- Zone 3 riding (the "middle intensity" trap):
  - What: Riding in Zone 3 (tempo / Stamina 1).
  - Limit: Best avoided with limited exceptions, especially when concentrating on aerobic endurance.
  - Who: All endurance cyclists (especially self-coached riders prone to riding in the middle).
  - Why: Zone 3 is too hard to reap the aerobic adaptations of LIT and does not do much to boost aerobic capacity.
  - Exceptions: (1) Specific workouts focused on boosting stamina; (2) Final weeks of preparing for an event largely conducted in zone 3 (e.g., gran fondo, century ride); (3) Prescribed exceptions in the Base period.

- Sprinting position and pack safety:
  - What: Head and eye position during standing maximal sprints.
  - Limit: Never drop head down blindly.
  - Who: All riders sprinting.
  - Why: Crash and collision danger when accelerating at high speeds in a pack or on open roads.
  - Cue: Head must remain up and eyes looking forward at all times for safety and maneuvering.

### [HPC-C13-006] Planning cautions (Chapter 4)

- Do not extend the intensity stage/Build beyond eight to 11 weeks of race-like workouts (Build 8-9 weeks): fitness declines, breakdown. Who: all, especially self-coached riders.
- Do not add more than 10 percent weekly hours over prior seasons; pushing beyond capacity risks performance and health.
- Keep Preparation/early Base strictly zones 1-2; do not compete in virtual apps; no heavy breathing in cross-training.
- Avoid multiple A races; two A races no fewer than 12 weeks apart.
- Avoid stamina workouts and weightlifting in race week.
- Transition: no structured training; if exercising, brief and very easy.
- Weighted-backpack walking: 10 percent of bodyweight or less; only for aerobic capacity above about 50; older/lower-capacity riders walk without load.
- Do not always stay race-ready: leads to sickness, injury, burnout, overtraining.

### [HPC-C13-007] Training load caps, progression limits, and periodization cautions

- High-intensity stage duration limit: Do not extend Stage 3 (race-like intensity workouts / Build period) beyond 8 to 11 weeks (Build itself should be no more than 8 to 9 weeks). Extending this stage causes fitness breakdown, stagnation, and loss of performance because the body cannot sustain high-stress intensity for prolonged cycles.
- Annual volume increase limit: When establishing Base 3 weekly volume, athletes may add at most 10 percent more weekly hours over previous seasons. Exceeding this limit leads to persistent fatigue, compromised health, and downward fitness trends.
- Virtual racing restriction during Base/Prep: Avoid competing with other riders on indoor virtual cycling platforms during early season (Preparation and Base). All workouts must remain strictly in HR zones 1 and 2; early unprescribed high intensity short-circuits aerobic base development.
- Spacing limit between A races: Athletes should focus on only one A race per season. If two A races are planned, they must be separated by no fewer than 12 weeks to allow for Transition, base rebuilding, and a proper Build/Peak progression.
- Rigidity caution: Never follow a periodized plan inflexibly. On days of marked fatigue, soreness, low motivation, or impending burnout, make immediate, significant adjustments.

### [HPC-C13-008] Weightlifting cautions

- Main concern: knee and back injury. Loads must be easily managed. Do not flex knees beyond about 90 degrees (more acute angle increases injury risk).
- Strongly recommends a weightlifting belt for back support. If worried about back: very conservative loads for free weights, excellent posture (straight back, tall, head balanced over torso, look forward not down). Deadlift riskiest for back trouble.
- Juniors under age 16: stay in Anatomical Adaptation with relatively light loads and high reps. Athletes new to lifting: avoid Max Strength phase for the first season. Wait until age 16 or at least one season in the sport before heavier loads. If unsure about heavy lifting: use lighter loads with more reps (as in Max Transition). Reason: high injury risk in young/inexperienced lifting heavy. Those new to lifting progress slowly.
- Do not lift to failure.
- Separate gym sessions by two or three days; only one day apart increases injury risk and reduces strength benefits.
- Avoid excessive gym fatigue that impacts the next bike or cross-training workout.
- Hot rides after a hard workout can be dangerous (see heat training entry).

### [HPC-C13-010] Overtraining and distress: cautions

- WHAT: frequent distressful efforts (races, race-like group rides, very hard intervals, field tests), hard workouts on consecutive days, large increases in volume/intensity over recent training, training through fatigue, training hard with limited R&R.
- LIMIT: distress must be rare with broad gaps; fatigue should be gone within two days at most; never stay in R&R-needing state. Volume and intensity must be blended cautiously; large increases over recent training are problematic.
- WHO: all riders, especially self-coached and highly motivated ones.
- WHY: risk of injury, weakened immune system, overtraining syndrome, which can cost a season or a career. Symptoms overlap Lyme disease and chronic fatigue syndrome, so see a doctor.
- EXCEPTION: none; if unsure, rest.

### [HPC-C13-011] Recovery-tool and carbohydrate-restriction cautions

- Any recovery tool must not interfere with sleep, nutrition or fluid intake; if it does, it is detrimental.
- Cold-water immersion over several days inhibits muscular adaptation.
- Running as active recovery after a hard ride is less likely to be beneficial and may be harmful; keep extra exercise after a hard ride very conservative.
- Percussion devices: no known downside unless the user is overly aggressive.
- Carbohydrate restriction before/after long easy rides: if done too frequently it can over-stress the body, leading to excessive overreaching and possibly overtraining through under-recovering. Once weekly in the Base period is probably sufficient.
- Sports drinks outside the immediate post-ride window are best avoided.
- Do not add a third hard ride because you feel recovered.

### [HPC-C13-014] Cautions on nutrition and weight loss (Ch 8)

- Sports products after rides or outside of rides: avoid (use only on bike beyond about 2 h).
- Snacks high in sugar: avoid entirely. Training is not a ticket to eat junk.
- Rapid weight loss: avoid; "can be disastrous". Limit loss to no more than 0.5 kg (1 lb) per two weeks.
- Severe calorie restriction and frequent fasting: not recommended; hurt workouts and recovery.
- Already very lean (<about 14% body fat women, <6% men): weight loss brings muscle loss; downsides outweigh benefits.
- More than 5% bodyweight loss in few weeks: risk of overtraining or illness; needs most of a season.
- Dietary restrictions, allergies, prediabetes, hypertension, vegetarian/vegan: seek registered dietitian/sport nutritionist.
- Carb intake above 60 g/h: nausea risk.
- Returning from illness: see C09 missed-workout rules (no high intensity until EF normal).
- Older riders: osteoporosis/sarcopenia risk; crash at 50 can be very serious.

### [HPC-C13-015] Heat training and hot race safety

- WHAT: heat adaptation rides and racing in heat. LIMIT: 60 to 90 min max per hot ride; beginners of layering method 1 h first week, 90 min max week 2; do not ride over 1 h with HR 20+ bpm above zone 1; hot cool-down after hard rides 30 min or less; AE1 only in heat; no hard rides in heat.
- STOP immediately if excessive fatigue for such a slow ride, at your limit regardless of time ridden, or lightheaded, dizzy or gasping: no exceptions ("not worth a trip to the emergency room").
- Response to symptoms: peel off layers, go to a cool, ventilated or air-conditioned room, sit or lie down, sip ice-cold water or sports drink, ice packs on head and torso; need a helper.
- Heat exhaustion means something was wrong (too hot, started fatigued, dehydrated, overdid intensity/duration). Serious: do not do another hot ride until cause found and corrected. After days away from heat training, be very conservative with temperature and duration.
- WHY: heat exhaustion (loss of fluids and sodium) and heat stroke (body unable to control internal temperature; medical emergency) are dangerous and potentially life-threatening. Racing in extreme heat without adapting is dangerous.
- Layered-clothing method risks "possible disaster": use great caution.

### [HPC-C13-016] High altitude: health risk and iron

- Altitude racing can cause an emergency: symptoms include shortness of breath, fatigue, headache, nausea, dizziness. Unprepared racing at altitude can be as dangerous as unadapted heat; life-threatening, health not just performance. First sign things aren't right: deep breathing.
- Iron: stores likely decline at altitude, lowering hemoglobin; hard to get enough iron from food alone, supplementation usually necessary. Have iron status checked by a doctor three months before travel; if low, discuss a supplement with the physician. Arrive with normal iron since adaptation (more red blood cells) requires iron.
- Be cautious with training load and sleep first 3 to 5 days at altitude.

### [HPC-C13-017] Appendix safety limits for workouts and tests

- Readiness: if unsure of readiness for a long or high-intensity workout, do less. Pushing beyond limits favors illness, injury, burnout and overtraining.
- Knees, hips, lower back:
  - MF1/MF2/MF3 carry high injury risk, especially to the knees. Skip them if you have especially sensitive knees, hips or lower back.
  - MF1: one set only at first; err toward a low gear; only one MF1 workout per week.
  - MF2: requires at least three prior MF1 sessions without knee pain. Stop at the first sign of knee tenderness, even slight. Keep full recovery between sets. No more than once a week. Never stand.
  - MF3: stop if knees become sensitive. Do not do it at all if prone to knee injury. No more than once a week.
- Road safety:
  - Use light-traffic roads for force reps and tests, with safe U-turn spots for MF2.
  - AC1 group rides: watch traffic and less-skilled riders.
  - Descending: crash risk is high, so be conservative with speed.
  - In sprints, keep your head up and eyes forward.
- AE1 and AE2 combination (easy-hard) workouts: no more than twice a week, separated by at least two days.
- T3 stamina test: at least two days of recovery afterwards.
- Tests T1, T2 and T4: do them after rest (a few days of R&R for T1; 3 to 5 days for T2 and T4).

## C14 Formulas & Calculations

### [HPC-C14-001] Efficiency factor (EF) for tracking aerobic fitness

Formula: EF = normalized power ÷ average heart rate, after a low-intensity ride (mostly zone 2). TrainingPeaks calculates it.
Use: compare with a similar ride (comparable course, duration, intensity) about eight weeks earlier. Output (power) vs input (HR) expresses efficiency.
Expectation: in early season (Base), EF rises gradually and steadily over a few weeks, not linearly (it "ratchets up"); by eight weeks it should be clearly higher if doing a lot of easy zone 1-2 rides. Zone 3 rides do not boost the aerobic system nearly as well.
Reasons EF rises: more capillaries in working muscles (more blood, oxygen, fuel; muscle works less hard to drive pedals); better fat use for fuel.

evidence: author-experience
src: Ch 2 "Training Intensity"

### [HPC-C14-002] Stamina benchmark formula

Target power = FTP × 0.75; target duration (minutes) = that same number in watts. Worked: FTP 300 W → 0.75 × 300 = 225 W for 225 min (3 h 45 min). Pro example: 400 W FTP → 300 W for 300 min.

src: Ch 2 "Stamina"

### [HPC-C14-003] Zone and test formulas in Chapter 3

- Zone HR = MHR (or FTHR) × the zone percentage range in Table 3.1.
- FTHR = average HR of the 20-minute test − 5 percent.
- LT1 at 2 mmol/L lactate; LT2 at 4 mmol/L.
- EF = (normalized) power ÷ heart rate.
- IF (stamina test) = workout or interval power as a percentage of FTP/LTP; target 88–92 percent over 90 minutes, above 92 excellent.
- Borg original scale 6–20: rating × 10 ≈ heart rate.
No worked example given.

### [HPC-C14-004] Efficiency Factor (EF)

EF = normalized power of a steady-state segment / average heart rate of the same segment (for a ride: normalized power divided by average heart rate). Higher = more power per heartbeat. Measured after every AE1 and AE2 ride in the Base period; should slowly ratchet up over a few weeks, indicating aerobic endurance is improving. No worked example given.
tags: type:formula sport:cycling metric:power metric:hr level:all subtopic:efficiency-factor
src: Ch 5 | Friel sidebar; Fitness Testing

### [HPC-C14-005] VO2max per kilogram formula and worked example

Relative VO2max formula:
Relative VO2max (ml/kg/min) = (Absolute VO2 [L/min] × 1000) / Body Weight (kg)

Variables:
- Absolute VO2 = maximal oxygen delivery to working muscles per minute (L/min)
- Body Weight = rider weight (kg)
- Relative VO2max = aerobic capacity (ml/kg/min)

Worked example from source:
- Baseline: 70 kg rider (154 lb), oxygen delivery 4.2 L/min: (4.2 × 1000) / 70 = 60 ml/kg/min.
- Post weight loss: rider loses 2 kg (4.4 lb, ~3% bodyweight reduction) to 68 kg; oxygen delivery assumed unchanged at 4.2 L/min: (4.2 × 1000) / 68 = 61.76... rounded to 61.8 ml/kg/min.
- Result: an aerobic capacity increase of ~3% (from 60 to 61.8) from a 3% weight reduction.

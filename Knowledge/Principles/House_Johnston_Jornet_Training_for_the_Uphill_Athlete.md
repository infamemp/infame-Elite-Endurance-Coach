## C01 Overview & Scope

### [TUA-C01-001] Overview & Scope

**What the book is.** *Training for the Uphill Athlete: A Manual for Mountain Runners and Ski Mountaineers* (Steve House, Scott Johnston, Kílian Jornet; Patagonia, 2019). It is a training manual for mountain running, skimo (ski mountaineering racing) and ski mountaineering. The three sports are treated together because their physiological demands largely coincide. Skimo and ski mountaineering differ mainly in uphill tempo and intensity, because of equipment weight. It is a successor to *Training for the New Alpinism*.

**For whom.** Competitive and recreational mountain athletes. Two categories are used throughout:
- Category 1: builds a base.
- Category 2: maintains and supplements an existing base.
The methods apply equally to noncompetitive athletes, because "your body faces the same physiological limitations".

**Problem it solves.** Athletes copy elite plans, follow fads (HIIT, VO2-max shortcuts, Zone 3 as a "one-stop shop"), overtrain, and never maximize the aerobic engine. The book gives a framework to self-coach: understand the physiology, monitor individual response, and build and adjust a plan.

**Central thesis.** Endurance is a metabolic quality. The key to uphill performance is maximizing aerobic capacity (the Aerobic Threshold, AeT) through a very large volume of low-intensity work. This is then supplemented with limited, well-timed high-intensity, strength and muscular endurance work. Training must be continuous, gradual and modulated, and individualized through monitoring. "This book is not a cookbook."

**Organization.**
- Section One: physiology (the why).
- Section Two: methodology (zones, principles, monitoring, testing, interval and continuous methods).
- Section Three: strength (assessments, general strength, specific strength and muscular endurance).
- Section Four: how to train (programming, Transition Period, Base Period, skimo chapter, mountain running chapter).
- Glossary.
Each part supports the next. Athlete stories carry transferable lessons only.

**What it does NOT cover.**
- No single prescription or recipe.
- Little nutrition (post-exercise fueling and fat adaptation are mentioned, but there is no diet plan).
- Skimo technique is only listed (space constraints).
- No cycling or swimming programs; these appear only as recovery or cross-training.
- No muscle-mass gain program.
- Full example plans for many cases are deferred to UphillAthlete.com.
- No clinical treatment of overtraining or illness beyond rest and graded return.

### [TUA-C01-002] What the book is and who it is for

Training for the Uphill Athlete (House, Johnston, Jornet) is a training manual for mountain runners, skimo racers and ski mountaineers. The three sports are complementary in the demands they make on the body. Many skiers run for training, and runners increasingly take up skiing. Where the training converges, the book discusses the sports together. Where it diverges, it explains why and how to apply different techniques.
- Skimo and ski mountaineering differ in the tempo, and hence the intensity, of uphill skinning, because ski mountaineering equipment is heavier.
- The methods work equally for competitive and recreational or noncompetitive mountain athletes. The body faces the same physiological limitations whether or not you wear a race number.
- The book draws on three lifetimes of knowledge of the science and methodology of endurance training. It builds on Training for the New Alpinism (2014), which applied conventional sport methodology to climbing. This book applies methods developed and tested in conventional endurance sports to unconventional sports.
- It is a distillation of hundreds of thousands of hours of racing, training and coaching.

### [TUA-C01-005] Chapter 11 overview: purpose of the Base Period

Mountain running and skimo are complementary seasonally and share most physiological demands, so one book covers both. Skimo and ski mountaineering spend well over 80 percent of time and energy on uphill sections; running more than 50 percent. A few percent gain in uphill speed can save several minutes on a long climb, so the focus is uphill performance.
- Aerobic base miles/kilometers in running have their analog in skimo as base vertical feet/meters: many thousands of feet (meters) of Zones 1–2 aerobic work, supplemented by some strength and higher-intensity training.
- Runners also need enough elevation gain and loss plus the distance of the goal event.
- The bulk of training is the same for both sports: time on feet going up and down mountains is the vast majority of training.
- Purpose of the Base Period: increase work capacity and improve fatigue resistance, so you can handle more work and later more intense work.
- Method: the progression principle, slightly exceeding work capacity in many workouts, then recovering and repeating for months. Progression must be gradual and continuous (no breaks). Random, sporadic exercise gives worse results.
- Plans assume you want to maximize performance and will do a focused plan for months. No shortcuts.
- Principles to follow: consistency, gradual increase of load, modulation from hard to easy over days and weeks.

## C02 Philosophy & Core Principles

### [TUA-C02-001] Core principles (summary)

**What the authors optimize for:** long-term (year-to-year) endurance built on a huge aerobic base, with injury and overtraining avoided. "Real endurance is built from year to year, not weekly or monthly."

**Principles and their reasoning**
1. **Endurance is a metabolic quality.** Speed depends on the ATP recycling rate. Endurance is sustaining high ATP turnover mainly aerobically.
2. **Maximize AeT first.** "You will never maximize your endurance potential without first maximizing your basic aerobic capacity (AeT)." A bigger aerobic "vacuum cleaner" (slow-twitch mitochondria) clears pyruvate and lactate, lets you do more anaerobic work and recover faster.
3. **Capacity before utilization.** Capacity training (aerobic base, strength, technique) takes the majority of time and continues in all phases. Utilization (race-specific, high-intensity) gives fast but volatile gains and also erodes capacity.
4. **Low intensity is the bulk.** About 80–85% of elite volume is in Z1–Z2, and the book states 80–90% of annual volume as the target. High intensity is a supplement, never a substitute.
5. **Continuity, Gradualness, Modulation.** These are the three principles of all successful programs. Frequent gentle nudges work; infrequent bludgeoning does not.
6. **Individuality.** Ten athletes doing the same workout show ten responses. Learn your own response through logs and tests.
7. **Specificity grows with level.** Beginners benefit from cross-training; better athletes need foot-borne, event-like work.
8. **Strength serves performance.** Strength is used to improve performance and prevent injury, not for gym numbers, and without added mass. Muscular endurance matters more than max strength.
9. **Recovery is part of training.** Adaptation happens in recovery. Monitor fatigue and act early.
10. **Better slightly undertrained than slightly overtrained.**

**What they reject**
- Fads, HIIT-only plans and VO2-max chasing.
- Copying elites or friends.
- Going hard as often as possible.
- Training through illness.
- Responding to a performance drop with more training.
- False precision in zones.
- Blind adherence to any plan.

**Non-negotiables**
- Build the aerobic base before intensity.
- Progress gradually.
- Stop training at the first signs of illness.
- Record training.
- Take recovery weeks.
- Keep the Zone 1–2 volume when adding intensity.

**Spirit.** Training should be enjoyable and low in stress. It exists to enable the mountains, not replace them.

### [TUA-C02-002] Not a cookbook: individualization within a narrow range of options

- Training is often presented as a formulaic recipe ("follow the recipe and your fitness cake will turn out perfectly"). The authors reject this.
- Humans share the same physiological systems, but responses to identical training, in quantity and in kind, vary a great deal. If a coach has ten athletes do the identical session and measures physiological systems, he will see ten different responses.
- General guidelines are needed, but without some idea of their individual response athletes are "flying blind".
- The book therefore explains the underlying systems that account for variation among athletes. It teaches how to determine your individual responses to training and what they mean.
- The goal is a set of tools that lets you operate within a fairly narrow range of training options while individualizing the minutiae so training yields the best results for you.

### [TUA-C02-003] Anti-fad stance; do not copy elite athletes' training

- The fitness world is described as "a minefield of snake oil salesmen, fads, and claims that are too good to be true". The book calls itself "the anti-fad".
- It offers proven knowledge to apply for decades. The only promise is "smart, hard, consistent work".
- Do not match the training (volume, technique, or most other aspects) of the elite athletes in the stories, Kílian included. Understand the theory behind what they say and the workouts they describe, and apply the same principles at your own level. "Blindly copying the workouts of world-class athletes is a bad idea."
- Learning from your own mistakes is the best teacher, but painful and a waste of time. The authors have made all the training mistakes, some more than once, and there are many more ways to train wrong than right. The book aims to steer readers around the pitfalls.

### [TUA-C02-005] The Big Motor and the foundation principle

Increasing aerobic capacity benefits all athletes regardless of event duration. Raising AeT (size of the vacuum) has direct, profound effect on raising LT (power of the vacuum); improves recovery rates from workouts including very high-intensity ones (a big advantage of Kílian Jornet); a big aerobic motor allows handling more anaerobic work, and since adaptation tracks time spent training, more anaerobic work can then be done. The whole fitness house must be built from the ground up on a solid foundation (high AeT) before worrying about the upper floors. Training can bias metabolism toward 1–2 minute anaerobic events or multi-hour/day events but never both optimally at once: target training to the outcome desired.

### [TUA-C02-006] Become a student of the sport

Experimenting on a sample size of one (yourself) is risky and likely to be misleading. Self-coached athletes should learn what methods do and do not work, and why. Understanding cause and effect turns training from blindly following a recipe into informed decisions. Coaching (especially of yourself) is artfully applying time-tested methods. Models are simplified and limited in predictive ability, but useful for understanding complex systems like the body.
Coaches, not scientists, led the establishment of effective training methods by trial and error; scientists later explained why they work.

### [TUA-C02-008] Capacity Training vs Utilization Training (Bowman)

Terms used by Bob Bowman (coach of Michael Phelps).
Capacity Training: improves the long-term performance potential; prioritized in the Base Period; improves fundamental qualities supporting the event and Utilization Training; often not sport specific.
Utilization Training: improves near-term performance results; prioritized in the build-up to competition/targeted event; models the specific demands of the event.
Debate: general capacity first with short utilization phase vs. event-specific work maximally utilizing current capacity. US running's 1990s-2000s disappointing results coincided with a shift to utilization; German rowing in the 1980s switched to capacity and came to dominate; in 2004 Dr. Jan Helgerud (spelled "Helgarud" in source) denounced Capacity Training as useless in cross-country skiing. The capacity-first approach largely won in the competitive arena.
Kílian's ability to handle huge, hard volume results from decades of capacity building. Copying elite training without their capacity-building years is actually Utilization Training and not best for long-term gains.
Capacity Training takes the majority of an athlete's time. Utilization gains are quick and seductive; real endurance is built from year to year, not weekly or monthly.

### [TUA-C02-009] Heart rate as the intensity tool for uphill athletes

Intensity dictates energy systems and which/how many muscle fibers (ST or FT) are recruited; training effect is directly related to intensity. Power meters (cycling) and pace (road/track running) are possible, but uphill athletes train on varied terrain and conditions, making heart rate the only practical method of controlling intensity. Kílian monitors with an HR monitor and after years can estimate HR within a few beats by feel.

### [TUA-C02-010] Why the five-zone system and why not more zones

All zone systems are flawed: they associate HR with intensity, but response varies day to day (recovery state is the most powerful factor); anchor markers can move several bpm daily; the same HR is a different training effect when recovered vs. after a hard effort (e.g. HR 145). Metabolic response does not fit neat zones with hard boundaries; individual variation is large. Systems range from three zones (AeT and LT only) to seven zones, which gives false precision (HR cannot be controlled within 1 bpm in real training). Authors use the traditional five-zone system: aerobic zones (Z1, Z2) easy-moderate; Z3 hard; anaerobic zones (Z4, Z5) very hard, shorter.

### [TUA-C02-012] Athlete stories: transferable lessons

- Dakota Jones: "more is better" and single-minded focus led to stress fracture, hamstring strain and Achilles injury; training is a means to do what you love — don't forget what you train for; train smarter, not more.
- John Kelly (Barkley 2017 winner): with no mountains or time, training is an optimization problem under constraints; he improved time efficiency (commute running, nearby short steep hill 95 ft in 1/20 mile, incline treadmill, intensity to maintain LT), got ~20 more miles and 6,000 ft more gain per week than 2015, though treadmill gave less downhill running. In 2015 poor nutrition, not fitness, ended his attempt (gels and bars insufficient for 30+ hours).
- Rickey Gates: rigid watch-and-metric regimentation failed; running by feel and consistency (one run a day rule) worked; listen to both mental and physical state.
- Mike Foote: durability (limit slowing down) is the goal in 100-mile races; builds it via high training volume, strength work (even once a week in gym), and self-care (massage, foam rolling, yoga, diet, sleep).
- Anna Frost: ultimately learned running is what she does, not who she is (after hitting physical bottom from constant racing).

### [TUA-C02-013] Monitoring principles: gradualness, listening to the body

- The higher the goal, the harder the work, and the closer the athlete is to the limit of physical and mental stress. Most athletes benefit from at least one monitoring tool; only a few rare individuals manage on intuition alone.
- "High" is relative: an intolerable load for a beginner is not even maintenance for a world-class athlete. There is no one-size-fits-all prescription, so the book teaches theory and practice instead of a fixed prescription. Sound principles plus attentive monitoring give the best outcome.
- Principle of gradualness: except for rare, premeditated overreaching, let the body absorb the previous load and build progressively. Bodies do not like drastic changes.
- Learn to interpret subtle body signals: listen while they are still whispered, before they become shouted alarms.
- Short-term tiredness is normal. It is usually dealt with by reducing the load for a day or so. Do not let it become chronic, or you risk overtraining.
- Do not train compulsively. Lack of motivation in an otherwise motivated athlete is the body saying to back off; it is a clear warning sign.
- A hard workout on the plan does not mean you are ready to execute it or benefit from it. Fatigue determines preparedness to train, so assessing it accurately is critical.

### [TUA-C02-015] Over- versus undertrained axiom; motivation vs compulsion

- "It is far better to be slightly undertrained than to be even a little overtrained." An undertrained, rested athlete can draw deeper on willpower in competition; an overtrained, tired athlete cannot conjure strength from an empty well.
- Most endurance athletes are type A and adhere to "more is better"; more endurance training is generally better, and drive is the most important attribute, but it can cloud judgment and become a merciless taskmaster.
- The cost of overtraining and underrecovering is far worse than undertraining: err on the conservative side.

### [TUA-C02-016] Aerobic base: volume is the key

You will never maximize endurance without first maximizing basic aerobic capacity (AeT). Genetics dominate power/speed sports; perseverance and ability to suffer mark the endurance athlete. How much is enough cannot be answered generally: it depends on individual capacity for the work (a warm-up for Kílian may overload others).
Optimal load is individualized: start small and work up. Excitement at season start makes it easy to overreach in the first weeks, which is demoralizing; it is much easier to raise a too-light load than to recover from too much too soon ("hard to progress if you have to move backward").
Expectations: results are directly proportional to time spent in preparation. Three 45-minute stair-machine workouts a week will not reach ultimate potential but may be a reasonable starting place. Do not copy pro programs: without a pro's work capacity you will likely be overtrained or injured. The body adapts to imposed loads given sufficient time; no secret, just work ethic and a lifestyle built around it. Ericsson's iceberg illusion: elite feats are the end product of years of preparation.

### [TUA-C02-017] Purpose of strength training for endurance athletes

- Athletes strength train to improve performance in their event and for injury prevention, not to become stronger in the gym. Especially true for endurance athletes.
- Strength is one of several tools; athletes get stronger to enhance performance, not to be stronger.
- Not all strength training is done in the gym lifting weights.
- Gaining strength without adding overall body mass is critically important: you have to carry the muscles around. No recipe for gaining muscle mass is given in the book.
- Improving muscular endurance can strongly improve performance and is an important part of most programs; by enhancing fatigue resistance it maintains higher running economy in long events.
- Warning against the "gym strong" mentality: too much general strength detracts from specific training time and can bring bulky muscles.

### [TUA-C02-018] Programming: plan is a suggestion, not a blueprint

- No one-size-fits-all plan exists; the book gives principles, example weeks and experience-based guidelines, not a cookbook recipe. The reader crafts a customized plan from background, goals and needs.
- A well-coached but less-than-ideal plan, well administered, beats the best plan poorly administered. Administration is the main challenge: the art of coaching takes over from the science.
- A written plan is mechanistic (do workout A, get benefit B) and black-and-white; the athlete/coach must work in shades of gray, assessing daily preparedness to do the workout and the benefit received from it (hence the monitoring tools of the first half of the book).
- The plan is a suggestion and will have to change as you proceed. Blind adherence to any training plan is an almost surefire route to inferior results, if not disaster.
- Self-coaching is difficult. Professional athletes use coaches for an objective observer to offer guidance in doubt and to bounce ideas off.
- Quote (Renato Canova): Training is not the work you do. It is the effect that work has on your body.

### [TUA-C02-019] Base Period fatigue, C-priority races and capacity vs utilization

- During much of the Base Period you will be a little too tired to perform at your best. This is why elite athletes build base before the main competitive period.
- Races or significant events inserted in the Base Period should be low priority (C level) and barely exceed what you typically do in training, so they minimally disturb progression.
- Capacity (fitness) cannot be built effectively while maximally utilizing it (races, important events) at the same time (see capacity versus utilization, p. 74).
- Starting a skimo program in December that overlaps with the racing season compromises training, race results, or both.

### [TUA-C02-020] Train your weaknesses, race your strengths (low-hanging fruit)

Quote: "Train your weaknesses and race your strengths." (Eddie Borysewicz). The Base Period must be individualized: identify what holds you back through detached analysis plus knowledge.
- Do NOT train only what you are good at or enjoy. People gravitate to strengths and avoid weaknesses; gains in strong areas get harder every year, whereas shifting emphasis to weaknesses can deliver big results.
- Examples:
  - Stronger on uphills in running races but dropped on flatter, faster terrain: aerobic endurance is probably relatively higher than running economy. Shift some emphasis to flatter, faster running and technique work.
  - Strong downhill skiing background, dominates skimo descents but loses time on uphills: put more effort into aerobic endurance.
  - Legs very sore and muscle fatigue lingering several days after a long run or race with lots of vertical gain and loss: increase leg strength and muscular endurance in the Base Period.
- Use racing history: on what parts or types of courses are you relatively worse than your peer group? Pay more attention to those.
- Beginners improve rapidly in all areas; the better you get, the more elusive gains become.
- Time and energy are limited, even for professionals. Just adding miles or vertical shows diminishing returns at some point; be open to changing the training stimulus.

### [TUA-C02-021] Best preparation for winter skimo: summer mountain running volume plus ski simulations

- The best way to improve winter skimo results and the ski mountaineering experience is to spend a summer running many hours and many thousands of vertical meters in the mountains.
- Running uphill is similar to skinning. Adding ski simulations (Ski Striding, Hill Bounding, roller skiing) and ski-specific muscular endurance in late summer and early fall eases the transition to on-snow training.
- Technique caps results: skimo has a large technical component; ignoring technique puts a ceiling on race results even though fitness plays the main role.

## C03 Integral Model

### [TUA-C03-001] Integral Model

**1. Chain: beliefs → mechanisms → methods → decisions**

*Belief:* mountain performance is limited by the aerobic metabolism, and there is no shortcut. Real fitness is built over years.

*Mechanism* (see "Metabolic mix and the fate of pyruvate"):
- Glycolysis makes pyruvate.
- Pyruvate either enters the mitochondria (aerobic, mainly slow-twitch fibers) or becomes lactate + H+ (fatigue).
- Fat oxidation makes no lactate.
- LT is where production exceeds removal.
- Two levers raise LT: (1) a bigger aerobic capacity (a high AeT), trained by volume at or below AeT; (2) a better lactate shuttle, trained by limited high intensity.
- Aerobic adaptations are structural and slow. High-intensity adaptations are functional and fast, which is why they are overused. A plateau therefore calls for more base, not more intensity.

*Methodology:* capacity vs utilization; the five zones anchored on personal AeT and LT; Continuity, Gradualness and Modulation; periodization (Transition → Base → Precompetition → Competition).

*Decisions:* where the AeT–LT gap sits, how much of each zone, how fast to ramp, and when to back off.

**2. Key variables, in priority order**
1. Aerobic volume (time, vertical, distance) at Z1/Z2.
2. The AeT–LT spread (Ten Percent Test).
3. Progression rate and modulation. See "Gradualness and annual volume progression" (25% per year for beginners under 350–400 h; 10% maximum for advanced over 500 h) and the weekly rule of 10% or less.
4. Recovery state (monitoring tools).
5. Intensity dose. Z3 is at most about 10% of annual volume; Z4 is under 10% of annual volume; Z5 is tiny.
6. Strength and muscular endurance (support only).
7. Technique and economy, trained on event-like terrain.
8. Event specificity.

**3. How the tools fit**
- *Tests* give anchors: AeT by MAF, ventilatory marker, lactate step test or gas exchange test; LT by a 30–60 min maximal uphill effort. Together they feed the "Ten Percent Test".
- *Zones* (Fig 3.3) turn the anchors into daily HR targets.
- *Category 1/2* picks a macrocycle (Figs 11.2–11.10). *Week types* B, I, R, S, T, G fill the weeks, and workouts are laid out only 1–2 weeks ahead.
- *Workouts*: continuous Z1–Z2, pick-ups, tempo and progressive distance, Z3 and Z4 intervals, 30/30s, Hill Sprints/Bounding, and ME gym or outdoor sessions.
- *Strength*: assessments → Stage 1–3 → specific work.
- *Monitoring*: perception, resting HR, step and stair tests, A–F grades, ferritin.

**4. Decision flow for a new athlete**
1. Define a goal and A/B/C events. Do the time reality test ("Planning outline").
2. Choose the category (400 h/year over the past two years, age 18, successful events at or near the target distance) via "Step 1: Athlete Category 1 vs Category 2".
3. Test AeT and LT, then run the Ten Percent Test.
   - Gap over 10%: ADS. Do nearly all base work in Z2 (slow pace is accepted), delay Z3, and allow at most one high-intensity session per week.
   - Gap of 10% or less: Z1 base with Z2 limited, and up to two high-intensity sessions per week. Drop Z2 when adding Z3.
4. Assess strength (knee tracking, push-up, pull-up, plank). Start remedial Stage 1–3 work, then add Hill Sprints/Bounding and ME.
5. Start the Transition Period if needed. Starting volume is 50% of the previous cycle after a break over 4 weeks and 80% after a sport switch with a break of 2 weeks or less. Build 7–10% per week with a recovery week every 4th week at a 50% cut.
6. Base Period: B weeks, then I weeks. Add Z3 from about 5% to 10% of weekly volume. Add Z4 by replacing Z3 at a 2:1 ratio, once Z3 reaches about 10%. Keep the loss of Z1–2 volume to 5% or less. Keep weekly increases to 10% or less, no more than 3 build weeks, one variable at a time, and recovery weeks at −40–60%.
7. Precompetition (S weeks; high-intensity events like VK and skimo emphasize Z4, ultras and ski mountaineers use back-to-back long days). Taper by event priority. C races are treated as workouts. Race season uses the A/B/C scenarios.
8. Daily: warm-up as a readiness check. Grade each workout (stop rule: more than two Cs in a row, or a C and a D in one week). Recovery rules: after a fatigue break of over 2 days, resume at 50% then 75% of duration; with illness, stop at the first signs, then do one easy day per sick day.
9. Review: if there is no progressive improvement over weeks, suspect too much load, too little recovery or life stress, and reduce rather than add.

**5. Tensions, trade-offs, exceptions**
- Aerobic and anaerobic stimuli are partly mutually exclusive. You cannot optimize both at once, so target the training to the event.
- Zone 3 is seductive and effective, but overusing it means utilization training.
- ADS athletes must accept very slow pace. High-AeT athletes find Z2 too taxing and use Z1 mostly.
- Elites (Kílian: about 1,300 h per year, little structured intervals, races as intensity; no taper) are an exception that rests on decades of base and fast recovery. Others should not copy them.
- Models (zones, five-zone HR, FT/ST) are "wrong but useful". Daily variation means zones are guidance.
- Fatter plans vs time-limited athletes: for those limited by time rather than energy, recovery strategies matter less.
- Overreaching is allowed for advanced athletes only, followed by a recovery week cut of up to 70%.
- Strength: no one knows how much is enough. Too much mass is harmful.
- Evidence mix: the polarized and fat-adaptation studies are cited, while much of the method rests on coaching experience (see C15).

## C04 Mechanisms

### [TUA-C04-001] Evolution of endurance and fuel storage

- Protohuman metabolism evolved for economical use of scarce calories: many hours of low-intensity searching with occasional short high-intensity bursts. Diet high in animal fat and protein with some complex carbohydrate.
- Fat is stored (intramuscular and subcutaneous) as fuel tank for lean times. Well-adapted hunter-gatherers could go longer between meals and work at higher intensities on fat, conserving scarce glycogen.
- Humans recover muscle glycogen quickly during rest (hours instead of days), unlike most animals.
- Fat is a complex molecule; carbohydrate yields about half the calories per gram of fat.
- Storage capacity: a lean, well-trained endurance athlete can carry up to 100,000 calories as readily accessible fat; carbohydrate storage no more than 2,000 calories even in a well-trained athlete.
- Body stores excess calories from any source as fat; on a purely physiological level this explains modern obesity (abundant calorie-dense food, rare shortage).
- Persistence-hunting theory: endurance plus hairlessness let hominids run prey to exhaustion; the high-protein diet allowed bigger brains. Humans are predisposed to endurance (long low- to moderate-intensity work).

### [TUA-C04-002] Endurance and fatigue; causes of slowdown

- Endurance = the maximum sustainable work rate (speed or power) an athlete can maintain for the duration of an event before fatigue forces a reduction. Fatigue limits endurance. In these sports it shows as reduced stride length and slower stride rate.
- Type of endurance needed and type of fatigue experienced are relative to the event (e.g., vertical-kilometer vs 50K trail run).
- Training makes you more fatigue resistant. Slowdown is caused principally by: 1) inability to meet the energy demands of exercise; this results from 2) depletion or accumulation of certain metabolites, or 3) a reduced motor nerve signal.
- Effective endurance training works by causing fatigue to the systems, which induces adaptation. Because systems are interconnected, monodirectional training (primarily one system) inevitably yields diminishing returns: a plateau and, in the worst cases, degradation of performance.

### [TUA-C04-003] Four systems model: brain, oxygen delivery, oxygen utilization, muscle

- Brain/CNS: Central Governor Theory (Tim Noakes, MD, PhD): brain is the ultimate arbiter of fatigue; whatever the source of fatigue, the brain's response is reduced muscle activation (reduced power output). Governor cannot be located physically but the model best explains endurance limits. Training raises the governor's limit so more work can be done before the alarm state.
- Oxygen delivery (heart, lungs, vessels): healthy lungs are overbuilt (surface area of one side of a tennis court) and do not adapt after puberty. Heart pumping capacity is the biggest limiter of O2 delivery in healthy people. Heart can be trained to increase stroke volume, up to a genetically predetermined limit; matures in adolescence, adapts fastest then. Untrained young or older individuals can see rapid, significant stroke-volume change; athletically mature individuals (long training history) see small if any change. Stroke volume sets the upper limit of aerobic power.
- Oxygen utilization (aerobic metabolic system): rate muscles use delivered O2; the most highly trainable system, improvable for many years into athletic maturity; the main focus of most endurance training.
- Muscular system: brain via motor nerves controls which fibers are recruited. Long-duration training improves aerobic qualities mainly of slow-twitch (ST) fibers; shorter high-intensity training improves anaerobic (glycolytic) qualities mainly of fast-twitch (FT) fibers.

### [TUA-C04-004] Endurance tripod: VO2 max, movement economy, lactate threshold

Endurance performance rests on three attributes:
1) VO2 max: maximum volume of oxygen taken up and used during intense exercise, in ml/kg/min (aerobic power-to-weight). Responds best in the young or undertrained; difficult to change in the well trained. It is the product of heart output and muscle O2 utilization; more working muscle mass means more O2 use (XC skiing, skimo use four limbs, often very high values).
2) Movement economy: energy cost to move your body a given distance at a given speed (race pace). Two components: mechanical (technique) and metabolic. Example: if athlete A uses 20 percent more energy than B to cover 1 km in four minutes, A needs more endurance or tires sooner. Metabolic economy depends on the pathway muscles use to make energy; pathways differ in energetic cost and stress. Both components are highly trainable.
3) Lactate Threshold (LT): maximum work rate sustainable for a long duration; pace at LT is the best predictor of endurance performance. The rate at which metabolism can sustainably produce energy is the single largest determinant of endurance, and perhaps the most adaptable to training.
Duration rule: in healthy, well-trained individuals in events under two hours, endurance is limited primarily by the rate muscle cells can sustainably produce energy; over two hours, fuel stores become the major limiter.

### [TUA-C04-005] ATP, metabolism and the two pathways

- ATP (adenosine triphosphate) is the short-term energy storage molecule of every cell; energy released when its bonds break powers muscle contraction. ATP production rate limits the speed sustainable beyond a few seconds. Endurance athletes must raise ATP turnover rate and sustain it as long as the event requires.
- Digestion breaks food into fat, carbohydrate, protein, then fatty acids and sugars used to make ATP. Metabolism = breaking molecular bonds in fats and sugars to reassemble ATP (an ATP recycling plant: ATP loses a phosphate to ADP for contraction; food energy re-adds it). Resting daily ATP need would be about 60–70 kg if not recycled.
- Anaerobic glycolysis: carbohydrate path; sugars converted to glucose by liver, used or stored as glycogen in muscle/liver. Ten-step breakdown of glycogen, no oxygen, ends in pyruvate plus 2 ATP. Much faster than aerobic; used when ATP demand exceeds aerobic supply (high intensity).
- Aerobic metabolism: in mitochondria, needs oxygen, uses pyruvate and fat; produces roughly seventeen times the ATP of the anaerobic path (figure: 36 ATP vs 2 ATP; text elsewhere: about thirty-six additional ATP per pyruvate fate) but is slower. Protein contributes only a tiny fraction of exercise energy. Recent studies of elite athletes show aerobic contribution can be significant up to the highest intensities in well-trained, fat-adapted athletes.
- Chain: ATP fuels muscle work; metabolism resynthesizes ATP; faster recycling = more work per second = power; more ATP = more muscle power = more speed (all else equal).
- Endurance = ability to sustain rapid ATP production, predominantly aerobic, for long durations. All endurance training enhances metabolic turnover (more ATP output rate = more muscle work rate). Endurance is a metabolic quality.
- Mitochondria: organelles in every cell except blood cells; ST fibers have high mitochondrial concentration (aerobic, fatigue resistant); FT fibers less, rely more on anaerobic metabolism.

### [TUA-C04-006] Metabolic mix and the fate of pyruvate (lactate as problem or benefit)

- Both engines always contribute; the mix depends on: Intensity (at a certain speed aerobic alone cannot meet ATP demand and glycolysis dominates); Genetics (more ST fibers delay glycolysis dominance); Training history (high-volume low-moderate training makes ST aerobically adapted; higher-intensity history predisposes toward anaerobic/carbohydrate use).
- Pyruvate fork: enters mitochondria for aerobic metabolism (about thirty-six additional ATP, desirable) or lingers in cytosol and becomes lactic acid, which dissociates into lactate plus a hydrogen ion. H+ is an acid; unchecked accumulation upsets cell acid/base balance, reduces glycolytic ATP production, felt as muscular fatigue and slowing. Lactic acid forms only when pyruvate hangs around and does not enter aerobic metabolism.
- Aerobic metabolism of fat produces no lactate, so fat is the more desirable endurance fuel.
- Below aerobic capacity, mitochondria take up the pyruvate and lactate production is minimal. A "traffic jam" (insufficient aerobic capacity, Fig 2.4) backs pyruvate into lactate + H+; with high aerobic capacity (Fig 2.5) homeostasis holds at higher outputs.
- Metabolic fate of pyruvate is the essential event determining endurance in events longer than two minutes; end goal of most endurance training is to get pyruvate into mitochondria.

### [TUA-C04-007] Two ways to raise LT; lactate shuttle; bigger vacuum cleaner

LT can be raised by: 1) reducing pyruvate/lactate production, 2) increasing removal from working muscles. These need different, to some extent mutually exclusive, training.
- Lactate shuttle (Dr. George Brooks, UC Berkeley): transporter proteins move lactate from where it accumulates to mitochondria of adjacent ST muscle to be used as fuel; best explanation of LT. Elevated muscle lactate signals an increase in transporter proteins, so high-intensity training causing lactate accumulation enhances the shuttle.
- A large volume of low-to-moderate aerobic work judiciously supplemented with higher intensity gives highest LT and best results. Key misunderstanding: how much high intensity; it is much less than commonly assumed.
- Vacuum cleaner analogy: aerobic metabolism (esp. ST) sucks up pyruvate. (1) Increase aerobic capacity (bigger vacuum: more mitochondria in ST) via high volume low-to-moderate work; (2) improve the shuttle (more powerful vacuum) via high-intensity work producing significant lactate. The bigger the vacuum, the more high-intensity work possible and the more power/speed sustained longer.
- Counterintuitive: to improve high-intensity endurance, maximize aerobic capacity of the low-power ST fibers.

### [TUA-C04-008] Structural vs functional adaptations; allure of high intensity

- Aerobic (lower intensity) adaptations are structural: new protein structures (denser capillary beds, increased mitochondrial mass, heart changes). Slow: weeks to months, continue for years; explains long careers improving into the forties.
- High-intensity adaptations are functional: changes in pre-existing structures (glycolytic enzyme concentrations, lactate transporters). Occur within hours, significant in days and weeks.
- Rapid, hard-feeling gains lead athletes/coaches to overuse high intensity. Result: after a few rewarding weeks, plateau then decline, because ST fibers lack stimulus and aerobic capacity drops (the vacuum shrinks). The cause is too little low intensity rather than too much high.
- Plateau or drop is often misread as need for more intensity; the opposite is needed: restore aerobic base. Maintain sufficient aerobic-capacity workouts during high-intensity phases. Intuition ("weak at something, train it more") fails if the vacuum is too small.
- Both low and high intensity are needed; balance and timing are critical.

### [TUA-C04-010] VO2 max: what it is and why it is overrated

- VO2 max is the upper limit of aerobic power (analogous to maximum strength); not a measure of endurance, though it relates to endurance potential. Most important determinant: stroke volume. Largely genetically predetermined; least trainable of the three tripod legs in the well trained; poorest correlation to performance.
- Elite athletes often show no change or even reduction in VO2 max over a season/career while performance improves (via economy and speed at LT). Authors coached high-VO2max athletes with modest results and modest-VO2max athletes with exceptional results; so skeptical of training to raise VO2 max rather than performance.
- VO2 max is a "first wave response": develops quickly in the young and novices (heart adapts rapidly).
- Why overemphasized: studies are short (functional HIIT adaptations appear quickly), use untrained/recreational subjects (almost any training raises VO2 max), report average changes ignoring individual response, and VO2 max is easily measured and comparable. Hill (Nobel 1922) did not intend it to describe endurance.
- Takeaways: beware studies claiming HIIT is "the" recipe because VO2 max rose X percent; no new shortcut exists; endurance training history is hundreds of years of trial and error by many coaches, tested by the stopwatch. No world-class runners, rowers, XC skiers or skimo athletes use exclusive HIIT. HIIT should supplement, not substitute for, aerobic base training.

### [TUA-C04-011] The Training Effect and supercompensation (Fig 3.1)

When physiological systems move too far outside homeostasis they enter a crisis state; a training stimulus doing this triggers the Training Effect (from Hans Selye's General Adaptation Syndrome; Selye coined biological "stress"). Quote (Renato Canova): "Training is not the work you do but the effect it has on your body."
Stress first shows as fatigue, later as adaptations giving greater resistance to the same stress. A session produces fatigue and temporarily diminished work capacity; adaptation occurs in the recovery phase, then supercompensation (above baseline). Applying a new stimulus during supercompensation, over weeks and months, improves fitness.
Fig 3.1 curves: Training too easy = minor drop, quick recovery, minimal supercompensation barely above baseline. Training adequate = moderate drop, recovery rises into clear supercompensation. Training too hard = deep drop, prolonged recovery, no supercompensation.
Too frequent stimuli with insufficient recovery, or too infrequent with too much inactivity, both prevent adaptation and the athlete becomes less fit.

### [TUA-C04-013] Capacity Training: types and characteristics

Capacity Training increases the ability to do work in each realm required for the sport.
- Aerobic Capacity: improves main locomotive muscles' ability to produce ATP aerobically (increases in mitochondrial mass, capillary density, aerobic enzymes, cardiac output). Top priority for all endurance athletes; one can never have so much aerobic capacity that it harms performance.
- Anaerobic Capacity: increases glycolytic ATP production. One CAN have too much, depending on event.
- Strength Capacity: increases maximum muscular force in sport-specific movements and local muscular endurance of main locomotive muscles. Beneficial up to a point; excess muscle mass causes problems for mountain endurance athletes.
- Technical Capacity: improves economy (energy cost) of locomotion, balance, movement patterning.
Notes: gains accrue slowly but last longer than Utilization gains. Usually trains qualities in isolation, so looks less sport specific. Meant to improve future potential and may even reduce near-term performance. Analogy: building the Interstate highway system: tedious, may slow traffic, but once completed lets much more traffic (work) flow faster.

### [TUA-C04-014] Utilization Training: types and characteristics

Increases performance outcomes in the short term (racing or race-specific workouts); maximally utilizes current capacities. Analogy: dumping fast traffic on the Interstate; if highway still under construction, work done is limited by road capacity. Quicker acting than Capacity Training; for maximum effect sport/event specific; intensity usually mimics the event.
- Aerobic Utilization: also called endurance training, LT training, or VO2 max training (Zones 3, 4, 5 in a conventional zone system). Increases maximum sustained output. Especially important for events under two hours where speeds are at/above LT. For events over three hours, Aerobic Capacity and Aerobic Utilization training are essentially the same because most of the event is below LT, nearer AeT.
- Anaerobic Utilization: increases the fraction of Anaerobic Capacity sustainable in competition. Typically training at speeds 5–10 percent faster than competition speeds, or workload significantly greater than the event, for total duration significantly less than the competition distance. Strong effect on muscular endurance capacity.
Notes: Utilization Training reduces the athlete's capacity, so it must be offset with Capacity Training during all phases of the training cycle. Its effects are dynamic, volatile, short lived; back-to-back awesome and horrible races or inability to string together a season of good races may signal too much Utilization versus Capacity Training. Needed for personal best, but not optimal if capacity is small. Athletes with large capacity can and must do much more Utilization Training, which explains why elites do more/harder training than novices and why copying elites is a common mistake.

### [TUA-C04-015] Fatigue as stimulus; three types of fatigue

- Endurance athletes must embrace fatigue: we train to increase resistance to it and race to see how well we tolerate it. Fatigue is the stimulus that makes you fitter. Staying on the good side of the fatigue curve is one of the most important factors in training. If load rises faster than the body can adapt, fatigue turns from friend to enemy.
- Three general types, which overlap and interact:
1. Calcium accumulation: calcium ions accumulate in muscle cells and inhibit contraction. The calcium pump fails to keep up and the muscle loses its ability to contract. Most noticeable in short, high-strength contractions (e.g. pull-ups), under 15 seconds. Recovery takes a few minutes. It is self-limiting. If driven into this state many times in a workout (e.g. several sets of pull-ups to failure), the other forms can be induced and recovery can take days.
2. Metabolic: short time scale - metabolic products (lactate; hydrogen-ion acidity) accumulate and inhibit ATP synthesis, causing a loss of muscle power and a slowing (e.g. a 60-second near-maximal hill sprint, with a burning feeling). Recovery takes a few minutes. Longer time scale - glycogen stores become depleted and power drops (the classic "bonk" in multi-hour events, with mental confusion and lack of coordination). Recovery takes a few hours to a few days.
3. Neurologic: neurotransmitters needed at each synapse become depleted through prolonged high firing rates, so the impulse weakens and power drops. Repeated or longer exhausting efforts of types 1 and 2 can cause it. Recovery takes a few hours to several days.

### [TUA-C04-016] The Central Governor (Noakes) and fatigue

- A.V. Hill (early 20th century) proposed the VO2 max theory, later modified and strengthened.
- Dr. Tim Noakes: the brain has a built-in self-preservation instinct that limits how far from homeostasis we can go before alarm bells sound.
- Whatever the cause of the fatigue type, the result is the same: the brain reduces muscle power output until homeostasis is restored. The authors call this the most robust theory of fatigue to date.
- Implication: frequently pushing the body out of its comfort zone (away from homeostasis) makes the governor more comfortable with ever-greater divergence before it shuts things down. This is what training does. The muscle-brain relationship is still poorly understood.

### [TUA-C04-017] Overtraining Syndrome (OTS): nature and risk

- Elite athletes balance close to the edge between supreme fitness and collapse; the higher the level, the narrower the edge. Too little stress misses potential by a few percent; too much risks falling off the overtraining cliff, missing it by a huge margin. Life stresses (family, school, job) add to load and must not be ignored.
- Overtraining is a medical issue. In its simplest form it shows as injury; insidiously, the body fails to adapt: you become less fit while training the same or more. It causes more failed goals and shortened careers than anything except injury; some stagnate for years.
- Endurance athletes are at highest risk because of high energy demands (can exceed 6,000 kcal/day in very heavy training). Athletes seldom recognize it intuitively; diagnosis often comes too late for anything but extreme measures.
- Overreaching (short-term stiffness, soreness, extra days to recover after a hard race) is normal in the training cycle. When chronic overreaching becomes overtraining, a physiological bridge has been crossed that is hard to recross without drastic action.
- Fig. 4.1 Overtraining: repeated sessions without sufficient recovery lead to a long-term decline in fitness (each session dips into fatigue, recovery is incomplete and never reaches supercompensation, peaks and troughs trend downward). Recovery can take months if not recognized and corrected early.

### [TUA-C04-019] Interval training: history and what it depends on

Gerschler (German track coach, late 1930s) coined interval training: high-intensity bouts separated by rest. His theory: stroke volume improvements occur during the rest intervals; hence Zone 4 intervals are called VO2 max training (stroke volume is the main limiter to VO2 max). Breaking the distance into short bouts allows more high-intensity volume than one continuous bout. Basic principle: go fast, recover, repeat. Many variations exist; the answer is "it depends" on desired training effect, athlete background, and timing in the training cycle.
Intervals yield best results on a solid aerobic base (high AeT) with a high proportion of purely aerobic training; not a shortcut nor stand-alone method.

### [TUA-C04-021] Why anaerobic training matters: economy and trainability

Anaerobic capacity work is really very specific strength training; improving it improves economy (lower energy cost at any speed), which matters over multihour events. It is a tiny but important part of training. Progress is slow; response varies greatly because speed/power is largely genetic ("Sprinters are born and distance runners are made"). Raising anaerobic capacity is possible with judicious training over several years. It improves uphill speed complementary to endurance.
Principle: rests must be long enough for near full restoration so each rep is at maximum intensity; work periods 8-15 seconds with at least 1-2 minutes recovery; rest:work ratio at least 4:1 or 5:1 up to 10:1. If rests are too short, high-power fibers (poor endurance) drop out of recruitment, endurance-trained motor units take over, reps slow and benefit falls.

### [TUA-C04-022] Strength vs endurance: neurologic vs metabolic quality

- Endurance sports use up to only about 25 percent of an athlete's maximum single-repetition strength, but for up to many thousands of repetitions.
- Nonetheless, increasing max strength seems to have a positive effect on endurance. Few studies were done on mountain endurance athletes; assumption: strength plays a big role where the body is lifted up and lowered over many thousands of meters.
- If more strength were always better, Olympic weightlifters would be the fastest marathoners; they are not. Reason: strength is largely a neurologic quality (brain's ability to recruit the greatest number of muscle fibers); endurance is largely a metabolic quality (rate of metabolic turnover of ATP). They overlap and are interdependent.
- Among similarly performing endurance athletes, the stronger tend to perform better (e.g. marathoners 2:10-2:15). Being able to deadlift more does not guarantee faster running, but deadlifting more than similar-speed runners suggests running faster.
- Sport-specific strength correlates better with performance than general strength: standing broad jump, single-legged hop and 30-meter sprint are decent predictors of 5K times among runners of similar times.
- How much strength is sufficient: "No one really knows."

### [TUA-C04-023] Frontier Fiber Model and local muscular endurance limit

- Leg fatigue slowing is caused by the fast-twitch fibers that allow the high speed dropping out of the recruited pool. Each speed increase requires recruiting more powerful, less enduring fibers.
- The endurance of these frontier fibers largely limits you. Zone 3-4 workouts traditionally recruit them and develop their endurance. If they fatigue too fast and drop out, they no longer get the training effect.
- Sign: you slow and heart rate drops despite increased effort; legs give out before breathing. Then you are limited by local endurance of working muscles and should add ME training.
- Effectiveness of any high-intensity session depends on adequate ME (Kílian has very high local ME, allowing him to sustain intensities for the full workout).

### [TUA-C04-024] Why strength helps economy and speed

- Sufficient (especially sufficient specific) strength improves movement economy: longer strides with less effort, since a smaller percentage of max strength propels the body farther per stride. For ultrarunners, decrease of stride length during races is one of the most powerful predictors of results.
- ME training can greatly improve resistance to late-race neuromuscular fatigue from pounding downhills.
- Speed of movement relates directly to strength in that movement; overcoming gravity uphill and resisting it downhill requires strength. Too much strength hurts if it adds mass and lowers strength-to-weight ratio. ME is more important than pure strength for all endurance events. Ways to develop it: specific ME, circuit-style workouts, uphill Zone 3 intervals, long runs on hilly terrain.
- Uphill is where the greatest speed difference between elite and amateur appears; improving uphill power/ME directly benefits performance.

### [TUA-C04-025] Core strength: role and transverse abdominis

- Core stabilizes pelvis, spine, shoulders so arms and legs propel effectively; pelvis supports body weight, anchors legs, is base for spine. Many running injuries trace to core weakness/imbalance (muscles attach to pelvis). Often overlooked by endurance athletes.
- Core muscles are mainly slow-twitch: lots of endurance, typically not much strength, so a little strength gain can matter a lot.
- Arm swing counterrotates shoulders relative to hips, twisting the spine; a weak core fails to transfer this energy and loses power.
- Transverse abdominis (TA): deepest core muscle, corset-like around visceral organs, ties pelvis and lower six ribs in front with thoracolumbar fascia; not used for movement, purely stabilization; hard to isolate. Slow progress is normal.
- Poor pelvic stability signs: back swaying, cannot hold small of back flat during leg lifts.
- Activation: lie supine, knees up, feet on floor; fingers on front hip bones (ilia), move inward one inch (given as 25 centimeters in source [Likely unit/typo error in source — recorded as printed]) and down one inch. Pull up through pelvic floor (as stopping urine flow) and feel muscle engage. Then isolate TA by drawing navel to spine or imagining zipping a tight jacket. Practice sitting/standing. If spine flexes like a crunch, you are trying too hard and recruiting rectus abdominis/obliques. Then move to plank (isometric, hold until shaking or loss of form); may start from knees.

### [TUA-C05-006] Aerobic Deficiency Syndrome (ADS)

### [TUA-C05-007] Aerobic Metabolism

### [TUA-C05-008] Aerobic Threshold (AeT)

The upper-most intensity of exercise where the production of ATP begins to be dominated by glycolysis rather than by the oxidation of fats. At this point, blood lactate begins to rise above the resting level. By convention, a blood-lactate concentration of about 2mmol/L is indicative of AeT. Another marker is the depth and pace of ventilation. When conversation can't be maintained at a normal cadence, the First Ventilatory Threshold (VT1) has been reached. VT1 corresponds closely to the AeT. It marks the upper level of the most important training zone for developing aerobic capacity, the cornerstone to all endurance activities lasting over two minutes. The marker is very trainable and will move higher as measured by both speed of movement and heart rate. Top endurance athletes can have an AeT that is within 10 percent (and less) of their lactate (or anaerobic) threshold.

### [TUA-C05-039] Lactate Balance Point

The point where lactate production is equal to lactate removal. ### [TUA-C05-040] Lactate Shuttle

A mechanism, identified by George Brooks, a professor of integrative biology, in the early 1980s, where lactate is moved out of the muscle cell where it is being produced and into slow-twitch muscle cells where it can enter the aerobic metabolic pathway to be used as fuel in the synthesis of ATP. This explains the importance of the Aerobic Base in supporting high-intensity training. The greater the aerobic capacity of the ST fibers to take up and utilize lactate, the more and longer high intensity can be sustained.

### [TUA-C05-041] Lactate Threshold (LT)

The maximum exercise intensity at which blood lactate removal can keep pace with lactate production, remaining elevated but stable for up to one hour. Corresponds to the Second Ventilatory Threshold (VT2), where breathing becomes noticeably deeper and more rapid, rendering conversation impossible (only a few words between breaths). ### [TUA-C05-042] Lactate Threshold (LT) and its synonyms

Lactic Threshold, Anaerobic Threshold, Maximum Lactate Steady State, and Functional Threshold are names for essentially the same metabolic event: where lactate production exceeds its rate of removal; beyond it exercise can be sustained only a few minutes before fatigue slows you. Footnote list: Anaerobic Threshold, Functional Threshold Power or Pace, Critical Pace, Critical Speed, Critical Power, Second Ventilatory Threshold; their meaning is disputed scientifically but lumped as the maximum pace sustainable for an extended (30–60 minutes) duration. Lactate can serve as a marker of intensity because it accumulates in lockstep with pyruvate.

### [TUA-C05-043] Lactate, Lactic Acid

A chemical product of glycolytic metabolism in muscle cells. It is immediately dissociated into lactate and a hydrogen ion (H+). Lactate has two main pathways to be used as fuel: 1) converted to pyruvate, which can enter the Krebs cycle of aerobic metabolism; 2) converted to glucose in the liver by gluconeogenesis. The release of hydrogen ions can lower the blood's pH (increase acidity). If this continues without adequate buffering, it results in a burning sensation in the muscles and a forced slowing of the pace.

### [TUA-C05-045] Local Muscular Endurance / Muscular Endurance (ME)

Training relatively small muscle groups for endurance without imposing a large load on the cardiovascular system, by making the muscular load high through added resistance. The aerobic capabilities of the high-power muscle fibers responsible for the movement become the limit, not the cardiovascular system's oxygen supply. Fatigue is localized to that small group of muscles. ME: the ability to do repeated muscular contractions of the same kind against a resistance; limited by localized muscular fatigue rather than cardiovascular endurance. In events such as a VK, performance is largely determined by local muscular fatigue, so maximal sustainable output is linked directly to local muscular endurance in the primary propelling muscle groups.

### [TUA-C06-002] Three metabolic states by speed and their zones

1) Low speed: mostly ST fibers; modest ATP rate; much ATP from fat (no lactate); ST fibers take up pyruvate aerobically; any lactate used in heart, liver, adjacent active ST muscle. Below aerobic threshold (AeT). = Zones 1 and 2 of conventional five-zone system.
2) Medium speed: some FT fibers recruited (more force, less fatigue resistant, fewer mitochondria, rely on glycolysis, more pyruvate/lactate). Sustainable while accumulation is less than removal (by ST, heart, liver). Comfortably hard pace sustainable many minutes up to one hour. = Zone 3.
3) High speed: past the Lactate Threshold (LT), pyruvate/lactate production exceeds removal; acidity disrupts homeostasis; glycolytic ATP slows; pace must slow after a few minutes above LT (consciously or involuntarily). = Zones 4 and 5.
These three states should form the basis of every zone system grading intensity. ### [TUA-C06-006] Zone 1: Aerobic Conditioning

Base for aerobic development; foundation for all higher intensity; undervaluing it undermines aerobic development. Benefits: increased blood volume, mitochondrial mass, capillary density and aerobic enzymes in working muscles; long workouts improve fat metabolism; early in a running program prepares body for impact loading (injury prevention). Blood lactate: less than 1 to about 1.5 mMol/L. HR at top of Z1 about 10 percent below AeT (top of Z2). Duration minimum 30 minutes continuous, up to several hours; aids technique and economy training. Should account for 50–70 percent of annual aerobic training volume for all mountain athletes.

### [TUA-C06-007] Zone 2: Aerobic Capacity

Requires maximum output of fat/oxidative metabolism. Greatest effect in raising AeT, a key benchmark; for all but elites possibly the most trainable aerobic quality. Raising AeT supports maximal improvement of speed at LT. Benefits like Z1 but to a larger pool of muscle fibers (more power, more recruitment). Continuous mode, usually 30–120 minutes. Used mainly in the base phase; smaller role once higher-intensity training starts as competitions near.
Beginners and those with Aerobic Deficiency Syndrome can do almost all aerobic base in Z2 (skip Z1) since pace is slow enough not to beat up muscles. Athletes with high AeT and fast pace can't do much Z2 without risking overtraining: do more in Z1 and Recovery; Z2 limited to one or two days per week, with total Z2 volume less than 10 to 15 percent of weekly volume.

### [TUA-C06-008] Zone 3: Endurance

Aerobic but different from Z1–2: higher ATP turnover; glycolysis becomes dominant source in ST and recruited FT fibers; lactate rises but remains sustainable up to an hour in well-trained athletes with no performance degradation. Both aerobic and glycolytic systems get a hefty stimulus; in less trained it has a strong Muscular Endurance effect (limited by aerobic capacity of FT fibers). Upper end is the LT; beyond it the athlete is on borrowed time.
Caution: many see Z3 as the magic one-stop intensity; overuse puts you in Utilization Training with its pitfalls. Seductive because it gives an immediate boost. Should typically be no more than 10 percent of total annual training volume. For those with insufficient aerobic base (per Ten Percent Test), overuse can easily lead to performance decline.

### [TUA-C06-009] Zone 4: Aerobic Power

Intensity above LT; maximally utilizes aerobic and glycolytic metabolism and lactate removal (shuttle). Necessary in all endurance programs. Rapid gains, noticeable workout to workout, seductive (see caution about Utilization). Strong muscular endurance effect; for the less trained, duration of Z4 and Z5 workouts is limited by local muscular endurance. Sign: legs screaming but HR tops out at Zone 3 levels — then you are not getting the intended Z4 benefit; improve muscular endurance first (see Strength Section).

### [TUA-C06-010] Zone 5: Maximum Anaerobic Effort

Maximum intensity, sustainable less than thirty to forty-five seconds. HR is not a good measure (cardiac lag). Requires recruitment of the bulk of available motor units; taxes glycolytic and neuromuscular systems; stimulus for sport-specific strength and technique; best way to develop anaerobic power endurance (e.g. skimo sprint). Can and should be included in most training periods. Early in the cycle: entirely ten-second, very powerful sprints, usually up a steep hill; duration extended later for endurance effect.
Always interval fashion with very long rests to replenish ATP; three minutes is sufficient to restore most ATP. If power drops significantly after a few reps and longer rests don't help, end the workout (FT motor units fatigued, job done). Mentally tough for athletes who think resting is counterproductive.
Downside: potential injury from high loads; a good strength base prepares you.

### [TUA-C06-012] Ferritin reference values for endurance athletes

- Ferritin stores and transports iron across the cell membrane into hemoglobin; elemental iron cannot cross that barrier without it. Low ferritin reduces blood oxygen-carrying capacity and so endurance performance.
- Standard "low" reading: 12 ng/ml. Normal ranges do not consider athletes' demands and cannot be relied upon.
- Endurance athletes: below 50 ng/ml = some reduced endurance performance. Below 30 ng/ml = dramatically reduced performance and chronic fatigue.
- Worth checking with a simple blood test if symptoms are present; often overlooked by physicians not used to working with athletes.

## C07 Testing & Assessment

### [TUA-C07-001] The Ten Percent Test (AeT–LT spread)

Purpose: judge whether aerobic capacity is sufficient for Zone 3 work ("when the vacuum cleaner is big and powerful"). Sufficient when AeT is within 10 percent of LT by HR or pace (elite athletes can have a Z3 spread of 6–7 percent or only 10 beats).
Procedure: determine AeT using methods on pages 152–155 (AeT Testing), then do the LT test (page 155). Percentage difference = higher HR divided by lower HR (unconventional, but works for purpose).
Example: AeT HR 128 (lab test), LT hill-climb test average HR 150. 150/128 = 1.17 → LT HR is 17 percent greater than AeT HR. Still lots of potential to improve aerobic base with Z1–2; not eager to add Z3 or higher.
Interpretation: more than 10 percent spread = aerobic deficiency, build more aerobic base. Less than 10 percent spread: reduce or even drop Z2 training, substituting Z3 workouts.

### [TUA-C07-002] Step Test (recovery state)

- Purpose: gauge recovery state / preparedness to train by lightly stressing the nervous system.
- Procedure: wearing a heart rate monitor, step up and down at a moderate pace onto a stair step for a minute or two, fast enough to raise HR about 50 to 70 beats/minute. The effort need not be intense, just enough to cause a sympathetic reaction. Note the maximum HR achieved, sit down immediately and note HR after one minute.
- Exact times and stepping rate are not critical provided they are repeated identically each test.
- Interpretation: repeat frequently and note the quality of the corresponding training; you will see a correlation between the drop in heart rate and recovery state.

### [TUA-C07-004] AeT testing 2: Ventilatory marker

In hundreds of field tests with moderate-to-well-trained endurance athletes, the heart rate that can be maintained while breathing only through the nose, or while carrying on a conversation, correlates well with blood lactate of 2 mMol/L (the commonly accepted measure of AeT).
Caveat: works only for those with good aerobic training backgrounds. ### [TUA-C07-005] AeT testing 3: Lactate step test

- Setup: treadmill at 5 percent gradient, or outside on a moderate hill (walk back down between stages).
- Warm-up: 15 minutes so the aerobic system is fully online.
- Stages: series of 3-minute stages at increasing speeds, each just enough to raise HR about 10 bpm. Get HR to stabilize as soon as possible at each stage and hold it steady.
- At the end of each stage take a small blood sample and measure with a portable lactate analyzer.
- Start at an apparently unnecessarily low HR (probably walking) and increase 10 beats per stage, sneaking up on AeT.
- AeT heart rate = HR when lactate reaches or exceeds 2 mMol/L, or the first stage where lactate rises by 1 mMol/L.
- Easy to self-administer or with a friend; a personal lactate meter costs less than lab tests (authors use Lactate Plus).

### [TUA-C07-006] AeT testing 4: Gas exchange test (lab)

- Done in a lab with expensive equipment and technicians; cost $150-$350; the gold standard for metabolic response. Gives AeT and LT in HR and pace (on a treadmill) and, if taken to failure, VO2 max. Going to failure is unnecessary for finding the two thresholds.
- Many labs give a graph of fat versus carb usage at various intensities.
- Most actionable result: HR at AeT and its position relative to LT. If AeT is more than 10 percent below LT in HR, considerable gains in raising AeT are still possible.
Caveat: many labs are unfamiliar with AeT and will administer a VO2 max test, reporting LT along the way. VO2 max protocols often use short stages, rushing through the aerobic zone so metabolism never stabilizes. To determine aerobic response you need exercise stages of at least three minutes.

### [TUA-C07-007] Lactate (Anaerobic) Threshold field test

- Effort: maximum sustained uphill effort of 30 to 60 minutes (treadmill is second choice). Less well trained: 30 minutes (otherwise muscle fatigue limits sustained hard effort). Well-conditioned athletes can sustain up to an hour.
- Warm-up at least 15-20 minutes; finish with 2-3 minutes of Zone 3 running or skiing.
- Do not test with tired legs; reduce training load a few days beforehand.
- Start the recording HR monitor at test start. Quickly settle into a maximum but sustainable pace for the full test. HR settles into a narrow range that feels comfortably hard; speeding up even 2-3 bpm forces slowing soon. Hold that sweet spot.
- Result: average HR for the test = LT intensity (top of Zone 3).

### [TUA-C07-008] Lower-body knee-tracking strength assessments (overview)

- Purpose: assess knee/hip stability before a strength program. Weak hip muscles, specifically gluteus medius, are the culprit in many running overuse injuries to knee and lower leg.
- Monitor: knee stays lined up over the toes viewed from the front (mirror or look down). Knee moving inward (toward midline) while bending or straightening = alignment issue likely from hip stability/strength deficiency. Knee side-to-side movement on one leg suggests weakness.
- Usually fixed by lateral hip strengthening; sometimes a professional strength coach is needed. Tests are not a substitute for professional advice.
- Tests go in order of difficulty: Air Squat (easy), Box Step (medium), Sit-to-Stand (difficult). Stop at the first test failed and use the corresponding stage.
- If after three weeks of the strength programs knee tracking/control does not markedly improve, consult a physiotherapist for an underlying structural issue. Move to the next stage when appropriate knee control is achieved.
- Some athletes are best served by unglamorous remedial strength work, just as some need slow remedial running for ADS.

src: Ch 7
evidence: author-experience

### [TUA-C07-009] Air Squat Test (easy)

- Position: feet shoulder width, toes pointed out slightly, arms held out horizontally at shoulder height. Squat bringing butt between heels as low as possible with neutral spine (not flexed or hyperextended); thighs at least parallel (Fig 7.1). Watch knees going down and up.
- Questions: Do knees collapse in at all? Can you hold knees outward, vertically aligned over toes?
- Result: cannot maintain alignment over toes -> stop, start Stage 1 leg/hip program. Can hold with conscious effort -> go to Box Step Test.

### [TUA-C07-010] Box Step Test (medium)

- Stand facing box/step just below knee height, toes no more than 12 inches (30 cm) from box. Step up watching for inward knee collapse, then step down. Repeat several times each leg.
- Observe how far each knee moves toward midline and whether hips shift outward as knee collapses.
- Result: knee collapses inward by more than about 1 inch (2.5 cm) and/or hips shift outward a similar amount -> stop, use Stage 2. If alignment held -> Sit-to-Stand Test.

### [TUA-C07-011] Sit-to-Stand Test (difficult)

- Stand in front of, facing away from, a hard chair or preferably bench so you sit with knees at 90 degrees, thighs parallel to ground. Lower to sitting; without relaxing leg muscles immediately stand using only one leg. Do not hinge upper body forward; keep torso fairly upright. Note knee tracking over toes of the standing foot.
- If impossible for strength, add a thick book or two to the seat so you can just barely rise on one leg, or use the box step test.
- Result: either knee collapses inward more than 1 inch (2.5 cm) (or hips move outward) -> Stage 3 strength program. Good knee tracking -> move directly to the specific strength program.

### [TUA-C07-012] Push-Up Assessment (upper body)

- Plank position on hands and toes, neutral spine (no sag or inverted V), hands below shoulders.
- Cannot hold top position 5 s without sagging/raising hips -> hands on chair/bench. If can hold 5 s there, rest two minutes and count push-ups. If cannot hold, hands on countertop (about one meter high) and try at least one inclined push-up from toes.
- Can do only inclined push-ups -> Stage 1 Upper-Body Program.
- Can hold normal top position more than 5 s; rest 2 min, count push-ups without losing form: fewer than five -> Stage 2; more than five -> Stage 3.

### [TUA-C07-013] Pull-Up Assessment for Skimo

- Use pull-up (palms away) or chin-up (palms toward) grip; hang and pull until chin just above bar. Start with slightly bent elbows and shoulder blades pulled down (retracted); not a fully extended hang (loose extended shoulders may injure weak shoulders, Fig 7.9).
- Cannot hang at bottom with shoulders retracted -> Stage 1.
- Can hang: hold chin above bar (use stool or friend to get there) as long as possible: 2 s or less -> Stage 1; 3 s or more -> Stage 2.
- One to four pull-ups -> Stage 3.
- Five or more pull-ups -> Specific Strength Training Methods for skimo (Ch 8).

### [TUA-C07-014] Core Strength Assessment: Plank and Windshield Wiper

- PLANK (tests TA): weight on toes and forearms, elbows under shoulders, neutral spine. Time hold until perfect form lost (stop when back sags or spine not straight). Stage 1: less than thirty seconds; Stage 2: thirty seconds to one minute; Stage 3: longer than one minute.
- WINDSHIELD WIPER (tests obliques): on back, arms outstretched from shoulders, palms on floor; flex hips so feet together, toes to ceiling; slowly rotate hips lowering feet to one side with knees straight and feet locked; resist shoulder rotation by pushing down hand on that side; lightly touch floor with side of lower foot; back to twelve o'clock and over to other side; returning to twelve o'clock = one rep. Slow, controlled. If knees cannot stay straight/legs together, bend knees and do same rotation. Stage 1: zero reps with straight legs; Stage 2: fewer than five reps each side with straight legs; Stage 3: more than five reps each side with straight legs. When you can do ten reps each side, add boots or ankle weights (Fig 7.18).

### [TUA-C08-002] Antiglycolytic (aerobic base) training method

- Duration is the single biggest stimulus from any exercise; adaptation grows with amount of training within ability to absorb it.
- One of the strongest stimuli for raising aerobic ATP recycling is glycogen depletion in ST muscles, best achieved by long-duration, low- to moderate-intensity sessions (long durations needed to deplete already well-adapted ST fibers). Short high-intensity work depletes FT glycogen but is too short to train ST fibers. Hence endurance training traditionally contains much easy volume.
- To reduce lactate production (more fat use, more aerobic power) do a lot of long-duration, low-intensity work: building an aerobic base. An athlete with a small aerobic base can never fully maximize endurance regardless of high-intensity work.
- Philosophy: you will never maximize endurance potential without first maximizing basic aerobic capacity (AeT). Approach with diligence, not ferocity.

Principles of all successful programs, to avoid gross errors. Training puts systems into a crisis state; adaptations occur at varying rates. Failure to adapt (stagnation) or worse overtraining (diminishing fitness) results when ignored. Reapplying a similar stress won't help if structural and functional systems haven't adapted to the current load.

### [TUA-C08-004] Continuity

Maintain a regular schedule with minimal interruption; some flexibility is needed. Lapses must be managed, not overlooked: if a week is missed (work, travel, sickness) you can't pretend you did it and advance; the body is unprepared and setbacks likely. Occasional breaks of a day or two are fine as long as they don't reduce training load by more than roughly 5 percent in a month. Frequent or prolonged breaks exceeding 5 percent of monthly volume require taking a step back in the plan.

### [TUA-C08-005] Gradualness and annual volume progression

Training load must gradually become larger than before; as fitness rises, what was too much becomes not enough. Body adapts well to frequent gentle nudges, not infrequent bludgeoning; sporadic bursts of enthusiasm are common among beginners and can be disastrous. Once adapted, the load no longer causes adaptation; change volume and/or intensity for the next cycle. Aerobic capacity: mostly increase volume. Simultaneous adaptation of different qualities (anaerobic capacity and speed; aerobic and muscular endurance) needs more elaborate methods with care.
Annual volume rules (by time): beginners (under 350–400 hours/year) can increase by as much as 25 percent per year. Advanced athletes (more than 500 hours/year): 10 percent yearly jump is the maximum. Elite athletes often make no change in annual volume, instead juggling type/amount of higher-intensity and specific training.
Kílian: increased about 10 percent more hours per year; for last eight years held 1,000–1,300 hours/year; to reach such numbers increase no more than 10 percent a year for several years. Some world-class skiers/mountaineers train 600 hours/year, sufficient for shorter races; really long races need higher volumes. Started at 600 hours in the first year of serious training at age 13.

### [TUA-C08-006] Modulation and overreaching

Modulation: fluctuating training load that lets the body recover homeostasis after build-up; stress targeted systems, then allow adaptation before the new load. Cycles range from hours to weeks; with care several systems can adapt simultaneously. Record daily perceptions before/during/after training in a log.
Overreaching: extreme modulation sometimes used by advanced athletes: a short planned period of very high load, preceded by a slightly below average week and followed by substantial recovery. Examples: two massive back-to-back weekend workouts followed by three or four light days; or a week in a hut with long days and a couple of interval workouts, possibly needing a full week to recover.

### [TUA-C08-007] Planned breaks in training

- If a break (business trip, family visit) can be anticipated, boost the load for a short period (say a week) going into it, then use the break as recovery with a few short, easy workouts. Such a break after an overload period can lift you to a higher level in following days and weeks.
- Build it into the plan a few weeks ahead so you can build into the hard week; this removes the anxiety of missing training, and the break is used to rebuild before the next block.

### [TUA-C08-008] Periodization: macro/meso/microcycles

- Periodization (originating in the former USSR, popularized in the West through Soviet sports scientists): training in discrete blocks with distinct aims, focusing on only a few physical qualities at a time; athletes may achieve better results. The Capacity vs Utilization distinction is a form of periodization.
- Some successful athletes maintain all qualities year-round, but this needs considerable self-awareness and an extensive training history. The book lays out a basic periodized plan for everyone else; it is open to interpretation.
- Macrocycle: training for a goal many months away (annual or seasonal). Divided into mesocycles (a few weeks to a few months; distinct training targets; each stands on the previous and supports the next). Mesocycles are divided into microcycles, typically seven days, in which daily prescriptions develop the mesocycle's qualities.
- Previously built qualities must not diminish much; it takes much less stimulus to maintain a capacity than to build it.

### [TUA-C08-009] Periods: Transition, Base, Precompetition, Competition

- Transition Period: prepares the athlete after a break following a major event/full season, or when new to regimented training. Length depends on break length, carried-over fatigue and athletic maturity: as short as two weeks for an old hand after a very successful season, up to eight weeks for a newcomer or very worn-down veteran.
- Base Period: most crucial work; increase work capacity of each fundamental quality needed for the event. Must be rock-solid foundation. Fitness increases but is masked by low fatigue carried day to day as an essential part of adaptation; do not expect best performances in training or competition.
- Precompetition Period: training mirrors the event's specific demands, combining the fundamental qualities; measured in weeks, not months. Capacity maintenance and recovery workouts fit between demanding Utilization sessions. Fatigue is still high, but recovery before/after key workouts is better. Examples: preparation for a VK: 8x3 min uphill in Zone 4 with 4-min easy walk-down recovery; preparation for a 100-mile race: back-to-back eight-to-ten-hour days in Zones 1 and 2, three to four weeks out.
- Competition Period: spend the "bank account" of work capacity; cycles of competitions followed by recovery, interspersed with capacity-rebuilding blocks in long inter-event gaps. With a long busy season, set aside a training block with at least two weeks between shorter races and four to six weeks between ultradistance events for recovery and rebuilding. Building fitness needs an extended period of chronic fatigue; attempting it in the competition period without a few free weeks has little benefit and can hurt results. With 2-3 weeks between races you can return to maintenance-level base training, but after recovery and taper three weeks may allow only ten days of capacity-building training.

### [TUA-C08-010] Getting started: aerobic base with up to 90 percent at or below AeT

- Up to 90 percent of endurance training is at or below personal AeT. For many athletes Zones 1-2 pace will be excruciatingly slow; the AeT pace is slow because aerobic capacity is low (ATP output supports only a slow pace). The only way to increase aerobic-level speed is to train at and below AeT.
- With ADS, initially almost all aerobic training can be at or near AeT (top of Zone 2) because the pace is slow enough not to tax muscles. Main problem is going slow enough; training right at LT is nearly an epidemic. Needs discipline; no shortcut.
- Low intensity does not mean slow in well-conditioned athletes.
- Build the base as well as time constraints allow (annual plan and daily schedule). When in doubt, go long and easy.
- Recheck every ~2 weeks with an AeT time trial: note time over the same course at the same HR; expect encouraging drops. Can be tossed into a regular workout.
- When AeT-to-LT gap closes to within 10 percent, reduce Zone 2 and do more Recovery and Zone 1 workouts; you are then ready to introduce Zone 3 and Zone 4. Do not feel you lose benefit by not running at your aerobic limit.

### [TUA-C08-011] Endurance training methods: balance and continuous training

Balance of long low-to-moderate work and short high-intensity work is art more than science; depends on event (specificity) and individual (individuality). Shorter events and more seasoned athletes need more high intensity.
Two methods: continuous and intermittent (interval).
Continuous training = steady state lasting a minimum of 30 minutes up to several hours; accumulates Zone 1 and Zone 2 hours that make up about 80 to 90 percent of total annual training volume. Basic = base of support for all other training, not skippable. Even a 30-minute morning jog helps maintenance. Continuity: these workouts must be frequent and regular.

### [TUA-C08-012] Aerobic vs anaerobic intervals: definitions and cautions

- Aerobic intervals: maximally stress aerobic system capacity (carb and fat fueled); work periods in Zones 3 and 4. Predominate interval volume.
- Anaerobic intervals: develop anaerobic capacity and specific strength; Zone 5; must recruit highest-power (FT) fibers, which fatigue quickly and need long recovery. Be well rested, take long rests, never with tired legs. Used sparingly for events in this book.
- Interval sessions are hard but should not drive you to near system failure; reaching far beyond current capacity may require extra recovery or days off, losing continuity.
- Kílian: high-intensity training falls into VO2 max/Z4, muscular endurance/Z3, and strength such as hill sprints.

### [TUA-C08-014] Leg and Hip Programs Stage 1-3 (progression rules)

- Stage 1: teaches the brain to turn on lateral gluteal groups then builds strength. High reps, low resistance: four sets of twenty reps or until fatigue; one-minute rest between sets. Between sets insert a set of upper-body exercises. Exercises: squats with knee band (Fig 7.4: rubber band just above knees), single leg lateral band (Fig 7.5: band around knees above or below, balanced on one leg, extend unweighted leg to side and back at about 45 degrees). Retest knee alignment after a couple of weeks; move to Stage 2 when hip stability gained.
- Stage 2: bodyweight to learn form: three sets of fifteen reps each leg, one to two minutes rest. Progress by adding reps each session to fatigue. Insert upper-body set between sets. When three sets of thirty reps with perfect knee control and balance -> Stage 3. Exercises: box step-up, box step-down, front lunge.
- Stage 3: add weight (vest, pack or barbell on shoulders) to Stage 2 exercises; choose resistance so you can just manage eight reps with perfect form; do four sets of six reps with that weight; two-minute rest between sets of the same exercise; upper-body set inserted between sets.

### [TUA-C08-015] Upper-Body Programs Stage 1-3 (runners and skiers)

Skimo context: upper body probably contributes 5-10 percent of propulsion in skimo uphill (up to 20 percent in classic diagonal stride XC skiing). Arm swing in running transfers energy to legs; endurance of back/shoulders to maintain arm swing for hours matters more than high strength.
Stage 1:
- Runners: inclined push-ups at the incline from assessment; three sets of as many as possible, 2-3 min rest between sets (leg exercise inserted). When more than eight in third set, move to lower surface; repeat; keep decreasing incline.
- Skiers: assisted pull-ups or lat pull-down; three sets of max reps with 2-3 min rest; when more than eight reps in third set, increase weight or reduce assistance. Assisted pull-up: find assistance/weight you can barely manage for six reps; use for prescribed sets/reps; after six such workouts try Stage 2.
Stage 2:
- Runners: knee push-ups (weight between hands and knees); three sets of as many as possible, 2 min rest (leg exercise inserted). At three sets of eight, move to regular push-ups: do as many good-form standard as possible each set, then switch to knee push-ups to complete up to eight reps. If more than three sets of eight standard, go to Stage 3.
- Skiers: get chin above bar using assistance, remove assistance and hold top position as long as possible; drop, rest 2 min (leg exercise), repeat four times. When top hold exceeds a count of five seconds in the fourth set, use the middle position with elbows bent at 90 degrees and repeat sequence (Fig 7.13 isometric bar hang).
Stage 3:
- Runners: start with at least one set of eight regular push-ups, then three sets with added resistance, limit four reps per set; 2-3 min rest (leg exercises). Add resistance by elevating feet (stair, bench, chair, exercise ball) or weight vest. Once you can manage fifteen push-ups consider elevated-feet variations (Fig 7.14); weight vest or plate best (Fig 7.15).
- Skiers: four sets of two to three reps with a 3-5 second slow lower on the last rep of each set; add weight so only 2-3 reps possible. When you can do five normal pull-ups/chin-ups, progress to Specific Strength Training Methods in Ch 8.

### [TUA-C08-016] Muscular endurance training: principles and cautions

- ME is one of the major components determining endurance performance; benefits felt quickly and dramatically, so it is tempting to overuse. Do a high volume of Zone 1 aerobic training for maintenance while doing ME work.
- Best results for athletes with high strength and aerobic capacity. Correct basic strength deficiencies (Ch 7) before specific methods.
- Common mechanism: extra resistance (added weight or very steep hill) beyond event loads forces local muscular endurance, not cardiovascular fitness, to be the limit.
- Need for ME? Slowing in workouts under two hours when glycogen should not run out; heavy dead legs losing bounce in long workouts; low-level burning/acute leg fatigue in high-intensity; downhills beat up legs.
- Frequency: new athletes one day/week; experienced with high-intensity or high ME can often manage two per week before aerobic volume suffers. Muscles will feel these for a few days. New athletes should use them in the Base Period as preparation before Zone 3/4 aerobic intervals; can alternate with Z3/Z4 interval workouts but separate by 72 hours and maintain high Zone 1 volume. Avoid using too close together or close to conventional Z3/Z4.
- Start with little to no added resistance to learn movements and build muscle/tendon strength and elasticity; ramp loads very gradually.
- Effect on uphill endurance almost immediate. If no week-to-week hill improvement, likely doing too much; insufficient recovery or easy aerobic training.
- Upper-body ME can be included in Base Period for advanced skiers.

### [TUA-C08-019] Planning outline: time reality test and A/B/C event priorities

Reality test for volume and race distance: tally average weekly hours over the last year spent at work/school, sleeping, eating, traveling, and with family. Remaining hours = hours available to train or race; use as the absolute upper limit of training time.

Event priorities (write target events on a calendar and code A, B, C):
- Ideally one or two A events as training focus. Several A events within a short time is difficult; only more experienced athletes can pull it off. Space high-priority events far enough apart for recovery and possibly training.
- A priority: the biggies; the plan builds toward them. Need a significant taper leading in and may need significant recovery afterward. If more than one A event in a training cycle, include a base-building period of at least several weeks between them.
- B priority: tune-ups/tests leading into the A event (test fitness, strategy, equipment, feeding, tactics, mental preparation). Should not be a big stretch beyond current capacity; include with only a slight drop in training load for a few days before and after. If so demanding that they disrupt the overall progression of training load, preparation for the A event suffers.
- C priority: trained right through, used as just another workout; need not be official competitions (may be test events to assess fitness). Should fit the progression without adjusting the plan; can be inserted later.
- No big goal: do only base training to increase basic work capacity. Many people benefit, since lack of basic work capacity is probably the biggest impediment to mountain performance; nothing will get you farther than improving the base. Athletes with a huge base (e.g. Kílian) can perform well off that base, allowing many races per year.

### [TUA-C08-021] A Review of Workout Categories (Chapter 9)

**Aerobic Conditioning and Aerobic Capacity: Z1, Z2.** Improves ability to do a high volume of aerobic work, best measured by AeT pace; supports high-intensity training.
- Multiple hours: Z1 and Z2, low intensity, long duration.
- Pick-ups or strides: 45- to 75-minute Zones 1–2 workout with 6×15 sec to 8×30 sec accelerations up to a relaxed fast pace (spread over the workout), two-minute easy recovery between.
- Steady: sustained Z2 effort for 30 to 60 minutes.

**Aerobic Endurance: Z3.** Increases maximum sustainable uphill pace and the time you can sustain it.
- Continuous Z3 tempo for 20 to 40 minutes.
- Intervals: intensity at and just below LT heart rate (Z3). Work periods 8–15 minutes. Active recovery intervals two to four minutes. Total work volume 40–60 minutes.

**Aerobic Power: Z4.** Trains aerobic and anaerobic systems to maximum combined capacity. Interval style, work periods 30 seconds to eight minutes, work-to-rest ratio 1:1. Very taxing; less than 10 percent of annual volume. Intensity 90–95 percent of maximum heart rate. Best done uphill.
- Recovery intervals sufficient to complete each repetition at the same pace and heart rate. Total work duration 15 to 30 minutes.
- 30/30s (see sidebar, page 177).
- High-tempo/high-resistance intervals (a Kílian favorite): two minutes high-tempo/high-HR running, two-minute active rest, then two minutes of long strides on foot or skis on uphill terrain. Repeat until pace and heart rate drop off.

**Anaerobic Capacity: Z5.** Hill Sprint or Hill Bounding (see page 181) for specific leg strength/power. Start in the Base Period one time per week if general strength is adequate. Near maximum intensity on the steepest hill available with good footing. Each rep 10 to 30 seconds. Full recovery (minimum two to three minutes) between reps. Start with 6×10 sec; add two more reps each week.

**General and Max Strength.** Core and General Strength programs in the Transition Period twice per week. Max strength in the early Base Period twice per week. In final preparation, use the ME program specific for VK, long skimo, or trail run/race.

### [TUA-C08-022] Transition Period: purpose and starting volume

- Purpose: preconditioning period preparing for the Base Period's specific demands. Mainly important for those without strong endurance background, new to structured training, or returning after a significant break. Length: three weeks (e.g. runner moving to skimo) up to eight weeks (first structured season). Advanced athletes with good body feel may go straight to Base. Conservative approach suggested when in doubt.
- Break from training more than four weeks: week one at about 50 percent of previous training cycle's average weekly hours.
- Only transitioning sport focus (e.g. running to skiing) with break of no more than two weeks: first week at about 80 percent of most recent training volume.
- New to structured training, no history, conservative annual (fifty weeks) training hours targets:
  - Juniors (age fifteen and under): 300 total annual hours
  - Juniors (age sixteen to nineteen): 450 total annual hours
  - Twenty to thirty-five: 400 total annual hours
  - Thirty-five to fifty: 350 total annual hours
  - Over fifty: 300 total annual hours
- Workload should not feel challenging in early weeks; if it does, you started too aggressively. Starting too hard when fresh means accumulated fatigue appears after several weeks ("wheels come off"), possibly needing a couple of very easy weeks. Easy to raise load later.
- First organized season: consider an eight-week Transition Period with more gradual progression.

### [TUA-C08-026] Cornerstone of the Base: aerobic base and Ten Percent Test rule for adding intensity

For most readers and all sports in the book, the number one training focus is increasing the aerobic base; without it the rest is "window dressing". Seasoned athletes with a big aerobic base need multi-dimensional, more complex training. An athlete whose weakness is not aerobic endurance should not keep training only strength/aerobic; re-allot some time (e.g., faster flat running) without stopping aerobic training.
Decision rule (Ten Percent Test, see p. 91; AeT vs LT heart rate):
- AeT–LT difference 10 percent or less: include up to two weekly high-intensity aerobic endurance sessions in the Base Period.
- Spread greater than 10 percent: delay Zone 3 workouts and limit higher-intensity workouts to no more than once a week.
Reason: those with higher basic aerobic capacity handle more intensity, recover and benefit better; those with a wider spread still have aerobic "low-hanging fruit", and the gains from intensity are smaller and shorter lived.
No simple rule of thumb exists for how much time to give running economy or skimo technique.
Strength in this period plays only a supporting role. If you lack basic strength per the strength assessment (ch. 7), start with remedial strength; those with a better base use Specific Strength. You will not spend many hours per week in the gym.

### [TUA-C08-028] Week types: B (Base) week

Foundational weeks; build aerobic capacity, running economy, strength. Aim: accumulate time, distance, vertical in Zones 1–2. If aerobically deficient (ADS, Ten Percent Test p. 91), nearly all aerobic base in Zone 2; otherwise Zone 2 sparingly, no more than 15 percent of total weekly volume, base work in Zone 1.
- Strength: two workouts/week, General or Specific per strength assessment. Two Core Strength workouts on these or other days.
- Active recovery: on problem areas (stretch, roll, massage, swim), at least three times per week.
- Recovery workouts: count in aerobic base distance; very light days after a heavy day, or any day you feel off after warm-up (would grade C or D). Cut intensity dramatically, shorten if needed. They should leave you feeling better immediately or a few hours later; if not, consider the next day off.
- Aerobic base: pick one long workout day at 30–40 percent of weekly distance/vertical (higher percentage in low-volume weeks; smaller as volume rises). Distribute the rest over at least four workouts, which include warm-ups/cool-downs for Hill Sprints/Bounding and ME. Two runs include fast downhill running pick-ups. Skimo may include skiing and roller skiing; ski mountaineers optionally Ski Striding (see p. 313).
- Muscular endurance: if a weakness (p. 240), begin focused ME now; ski mountaineers once per week in early weeks.

### [TUA-C08-029] Week types: I (Intensity) week

Introduces Zone 3 and 4 training to improve endurance; supplements, does not replace, B-week training. ADS: nearly all aerobic base in Zone 2; otherwise drop Zone 2 and do base work in Zone 1.
- Strength: one Hill Sprint/Bounding workout (ski mountaineers use Hill Bounding); continue two Core Strength workouts.
- Active recovery: at least three times per week.
- Recovery workouts: as B week, terrain less steep.
- Aerobic base: one long workout 30–40 percent of weekly distance/vertical; remainder over at least four workouts (including warm-ups/cool-downs). Two runs with fast downhill pick-ups. Skimo also ski simulations; ski mountaineers optional (p. 313).
- Muscular endurance: drop ME and begin integrating Zone 3 or 4 workouts; ski mountaineers move to two times/week.
- High intensity (see p. 305): start with Zone 3 intervals at no more than 5 percent of weekly total time. Choose duration/volume that lets you recover in 48 hours; if legs still tired at 72 hours, cut back next workout. When you can handle 10 percent of weekly time in Zone 3, begin replacing some Zone 3 with Zone 4. Skimo in ski-specific mode. Ski mountaineers disregard.

### [TUA-C08-030] Week types: R (Recovery) week

Consolidation week; cut to 50 percent of total volume. Purpose is to recover as fast and well as possible, not to add fitness. Loosely structured; days off or especially light training allowed. By Sunday you should feel like attacking the next week; if not, you did not recover. Alternative recovery activity (swimming, easy cycling). ADS: nearly all base in Zone 2; otherwise Zone 2 ≤15 percent of weekly volume, rest Zone 1.
- Strength: one Core Strength workout; cut Hill Sprint/Bounding volume in half, late in the week (ski mountaineers: Hill Bounding halved).
- Active recovery: on problem areas, at least three times/week.
- Recovery workouts: at least two this week, in Recovery zone, less steep terrain; same rules as B week.
- Aerobic base: long workout about 40 percent of weekly distance/vertical, late in week. One other aerobic-intensity workout about 20 percent of total; rest in Recovery Zone. One run with fast downhill pick-ups. Skimo may include ski walking, roller skiing, Moose Hoofing.
- Muscular endurance: drop or cut by 50 percent.
- High intensity: cut by half from last week; one workout late in the week. Ski mountaineers disregard.

### [TUA-C08-031] Week types: S (Specificity) week

Used late in the plan to prepare for the A event. Ultrarunners and ski mountaineers: back-to-back long runs/skis (similar effect to one very long day with less stress and much shorter recovery). Shorter high-intensity events (VK, skimo race): reduce aerobic base and add more Zone 4 interval work. ADS: nearly all base in Zone 2; otherwise Zone 2 ≤15 percent of weekly volume, rest Zone 1.
- Strength: one Hill Sprint/Bounding workout; two Core Strength workouts.
- Muscular endurance: ski mountaineers continue once per week; runners and skimo racers cut so high intensity is higher quality.
- Active recovery: at least three times/week.
- Recovery workouts: at least two, less steep terrain, same rules.
- Aerobic base (long events): back-to-back long workouts totaling 60–80 percent of weekly distance/vertical; remaining over at least three workouts (including warm-ups/cool-downs). Two include fast downhill running/skiing pick-ups. Skimo may add Ski Striding, roller skiing, Moose Hoofing. For shorter skimo/VK: double up high-intensity workouts.
- High intensity: Ultra runners: cut all Zone 3; only one Zone 4 interval session at no more than the previous week's volume, separated by at least 72 hours from the back-to-back long runs. VK/skimo: cut all Zone 3 and add a second Zone 4 session separated by at least 72 hours from the previous one with recovery workouts between; total weekly Zone 4 time no more than 15 percent of the previous week [as printed].

### [TUA-C08-032] Week types: T (Taper) week and G (Goal) week

TAPER: highly individual; some top athletes like Kílian prefer not to taper, especially when racing often (too much tapering loses fitness over the season). Traditionally load is reduced for days or weeks (very long races) so you are fresh. Learn duration/steepness through experience. A priority races deserve a more dramatic taper than B; C events are treated as normal workouts. Training is over once in taper; do not sneak in special or new workouts. Reduce load gradually, as little as necessary to refresh the legs without losing fitness.
- Strength: one Core Strength workout; cut Hill Sprint/Bounding by 30 percent week to week.
- Active recovery: at least three times/week.
- Recovery workouts: all but two sessions in recovery range.
- Aerobic base: long run/ski about 50 percent of weekly distance/vertical (including warm-up/cool-down for hill sprints and intensity). One other Zone 2 workout of no more than 10 percent of weekly distance/vertical. A few short fast downhill runs; skimo racers a few quick-tempo pick-ups.
- High intensity: short with long rests; intensity volume drops about 30 percent week to week; for sharpening only.
GOAL (race week): part of taper and self-care; may involve travel. Nothing this week makes you faster, many things can make you slower. Rest and relax but be active daily; too much rest makes you sluggish.
- No strength. Active recovery at least three times.
- Recovery workouts: only Recovery Zone, no more than four days, to keep legs loose.
- High intensity: spice two workouts with three or four accelerations of 30 seconds each to a fun, fast pace, with three minutes easy jogging between. None for ski mountaineers.

### [TUA-C08-033] Base Period guidelines: progression of training volume

Progression is among the most important elements. Too fast: at best tired and need unplanned break; at worst injury or overtraining derailing weeks/months. No one can tell your starting vertical; it depends on age, training history, genetics, life stressors. Charts are conservative; listen to your body.
Rules:
- Keep distance, vertical, and time increases at 10 percent or less week to week, except after a recovery week when you may increase much more.
- Do not increase distance and vertical for more than three weeks in a row.
- Do not increase distance, vertical, and intensity in a single week; increase one at a time. If two, keep increases under 15 percent (e.g., 10 percent distance + 5 percent vertical, or 8 percent Zone 3 time + 7 percent distance).
- No distance/vertical increases greater than 15 percent two weeks in a row.
- Follow large jumps (near 15 percent) in distance, vertical or intensity with a recovery week.
Below 400 hours/year: expect extra base-building cycles; do not skip to Category 2 too early.

### [TUA-C08-034] Modulation of the training load (3:1, 2:1, 1:1 cycles)

Macro examples mostly use three weeks of gradual build then a recovery week (3:1). Not inflexible; some do best with 2:1; experiment and adapt to how you respond. Build modulation in: rest before you are forced to; gains occur in recovery. In recovery weeks take enough days off to be fresh by the end.
- Keep a log; experiment.
- Modulate vertical/distance like the macro examples; stick to a progression, no random jumping.
- Recovery weeks drop load typically at least 40 to 60 percent of the last big week's volume; no more than one intensity session.
- With little (especially successful) high-intensity experience, shift to 2:1 or even 1:1 weekly cycle.
- After an intentional overreaching period (p. 99), plan a correspondingly lighter recovery, dropping load by as much as 70 percent.

### [TUA-C08-036] Adding high intensity in the Base Period: guidelines

Low-intensity volume is easy to prescribe (more is almost always better); intensity is highly individual (training history determines dose and recovery). If new to adding intensity into a high-volume base, be conservative.
- Skimo: races are at much higher intensity than ultra running, so intensity matters, but it supplements, not replaces, aerobic work. Especially Cat 1 athletes: Zones 1–2 vertical base takes priority. Ideally add higher intensity only once AeT HR or pace is within 10 percent of LT HR (Ten Percent Test, p. 91), though not always practical.
- Runners: with adequate base volume at the distance, intensity raises performance; moving up in distance, prioritize distance and vertical before intensity.
- Begin one Zone 3 workout per week when within two months of your first race, regardless of aerobic state. Adding intensity should not require cutting volume more than 5 percent, otherwise you risk losing base.
Guidelines:
- Kílian introduces intensity with Zone 3: one workout per week once handling Zones 1–2 volume well. Highly trained athletes may do two sessions/week totaling 1–2 hours in Zone 3, but it takes years to build. Cat 1: one workout/week totaling 20–30 minutes of Zone 3 as intervals such as 3×7 or 3×8 min with short active rest, work-to-rest 3:1 or 4:1. If you do not recover well, you pushed too hard or overall volume was too high; adjust.
- Increase intensity volume gradually; add only as much as does not force Zones 1–2 volume down by more than 5 percent (else a short peak followed by a performance drop).
- Type depends on event: long Zone 3 sessions for long races; shorter Zone 4 and Zone 5 for VK or shorter.
- Start with about 5 percent of weekly volume in Zone 3; add not more than 10 percent per week.
- Increase only when comfortable with the current mix; too-fast intensity is a very common mistake.
- Drop or reduce Zone 2 when adding Zone 3/4; shift to more Zone 1.
- Add Zone 4 only when Zone 3 reaches about 10 percent of weekly volume, you recover well, and weekly volume has not dropped.
- Add Zone 4 by gradually replacing Zone 3; start with no more than 5 percent of weekly volume in Zone 4. If you must drop Zones 1–2 volume by more than 5 percent, use Zone 3 more until easy.
- New to structured training: start Zone 4 with 30/30s (p. 177) before longer, more taxing intervals.

### [TUA-C08-042] Category 1 mountain runner: training philosophy and progression cautions

- Biggest single improvement: train more (hours, vertical, km), not harder. Performance depends almost entirely on basic aerobic capacity, improved only by more low-to-moderate intensity (Zones 1–2). No shortcuts or secret workouts. Condition the body to handle lots of training first; later layer higher intensity. If you cannot run continuously for 1–2 hours on mountainous terrain day in, day out, do not worry about high intensity. Bottom-up approach: each layer rests on the layers below.
- Very gradual volume ramp-up; the suggested progression (fig. 11.7, p. 291) is conservative on purpose. Most common running injuries are lower-leg overuse injuries from increasing volume too fast (chronic impact stress); by the time they are felt much damage is done and weeks may be lost. Avoiding them is first priority. Once you are injured, no plan will work for you.
- Connective tissue (tendons, fascia) adapts slowly: takes weeks of sustained and repeated running for tendons and fascia to increase elasticity and strength to handle thousands of impacts a day, each at least 2× body weight and much more on downhills.
- First-time 50K: minimum training period twenty weeks. With little recent running history, precede with at least a six-week Transition Period (page 145). If already running regularly about 20–25 miles (35–50 km) per week, start at week 1. Even after winter skiing, a short Transition Period is needed to get running legs back; do not jump into a 40-mile week from the couch.
- You will not really begin running training until you have accumulated about 100 miles (62 kilometers) [Likely unit/typo error in source — recorded as printed: 100 miles is ~161 km, or 62 miles is ~100 km] in your legs; before that you are just conditioning legs to handle daily running. Seasoned runners may take two weeks, beginners at least six weeks.
- Intersperse big weeks with lower-load weeks so the body can absorb and adapt to build period training; do not hold training load constant for multiple weeks (lack of modulation causes stagnation and possibly overtraining).
- Specificity: run (and hike some steep sections) for training. Biking and swimming are not substitutes unless injury necessitates, but can be effective recovery workouts to give tired legs a break. Choose terrain as similar to goal event as possible (rocky/rough trails demand very different coordination than smooth trails). Incorporate frequent short sections of fast downhill running to develop strength and quickness, especially for rough trails.

### [TUA-C08-043] Notes on adding more intensity (Category 2 mountain runners)

- Intensity type depends on target event: long Zone 3 sessions for long-distance races; shorter Zone 4 and Zone 5 sessions for VK or shorter races.
- Use a mix of intensities overall. Opinions vary widely; no conclusive evidence supports one method over others for building endurance. Fitter athletes benefit from longer repetitions (more than six minutes) and higher overall high-intensity volume.
- Start high-intensity volume low (weekly and per workout) and increase gradually. Add only as much as does not force you to drop Zone 1 and 2 base volume by more than 5 percent. If you cannot maintain Z1–Z2 volume you risk a short peak followed by performance decline.
- Drop all Zone 2 training when adding Zone 3 work; do all aerobic base work in Zone 1 (Zone 2 is too stressful for most when intensity is added).
- Include one Hill Sprint workout (page 182) in most weeks to build and maintain specific leg power.
- Muscular endurance (ME, page 240): if needed, add ME workouts in the early weeks after at least four weeks of only Z1–Z2 base training; once per week for eight weeks; drop when introducing Z3 and Z4 workouts, which then maintain the ME gained.
- After six to eight weeks of successfully incorporating Z3, introduce Z4: replace some Z3 with half the volume of Z4 (30 min Z3 replaced by 15 min Z4). Drop any Z2 work; maintain aerobic base in Zone 1 or lower at Recovery effort. Kílian's favorite way to introduce Z4 is 30/30s (page 177).

### [TUA-C08-044] Notes for weekly vertical (Moving Up in Vertical)

Weekly vertical depends on goal-event vertical, access to hills/mountains, and training history. Choose goals according to what you can train on, and train on hills similar to the goal race. If legs are the weak link despite vertical meters, add a period of ME training.
- Begin the vertical progression with no more than half of the goal event's total vertical in the first week.
- Incorporate muscular endurance work from the start; keep it as long as gains are seen (page 240).
- Train at least the goal event's total vertical during at least two precompetition-period weeks, corresponding to the bigger distance weeks.
- Do not increase distance, vertical and intensity in a single week; increase one at a time. If two must increase, keep increases under 15 percent total (e.g., 10% distance with 5% vertical, or 8% Zone 3 time with 7% distance).
- Increase vertical no more than 10 percent in consecutive weeks and no more than 20 percent in a four-week span (three building weeks followed by a recovery week).
- Cut vertical by a similar amount as distance during recovery weeks (40–60 percent).

### [TUA-C09-001] Lesson from overtraining: layer training volume on gradually

Lesson from Steve House's story: after beginning with a coach who did not understand the demands of climbing, he was overtrained. He became ill (IVs, fever), did not eat for 21 days, and went from 165 lb to 135 lb. ### [TUA-C09-002] ADS: accept the slow AeT pace; real-world example

### [TUA-C09-003] Rules of thumb from Chapter 2

### [TUA-C09-006] Sore muscles (DOMS): do's and don'ts

- Causes: higher-intensity strength workout, faster or longer downhill running. Muscles sore to the touch; stairs painful. Delayed Onset of Muscle Soreness (DOMS) usually peaks 48 hours after the workout. Mostly tough it out with light aerobic exercise during the DOMS period.
- Do: light aerobic recovery workouts; swim or at least lightly kick in the pool; E-stim on the recovery setting; light massage; ibuprofen after twelve hours post workout; ice bath after twelve hours post workout; frequent light stretching to reduce stiffness; extra sleep; extra protein, especially before bed; self-maintenance rolling with roller or ball.
- Don't: speed, power or strength work; high-intensity aerobic interval work; overstretch.

### [TUA-C09-007] Dead, heavy or flat-feeling leg muscles

- Usually caused by raising training load too much in too short a time. Check questions: have you increased distance, vertical or intensity in the past ten days? Lack of spring in running stride? Legs extra fatigued on a set of stairs? Legs ache at night in bed? Performance or pace dropped and workouts take more effort? All are big red flags; if ignored they can lead to overtraining. Likely a combination of Group B (glycogen depletion) and neurological fatigue.
- If legs are not refreshed after a couple of very light days, do more of the following.
- Do: extensive use of the pool for swimming/light kicking; self-maintenance rolling with roller or ball (may need twice daily); deep-tissue massage; compression garments; elevate legs above heart; increase post-workout carb intake; light recovery-effort aerobic cross-training (cycling, rowing); increase quality and quantity of sleep.
- Don't: extensive aerobic workouts, especially with much vertical; intensive workouts such as Zones 3-5 interval sessions; muscular endurance workouts; anaerobic endurance workouts like extended hill sprints; drink much alcohol.

### [TUA-C09-008] Group B: Energy fatigue

- Warning sign: feeling unmotivated and low in energy, in an athlete who normally loves training. Check: waking up tired even when sleeping more? needing more sleep in the day? feeling flat? avoiding friends? work or family more stressful? Follow the course below for a few days.

### [TUA-C09-009] Grading your workouts (A-F) and the stop rule

- Grade each workout by feel and record it in the log; use together with a recovery-monitoring test.
- A: felt like Superman, plenty in reserve. B: good workout, completed with no problem. C: did the workout but felt flat or off. D: could not finish the planned workout or had to reduce it. F: could not train that day due to fatigue or illness.
- Rule of thumb: more than two Cs in a row, or a C and a D within one week -> stop and assess. You are not absorbing training: load too high for current fitness, or recovery insufficient.
- Persistently poor grades: the body is signalling; you may be getting sick without symptoms. If nothing changes, Cs become Ds, you are no longer adapting and are headed toward overtraining.

### [TUA-C09-010] Returning to training after a fatigue-related break

- Applies to a fatigue-related break of more than two days. Do not resume where you left off; that load may have caused the fatigue.
- Ease back with low-intensity workouts reduced in duration by 50 percent for a couple of days to assess recovery. If they go well, increase to 75 percent for a day or so and reassess. Ramping up too fast risks relapse.
- Fatigue is the most common reason for unplanned disruptions.

### [TUA-C09-011] Illness: stop training at first signs; return rule

- Illness is the second biggest cause of derailment and carries the most long-term damage potential. Denial ("just a cold, I can train through it") can turn a cold into sinus infection, upper-respiratory infection or pneumonia taking several weeks.
- Reason: being sick often means training too hard and/or resting too little. Light-to-moderate loads can boost immunity; heavy loads (high intensity and/or long duration) suppress immunity, especially in the first hour after the workout. Precautions: change into dry clothes after a hard workout, wear a hat in winter.
- Recommendation: at the first signs of illness, STOP training. Energy reserves are engaged making antibodies; extra load weakens immunity, delays healing and opens the door to more infection.
- Cautionary pattern (14-day example): training through, partial days off, a hard workout while still ill, ending with bronchial infection and antibiotics; a one-week illness becomes most of a month.
- After a minor illness: a head cold takes 2-3 days to shake; do not jump back on day 3-4. The aerobic system takes the biggest hit. Return first with Zones 1-2 efforts. Resuming the full prior load raises perceived exertion and forces more anaerobic metabolism, limiting aerobic quality.
- Prescription: one day of easy training for every day you were sick and away from training; gradually increase duration and intensity during these days. In most minor illness you feel like your old self within a week.

### [TUA-C09-017] How much general strength is enough; priorities

- Mountain locomotion is single-legged propulsion, so even general strength should emphasize single-legged exercises; hip stabilization is key.
- If you deficient in general strength, a simple program can make specific training more effective. Many mountain athletes skip general strength; consider your own needs.
- Benchmarks (suggestions, not categorical): if you cannot half squat your own body weight on the shoulders, you could gain performance and durability. Squatting twice body weight probably adds nothing and may detract; extra hours are better spent on specific strength than adding 20 pounds to squat.
- Skimo racer unable to do a single pull-up would probably gain from improving it.
- Squat strength correlates closely with performance in sprinting, throwing, jumping, football; for single-leg endurance sports it is only general.
- Amateurs have limited time: examine sport strength demands before e.g. a bench press program when the goal is VK time.

### [TUA-C09-018] Rationale for specific strength and hill sprints/bounding

Brain muscle-recruitment patterns are a learned skill. Heavy slow work in a semi-specific exercise (e.g. single box step) builds general strength, but at some point it must be transferred to a specific exercise like Hill Sprints (runners) or Hill Bounding with poles (skiers), where contraction speed is much faster and motor neurons must be trained for the sport-specific pattern and speed. Sprinting or bounding uphill adds resistance via speed and gradient using sport-specific patterns. Include Hill Sprints/Bounding from early Base Period for intermediate and higher athletes if basic strength is adequate. If you already have high uphill leg strength, you can go straight to ME workouts. Occasional Hill Sprint workouts can maintain it through the plan. ### [TUA-C09-019] Ultradistance pacing heuristic: rebounding from energy troughs

- In ultradistance events, "it never always keeps getting worse": physical and mental low points are unpredictable and often occur not only in the final quarter, but equally in the first 20-30 miles (especially when coming off a long taper).
- Rebounding heuristic when hitting an unexpected energy trough or psychological funk:
  1. Back off the intensity slightly.
  2. Push the seemingly insurmountable remaining distance out of mind; do not project too far ahead; stay present, patient, and positive.
  3. Be diligent with nutrition and hydration (eat and drink a little extra).
  4. Have faith that the body can and will rebound with a resurgence of energy; do not drop out prematurely during an early low point.

### [TUA-C09-020] Starting volume when no training history; progress check rule

- Without structured training history, starting volume is a guess; no one can predict response without a benchmark. People without logs recall only memorable big weeks, not the average week.

### [TUA-C09-021] Training during race season (Category 1 and 2): principles

- Early-season plan aims to bring racing shape yet allow gains as the season progresses; races build race-specific speed endurance.
- With frequent racing, the challenge is maintaining aerobic base; long Z1/Z2 days and hard workouts are hard to fit. Tapering days, travel to the race, and travel/recovery after leave little time between races.
- Unless at a high fitness level, squeezing productive training between races only 5–6 days apart (two days largely travel) risks reducing the next race's performance.
- The bigger the training base, the more easily frequent race intensity is managed; this takes years of consistency. Racing too much and too often with a small base leads to declining performance.
- Prioritize races: A = main focus of season; B = less important, maybe a day or two lighter before; C = used as hard workouts, trained through.
- If possible plan one or two training blocks of two to three weeks between races mid-season (e.g., around one or two C races or leading into a B race) to maintain fitness through the season.

## C10 Applied Guidance

### [TUA-C10-001] Fat adaptation: what it is, benefits, how to achieve

- Old dogma: fat contributes only at low-moderate intensity, capped (about 1 g/min) and zero at LT. True for general and recreational populations. New studies show well-trained athletes use fat up to twice as much and sustain it up to maximum intensities (Fig 2.9 dashed line 1 g/min; Fig 2.10: fat-adapted mean 1.54 g/min peak fat oxidation vs non-fat-adapted 0.67 g/min; Volek et al.).
- Benefits: (1) fuel: fat stores virtually limitless (up to 100,000 calories), glycogen rarely reaches 2,000 calories; glycogen remains critical, depletion (bonk) gives swift power drop; sipping glycogen while using fat allows higher output longer. (2) Reduced lactate at submaximal efforts, lower perceived exertion, more economy.
- How: long-duration low-intensity workouts; do some aerobic capacity-building workouts fasted (e.g., morning before any calories = mildly glycogen-depleted, forces better fat use and raises aerobic capacity); a diet reduced in carbohydrate calories and higher in fat accelerates adaptation. Athletes training more than twelve hours per week see less effect from diet modification because training volume already gives ample stimulus.

## C11 Recovery, Monitoring & Lifestyle

### [TUA-C11-001] Recovery times and Fig 3.4 Recovery and Adaptation Times

Stimulus then recovery is a balancing act; writing the plan is easy, implementing it to peak at the right time is tricky. Recovery is the most underestimated component. Fatigue is body feedback; learn to interpret it.
Intramuscular glycogen restoration takes 8 to 72 hours; usually delay next heavy bout a day or two. If fatigue prevents a similar or harder workout, do Zone 1 or Recovery intensity on intervening days. Well-trained athletes have up to three times the intramuscular glycogen of the untrained, so recovery times are individual but roughly balanced by their harder, longer training.
Guidelines: high-intensity exercise and strength training need two to three full days before absorbing a similar load. Low-intensity endurance workouts of two to three hours or less: usually recovered in 8–24 hours. Long (over two hours) training even at low intensity: 24–48 hours for glycogen restoration. Eating during and within 30–60 minutes of finishing long workouts greatly reduces recovery time, sometimes by days.

Kílian: hard muscular endurance work can take 72 hours to recover; a fast race or big vertical day takes ~2 days (can continue next day with heavy legs, slower). Aerobic fatigue differs; listen to body. Quality rest means doing nothing or everything slowly (housework, office is not rest). If training seems pointless, take a rest day; if every day feels like that, you may not be trained enough for the level pushed; if doing it just to do it, rest.

### [TUA-C11-002] Enhancing recovery: methods in order of importance

Recovery strategies are integral for athletes at the limit of what the body can absorb; less essential for those time-limited rather than energy-limited.
1. Diet (most important): sooner calories arrive the sooner repair begins. Consume 100–200 calories in the first thirty minutes post exercise of over an hour, in a form that doesn't upset the stomach, at carbohydrate:protein ratio 3:1 or 4:1. Missing this window can delay recovery by up to days.
2. Sleep: REM sleep (several blocks of 90 to 120 minutes per night) aids repair; hormone release; REM and vigorous exercise best stimulate natural HGH (somatotropin), a primary tool for adapting to higher loads. Anything that disrupts REM impairs recovery. Even fifteen minutes of deep sleep in a nap is refreshing.
3. Massage: skilled massage increases circulation, speeds nutrient replenishment, reduces inflammation (inflammation stimulates some adaptations but can be too much). Used by pro cyclists. Study by Dr. Mark Tarnopolsky (McMaster): as little as ten minutes of deep-tissue massage dramatically sped recovery.
4. Self-massage: balls (favorites: MobilityWOD 80mm ball, Roll Model balls; book The Roll Model by Jill Miller) produce shearing on muscle fibers, reducing adhesions; user controls pressure. If ball too intense start with foam roller; progress to balls once full weight on roller is tolerated. Use before bed to relax and sleep. Electro stim machines (e.g. Compex) act like massage for recovery.
5. Reducing life stressors: body reacts to work and training stress similarly; relaxation techniques such as meditation aid recovery; for stressful lives, reducing life stress may be the best training strategy.
6. Recovery workouts: not normal training; very low intensity, can have powerful restorative effect (coaches know; science still unsure why). Must feel very easy; most used when stacking workouts closely (often twice a day) but helpful after tough workouts; alternate modes (cycling, paddling, especially swimming) relieve overused legs; even a brisk walk works if very tired or a little sick. Light exercise almost always beats being sedentary.

### [TUA-C11-003] Perception, resting heart rate and stair test

- Perception: how you feel when preparing to train or after warm-up is the standby. Even with concrete metrics, do a reality check that body sensations agree with the numbers. A key function of the warm-up is to assess readiness; be prepared to reduce or cancel the workout.
- Resting heart rate: an elevated value suggests the sympathetic nervous system is in high gear (big training load, infection or impending sickness, poor sleep, other life stress). Monitor response more closely over the next days to see if it deviates further or returns to normal. Results are often spotty because other factors affect it; weigh it with other factors. Measure at the same time and under similar conditions each day (morning test).
- Stair test: walk or bound (two steps at once) up a set of stairs you climb daily and check how legs feel at the top. You learn the feeling of a good workout versus one needing more recovery.

### [TUA-C11-005] Training log: what to track (time, vertical, technicality)

- Training load is a function of volume and intensity. Use a plan and record workouts in a log; the more accurate, the more useful for future planning. A log can be a notebook or an online platform. The authors use TrainingPeaks (proprietary metrics for fitness and fatigue monitoring). Strava, MovesCount, Garmin Connect, Polar Flow also log and sync to TrainingPeaks but lack its tracking sophistication (as of writing).
- Time: simplest metric, in hours or miles/km. Kilometers/miles are standard for road runners; hours is standard for mountain athletes because mountain miles are not equal.
- Elevation gain/loss: combined with time, gives a clearer load picture. For mountain runners and ski mountaineers, vertical normally carries the intensity aspect; even at low intensity it recruits more muscle mass than flatter terrain. More vertical = more load. Example: a 50-mile (80 km) week with 6,000 ft (1,800 m) vertical gain and loss has less load than a 40-mile (60 km) week with 10,000 ft (3,000 m). No formula accounts for steepness, footing, step size. Record vertical like time and distance. Base period load must build progressively (increments in Section Four, "How to Train").
- Kílian's recording: running = time, vertical meters, distance; skiing = time and vertical meters only. Technicality scale: Tech 1 on-piste ski or good track/easy trail; Tech 2 off-piste or rough track/medium trail; Tech 3 off-piste with some scrambling / off-trail scrambling; Tech 4 climbing.

### [TUA-C11-006] Recovery workouts: role, duration, rules

- As AeT improves, Zone 2 workouts impose higher neuromuscular loads and need wider spacing; lowest-intensity recovery workouts fill a growing share of volume, giving more modulation of load. Light aerobic work has a dramatic restorative effect.
- Why unresolved ("jury is out on the why"), but almost all endurance coaches prescribe them. Johnston coached cross-country skiers who thrived on long recovery workouts; Western coaches of Kenyan runners note dependence on very long, stumblingly slow recovery runs.
- Most people find 30 min to 1 hour useful per workout; may be only a 20-minute brisk walk before bed.
- Done properly you feel better at the end than at the start, and better the next day. Next-day workout should almost always feel great; if not, you needed more recovery work or an easier pace.
- Good place for cross-training (biking, swimming), especially if legs are beat up.

### [TUA-C12-002] Specificity and cross-training

Specificity: train in ways resembling the event; the better the athlete, the more event specific training must be. Beginners and those with long lapses can gain fitness from almost any prolonged endurance activity (cross-training); benefits diminish quickly at competitive recreational level. Gains become elusive unless training shifts to the intended activity (e.g. Kílian gains nothing in ascent speed from swimming or kayaking, though they can be recovery).
Uphill athletes should prioritize weight-bearing exercise. Cycling: great general exercise but too efficient — seated, no full body weight, less muscle mass, limited range of motion, no balance/uneven footing; good for recovery and injury rehab. Kílian formerly used cycling in a high gear for muscular endurance, small volume, now dropped. Swimming: little carryover but excellent recovery tool (prone position, heart works less, water cools leaving more blood for muscles).
Nonelite: if you can handle only less than 25 miles (40 km) running or 6 hours per week, adding hiking, cycling, rowing, stair machine, even swimming helps. With much higher volume and desire to maximize, extra training energy goes to running. Foot-borne sports require foot-borne training adaptations.

### [TUA-C12-003] Individuality and FT/ST athletes

Quote (Canova): "If you give ten different athletes the same workout, you will see ten different responses." Factors: lifetime training history (childhood/adolescent endurance training retains structural adaptations; adaptations come slower after teens and twenties and need much more work after middle age); recent training history; life stressors (school, work, family, relationships); FT- or ST-dominant fibers.
Copying another's (especially a champion's) training is a common mistake; media report final six weeks, not the ten years of capacity-building that let them handle and benefit from those workouts.
FT dominant: good sprint, good vertical leap, produce high lactate during easy workouts, can't hold peak fitness long. Needs: more aerobic base (Z1–Z2) to rely less on anaerobic system; little long Z4; better shorter repetitions (<2 min) to keep lactate low.
ST dominant: no fast sprint, poor vertical leap, little lactate at high intensities (low anaerobic capacity), maintain peak form longer, recover quickly from long hard aerobic training, need less fueling in long workouts. Needs: frequent, small doses of speed to improve anaerobic capacity.

### [TUA-C12-004] Marc Pinsach Rubirola: lessons on individualization and load limits

- Do not copy a more gifted athlete's training; no two bodies respond identically. Find what your body needs regardless of others; control emotions to trust your own plan. Treating each session as a race and needing constant proof of fitness led to overtraining, demotivation and near burnout; root cause was low self-esteem.
- His numbers: load worked up to age 23 at 700 hours/year and 190,000 vertical m/season (ski mountaineering); raising to 850 hours/year and 220,000 vertical m/season produced four overtrained seasons.
- Mistakes: endurance pace too fast (Zone 2 and above), especially with others; many long aerobic intervals (three 10-15 min at Zones 3-4), spending too much time fast while not targeting race pace.
- Corrections: do not increase training more than 10 percent between two consecutive seasons; build gradually over years; his limit is 700 hours/year and 200,000 vertical m/season; bulk of training in Zones 1-2 at a pace he could hold for hours without fatigue; go own pace when training with others ("Training isn't competition day"); intervals are 30 seconds to 3 minutes at maximum speed - if you cannot do them at maximum, go home and recover. Skimo/skyrunning races of 1.5-2 hours reach up to Zone 4.

### [TUA-C12-006] Step 1: Athlete Category 1 vs Category 2

Choose honestly; two categories.
CATEGORY 1 (needs to BUILD a base; beginner/less experienced). Any one of:
- Has not averaged more than 400 hours per year of focused training for running or skimo during the past two years; or
- Is under age eighteen; or
- Has not, in the previous twelve months, successfully* completed races/events at the distance/duration aimed for in the next training period.
Most effective thing: increase training volume, ideally over a long off-season. If well below 400 hours/year, expect additional training cycles in base-building, especially juniors and the inexperienced. Do not skip to Category 2 too early or you will struggle with the workload and may not improve.
CATEGORY 2 (MAINTAIN and supplement an existing base). All of:
- Trained consistently at over 400 hours per year for at least the past two years (never really stops other than short breaks);
- Over age eighteen;
- In previous twelve months successfully* completed races/events at or near** the target distance/duration;
- Races several to many times in a year.
Biggest gains: more specificity (training that mimics event demands) and, as long as Zones 1–2 base volume is not appreciably reduced, more high intensity.
*Successfully: performed well, happy with result, achieved or came close to your goals, not destroyed or injured by the event.
**Near: successfully completed an event that is the next step below the targeted event in the conventional progression of difficulty or distance.

## C13 Contraindications, Cautions & Safety Limits

### [TUA-C13-001] Caution against copying elite athlete training

- WHAT: Matching or copying the training volume, technique, or specific workouts of world-class athletes (e.g., Kílian Jornet or featured elite runners).
- LIMIT: Avoid completely ("Blindly copying the workouts of world-class athletes is a bad idea").
- WHO: All athletes and self-coached readers.
- WHY: Elite training loads and intensities will overwhelm non-elite capacities, causing overtraining, injury, or burnout. Individual responses to identical training inputs vary substantially.
- EXCEPTION: Understand the theory and physiology behind elite workouts and apply the same principles scaled to your own level.

### [TUA-C13-002] Caution against rapid volume increases and non-specific coaching

- WHAT: Rapid training volume progression and training prescriptions from coaches or plans that do not understand the specific physiological demands of mountain sports.
- LIMIT: Volume must be layered on gradually; never increase volume abruptly.
- WHO: All athletes entering structured training.
- WHY: Overtraining from rapid volume layering causes systemic breakdown, immune suppression, and severe illness (exemplified by Steve House suffering complete physical breakdown, IV fluids, 21 days unable to eat, and weight dropping from 165 lb to 135 lb).
- EXCEPTION: None.

- High-intensity-dominated training: plateau then performance decline (loss of aerobic base). Limit HIIT to a supplement.

### [TUA-C13-004] Zone 3 annual volume restriction and Aerobic Deficiency caution

- WHAT: Zone 3 (Endurance / tempo intensity between AeT and LT) training volume.
- LIMIT: Must typically comprise no more than 10 percent of total annual training volume. If the Ten Percent Test shows a spread greater than 10 percent between AeT and LT, Zone 3 training should not be added; aerobic base (Z1–Z2) must be prioritized instead.
- WHO: All endurance athletes, particularly those suffering from Aerobic Deficiency Syndrome (ADS).
- WHY: Zone 3 is Utilization Training. Overuse provides rapid short-term fitness surges but degrades long-term aerobic capacity, rapidly leading to performance plateaus, chronic fatigue, and performance decline.
- EXCEPTION: Athletes who have closed the threshold spread to less than 10 percent (elite spread can be 6–7 percent or 10 bpm) must reduce or drop Zone 2 and substitute Zone 3 workouts to continue progressing.

### [TUA-C13-005] Zone 2 volume restriction for athletes with high Aerobic Threshold

- WHAT: Zone 2 (Aerobic Capacity) weekly volume and frequency.
- LIMIT: Maximum 1 to 2 sessions per week, with total Zone 2 volume strictly under 10 to 15 percent of weekly training volume.
- WHO: Well-trained athletes with a high AeT and fast running/movement pace at that threshold.
- WHY: Because of their high pace and mechanical power output at AeT, Zone 2 sessions impose substantial musculoskeletal and metabolic strain; performing Zone 2 daily risks systemic overtraining. These athletes must complete the bulk of their aerobic volume in Zone 1 and Recovery Zone.
- EXCEPTION: Beginners and ADS athletes whose pace at AeT is slow can perform virtually 100 percent of their base training in Zone 2 without excessive fatigue.

### [TUA-C13-006] Zone 5 sprint duration and repetition termination rule

- WHAT: Zone 5 maximum anaerobic sprinting / power intervals.
- LIMIT: Work bout duration must be under 30 to 45 seconds (early in the training cycle, strictly 10-second steep uphill sprints). Rest intervals must be at least 3 minutes to replenish ATP. Workout must be immediately terminated if a noticeable drop in power occurs after a few repetitions and longer recovery does not restore output.
- WHO: All athletes undertaking Zone 5 work.
- WHY: High neuromuscular and musculoskeletal loads create severe risk of acute injury (muscle strains, tendon pathology). Continuing repetitions after fast-twitch motor units have fatigued produces no further adaptation and significantly escalates injury risk.
- EXCEPTION: None. A solid strength base is an absolute prerequisite before undertaking high-volume Zone 5 work.

### [TUA-C13-007] Annual training volume progression caps

- WHAT: Annual training volume increases (measured in total training hours).
- LIMIT:
  - Beginners (<350–400 hours/year): maximum 25 percent increase per year.
  - Advanced athletes (>500 hours/year): maximum 10 percent increase per year.
  - Elite athletes (e.g., 1,000–1,300 hours/year): 0 percent increase in annual volume (gains achieved solely by modulating intensity, distribution, and event-specific work).
- WHO: Endurance athletes across all developmental levels.
- WHY: The structural and metabolic systems of the body require months to years of gradual exposure to absorb training stress. Exceeding these limits leads to structural breakdown, chronic injury, stagnation, or overtraining syndrome.
- EXCEPTION: None.

### [TUA-C13-008] Training interruption threshold for plan regression

- WHAT: Resuming structured progression following missed training.
- LIMIT: If training lapses (due to illness, work, travel, or injury) exceed 5 percent of the planned monthly volume, the athlete must not advance to the next scheduled training block; they must take a step back in the training plan.
- WHO: All athletes following a periodized plan.
- WHY: The physiological systems are unprepared for the escalated training load of the subsequent block; pretending the missed work occurred reliably triggers overtraining, acute fatigue, or injury setbacks.
- EXCEPTION: Isolated breaks of 1 to 2 days that reduce monthly training load by roughly 5 percent or less do not require plan regression.

### [TUA-C13-009] High-intensity training restriction for fast-twitch dominant athletes

- WHAT: Zone 4 (above Lactate Threshold) duration and repetition length.
- LIMIT: Avoid long continuous or extended Zone 4 interval sessions; restrict high-intensity repetitions to short durations under 2 minutes.
- WHO: Fast-twitch (FT) dominant athletes (identified by high sprint power, strong vertical jump, excessive lactate production during easy work, and poor peak fitness retention).
- WHY: FT-dominant athletes rapidly accumulate large concentrations of blood lactate; extended Zone 4 repetitions flood their working muscles with lactate, compromise aerobic adaptation, and precipitate severe fatigue.
- EXCEPTION: None. These athletes require substantially more low-intensity Zone 1–2 aerobic base work to suppress glycolytic reliance.

### [TUA-C13-010] Overtraining: early signs, symptoms and required response

- WHAT: overtraining syndrome. WHO: any endurance athlete under heavy load, higher risk with very high energy demands. WHY: breakdown of adaptation; endocrine and nervous systems no longer behave as expected; former light load becomes destructive, so hard/easy must be recalibrated. Family doctors may dismiss it as hypochondria; typical illness symptoms appear only in late stages.
- Early sign: repeated performance shortfalls with flatness/low energy. Common wrong response: adding more training, leading to a downward spiral with motivation and confidence costs. Do not assume lost fitness.
- Early effects (sympathetic): raised heart rate at submaximal effort, lack of pep, cortisol staying elevated between sessions (needs a doctor/blood test).
- Progression (parasympathetic): more negative hormonal effects and LOWERED heart rate at all effort levels.
- Symptoms to watch: persistent deep fatigue; prolonged elevated cortisol (blood test); lowered testosterone (blood test); decreased heart rate variability (special HR monitor or EKG); irritability; depression; weight loss; amenorrhea; insomnia; lowered libido; loss of enthusiasm and motivation.
- LIMIT/ACTION: at this profound neurological fatigue, at minimum several weeks of complete rest, then gradual reintroduction of easy exercise. Avoid at all costs; prevent by monitoring.
- Detection aid: easier to see on measured courses/paces; harder in mountain sports where terrain varies, so keep benchmark workouts to check progress or regression.
- Overuse injury indicator: loading a muscle before adequate recovery gives no fitness gain and accumulates small injuries; the muscle/brain connection self-inhibits contraction; a minor tear can become a major inflammatory cycle, scarring, chronic tendonitis or full tendon/muscle rupture. Not necessarily overtraining, but shows adaptation is not occurring and signals a collision course; take remedial steps. Focus on the bigger picture rather than numbers and times.

### [TUA-C14-001] VO2 max (relative aerobic power-to-weight ratio)

Formula:
$$\text{VO}_2\text{ max} = \frac{\text{Volume of } \text{O}_2 \text{ consumed per minute (mL)}}{\text{Body mass (kg)}} = \text{mL}\cdot\text{kg}^{-1}\cdot\text{min}^{-1}$$

Variables:
- Volume of $\text{O}_2$ consumed: maximum volume of oxygen an individual is capable of taking up and utilizing during intense exercise in milliliters per minute ($\text{mL/min}$).
- Body mass: total body weight in kilograms ($\text{kg}$).

Units: $\text{mL/kg/min}$. Represents an aerobic power-to-weight ratio.

### [TUA-C14-003] MAF formula (180 minus age)

### [TUA-C15-003] Seiler: Polarized Training

Stephen Seiler studied training logs of world-class athletes (cross-country skiing, running, cycling, rowing; Olympic gold medalists, world champions, Tour de France riders) and found similar distribution: ~80 percent of time below AeT, roughly 6 to 8 percent between AeT and LT, 12 to 14 percent above LT. Counterintuitive given Z3 benefits. Seiler coined "Polarized Training". Another study showed gains with polarized training in recreational runners.
Refs: Seiler, "What is the best practice for training intensity and duration distribution in endurance athletes?" Int J Sports Physiol Perform 2010 Vol 5; Seiler, "Does Polarized Training improve performance in recreational runners?" IJSPP 2014 Vol 9.

### [TUA-C15-005] Olbrecht: Recovery kinetics across training intensities

Jan Olbrecht (*The Science of Winning*, F&G Partners Publishing) collated empirical physiological recovery data from hundreds of swimmers over many years, establishing the recovery and supercompensation timelines reproduced in Figure 3.4:
- Low-intensity aerobic sessions (Zones 1 & 2, ≤2–3 hours) produce minor fatigue and achieve full recovery/supercompensation within 8 to 24 hours.
- Long-duration sessions (>2 hours), even at low intensities, require 24 to 48 hours for complete intramuscular glycogen restoration.
- Zone 3, Zone 4, Zone 5, and Strength Training produce progressively deeper performance depressions and require 48 to 72 hours (2 to 3 full days) of recovery before a similar training load can be effectively absorbed.

### [TUA-C15-006] Seiler interval study and Åstrand question

Åstrand (Textbook of Work Physiology, 1970) posed the unresolved question: 40 min at 90 percent of max oxygen uptake vs 16 min at 100 percent (Zone 4).
Stephan Seiler study: well-trained endurance athletes did 4×4, 4×8 and 4×16 minutes with 2-minute rest. 4×8 min gave significantly better endurance gains. Going as hard as sustainable, the length of the work period selected the intensity: 4-min periods at top of Zone 4-5, 8-min in Zone 4, 16-min in Zone 3.
Applies to well-trained/elite; less trained athletes with lower muscular endurance cannot sustain these intensities, so Zone 3 intervals and muscular endurance training are suggested first for most athletes.

## C17 Validity Notes

### [TUA-C17-001] Validity Notes

[VALIDITY NOTE — not from source] Author statement ("Ferritin" sidebar, Ch 4): standard low ferritin is 12 ng/ml; below 50 ng/ml reduces endurance performance and below 30 ng/ml gives dramatic loss. Current sports-medicine practice often uses thresholds around 30–35 ng/ml (some use up to 50 ng/ml) for iron deficiency in athletes. This range is broadly consistent but varies by organization; confirm clinically.

[VALIDITY NOTE — not from source] Author statement ("Recovery times", Ch 3): eat 100–200 calories within 30 minutes post exercise at a 3:1 or 4:1 carbohydrate to protein ratio. Sports-nutrition consensus (e.g. ACSM/AND/DC 2016) recommends higher amounts after prolonged efforts (about 1.0–1.2 g/kg/h carbohydrate), so this figure is a minimum for long or heavy sessions.

[VALIDITY NOTE — not from source] Author statement ("Sore muscles (DOMS)", Ch 4): ibuprofen after twelve hours post workout. Current evidence suggests NSAIDs may blunt training adaptation and carry kidney and GI risks during prolonged endurance exercise; medical advice is to use sparingly.

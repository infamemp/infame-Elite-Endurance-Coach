## Methodological Framework & Core Philosophy

The book's central premise is that power output in watts—measured directly, in real time, on the bicycle—is the definitive objective indicator of cycling performance. Heart rate is described as a lagging, confounded response to effort; RPE as a real-time but imprecise proxy. Power is "the intensity of your work," while heart rate is "the intensity of your intention." Together, they form a more complete picture than either alone.

The overarching coaching philosophy is derived from Joe Friel: "An athlete should do the least amount of the most specific training that brings continual improvement." The power meter is positioned as the tool that makes "least amount" and "most specific" quantifiable rather than guesswork.

Three pillars structure the methodology:

1. **Determine FTP**, which anchors all training zones, TSS, IF, and NP calculations.
2. **Develop a Power Profile and Power Duration Curve**, which characterizes the athlete's phenotype and reveals relative strengths and weaknesses across the full spectrum of physiological systems.
3. **Manage training load via the Performance Manager** (ATL, CTL, TSB), which allows coaches and athletes to predict when form will peak and to design taper protocols accordingly.

A central recurring maxim: "Training is testing; testing is training. Treat every training session as an opportunity to build toward a peak performance."

The authors explicitly state the book is not a general training manual: it does not prescribe periodization philosophy in detail or cover the full scope of exercise physiology. Its scope is specifically how to use power-meter data to enhance whatever training approach the athlete already employs.

### Evolution from Classic to Power Duration Model

The third edition introduces the Power Duration Model (developed in TrainingPeaks WKO4) as a refinement of the Power Profile approach. The key limitation of the earlier Power Profile was reliance on discrete, standardized test durations (5 sec, 1 min, 5 min, FTP) that real-world riding data rarely matches exactly. The Power Duration Model uses non-linear curve-fitting across all mean maximal power data to produce continuous estimates of performance capacity at any duration, enabling more ecologically valid profiling and the derivation of new metrics (Pmax, FRC, mFTP, TTE, Stamina).

## Physiological Foundations

### Lactate Threshold as the Performance Anchor

"For nearly 40 years, exercise physiologists have known that the exercise intensity at which lactate begins to accumulate in the blood, called lactate threshold, is a powerful predictor of performance, specifically endurance." LT determines what fraction of VO2max can be sustained and integrates fuel selection (carbohydrate vs. fat) and fatigue development. It is described as "the single most important physiological determinant of performance in events ranging from something as short as a 3 km pursuit to a stage race lasting as long as three weeks."

FTP is the field-practical expression of LT: "the highest power that a rider can maintain in a quasi–steady state without fatiguing. When power exceeds FTP, fatigue will occur much sooner (generally after approximately one hour in well-trained cyclists), whereas power just below FTP can be maintained considerably longer."

### Energy Systems and Their Duration Associations (Power Profile context)

The Power Profile uses four target durations to represent four physiological systems, with the caveat that no effort is purely reliant on one system:

| Duration | Primary System Represented | Caveats |
|----------|---------------------------|---------|
| 5 seconds | Neuromuscular power / ATP-PCr | Small aerobic contribution even at 5 sec |
| 1 minute | Anaerobic capacity | ~40–45% aerobic contribution at 1 min |
| 5 minutes | VO2max | Most athletes sustain ~105–110% VO2max for 5 min |
| ~60 minutes (FTP) | Lactate threshold | Sustainable range is ~30–75 min depending on individual |

### Fiber-Type Recruitment and Quadrant Analysis

Functional threshold power represents "not only a threshold in terms of the power that an athlete can sustain, but also somewhat of a threshold in terms of fast-twitch fiber recruitment." Below FTP at self-selected cadence, predominantly slow-twitch (Type I) fibers are used. As power crosses FTP, significant Type IIa recruitment begins, and at supra-maximal intensities, Type IIx fibers are recruited. This forms the physiological basis for the Quadrant Analysis boundaries (AEPF threshold line drawn at FTP cadence and force).

### Carbohydrate Metabolism and kJ/kcal Relationship

Mechanical efficiency for trained cyclists is approximately 20–25%. Because of canceling conversion factors (4.184 kJ/kcal divided by 4–5 due to efficiency), total work in kJ is a practical 1:1 approximation for kilocalorie expenditure. The authors note this is an approximation and the true ratio depends on individual efficiency.

### Power vs. Strength

Strength (maximal force at zero velocity) rarely limits cycling performance. In analysis of multiple subjects' maximal AEPF-CPV lines, even during maximal sprint efforts peak AEPF was only ~55% of the theoretical maximum, and during time trialing ~25% of maximal AEPF. Strength endurance training at very low cadences (45 rpm) generates AEPF levels below 50% of the athlete's velocity-specific maximum force, which the authors argue is insufficient to cause meaningful hypertrophy or meaningful Type II fiber overload. Scientific studies using isokinetic dynamometry confirm no increase in maximal force production from such training.

## Intensity Model & Zone System

### Coggan Classic Training Levels (7-Zone System)

All levels defined relative to functional threshold power (FTP). Heart rate values are approximate guidelines due to HR variability. RPE uses Borg's 10-point CR scale (not the 20-point scale).

| Zone | Name | % FTP | HR (% FTHR) | RPE (0–10) | Typical Duration |
|------|------|-------|-------------|------------|-----------------|
| 1 | Active Recovery | <55% | <68% | <2 | Up to several hours |
| 2 | Endurance | 56–75% | 69–83% | 2–3 | 2–8+ hours |
| 3 | Tempo | 76–90% | 84–94% | 3–4 | 20 min – 2 hours |
| 4 | Lactate Threshold | 91–105% | 95–105% | 4–5 | 8–60 min (continuous) |
| 5 | VO2max | 106–120% | >106% | 6–7 | 3–8 min per interval |
| 6 | Anaerobic Capacity | 121–150% | N/A (too short) | >7 | 30 sec – 2 min |
| 7 | Neuromuscular Power | >150% | N/A | Maximal | <10 sec |

**Key notes from author:**
- Average power during a workout or series of intervals, not instantaneous power, determines the training level characterization.
- Level 3 (Tempo) is "the meat and potatoes of every cyclist" and yields "the best bang for the buck" but should not be the sole focus; it will not raise VO2max.
- Within-level variability is already factored into definitions, especially for Levels 2 and 3.
- The RPE values refer to effort "as determined relatively early in a training session or series of intervals" since RPE drifts upward even at constant power.

### Primary Physiological Adaptations by Level

| Zone | Primary Adaptations |
|------|---------------------|
| 1 | Recovery, glycogen repletion, waste product clearance |
| 2 | Mitochondrial biogenesis, capillary development, fat oxidation, cardiac hypertrophy |
| 3 | Muscular endurance, raised LT, carbohydrate utilization efficiency |
| 4 | Raises FTP directly; recruits both fiber types at threshold; glycogen flux training |
| 5 | Increases VO2max; cardiac output improvements; fast-twitch aerobic capacity |
| 6 | Anaerobic capacity expansion; PCr and glycolytic system development |
| 7 | Maximal neuromuscular power; motor unit recruitment; rate of force development |

### Sweet Spot (Lower Level 4 / Level 4a)

Defined at "about 88–94 percent of your functional threshold power—on the cusp of both the Tempo level and the Lactate Threshold level." The authors recommend heavy Sweet Spot emphasis at the start of the racing season before moving to pure threshold work: "one of the most beneficial places to spend your training time." It is distinct enough to warrant its own iLevel designation (iLevel 4a).

### iLevels (Individualized Power Levels)

Nine iLevels that replace the Coggan Classic above Level 4 using inflection points on the rider's individual Power Duration Curve. iLevels 1–4 remain percentage-based and nearly identical to Classic levels; iLevels 5–7 are athlete-specific wattages derived from the PDC model.

| iLevel | Name | Determining Metric | Duration Range |
|--------|------|-------------------|---------------|
| 1 | Active Recovery | <55% mFTP | Unlimited |
| 2 | Endurance | 56–75% mFTP | Long |
| 3 | Tempo | 76–90% mFTP | Moderate-long |
| 4 | Lactate Threshold | 91–105% mFTP | 8–60 min |
| 4a | Sweet Spot | 88–95% mFTP | 10–40 min |
| 5 | FRC/FTP Blend | PDC inflection: FTP-to-FRC transition | 1:33–~17 min (athlete-specific) |
| 6 | FRC | PDC inflection: pure FRC | 9 sec–1:33 (athlete-specific) |
| 7a | Pmax/FRC Blend | PDC inflection: FRC-to-Pmax transition | 9–28 sec (athlete-specific) |
| 7 | Pmax | PDC: single maximal pedal revolution | <9 sec |

The key distinction: above Level 4, some athletes (e.g., those capable of 150% FTP for 5 minutes) require dramatically different wattage targets than the Classic percentages would prescribe. iLevels correct for this individual variation.

## Training Principles & Periodization

### FTP as Universal Anchor

Because IF = NP/FTP and TSS = duration(hrs) × IF² × 100, an inaccurate FTP cascades into all load calculations. A 4% underestimate in FTP causes ~8% overestimate in TSS. The authors repeatedly stress FTP must be re-tested every 6–8 weeks and immediately updated after major fitness changes.

### Intervals-to-Exhaustion Principle

Optimal interval count is determined by the power drop-off from the third interval. The third interval is used as the "repeatable" baseline (the first two are performed with greater glycogen reserves and anaerobic work capacity):

- Stop when you can no longer maintain ≥95% of the third interval's power (i.e., a ~5% drop-off), for efforts of 3–8 minutes (VO2max range).
- For Level 6 (Anaerobic Capacity): stop when average drops below ~88–90% of third interval's power (~10–12% drop).

### Performance Manager: Form = Fitness + Freshness

Derived from Banister's impulse-response model but simplified by removing gain factors (ka, kf) and substituting exponentially weighted moving averages:

**Chronic Training Load (CTL)** = exponentially weighted moving average of daily TSS, default time constant τ = 42 days. Represents accumulated fitness; primarily reflects training from the past ~90 days.

**Acute Training Load (ATL)** = exponentially weighted moving average of daily TSS, default time constant τ = 7 days. Represents recent fatigue; primarily reflects the past ~14 days.

**Training Stress Balance (TSB)** = CTL − ATL. Positive TSB = more freshness; negative TSB = more fatigue.

"Form equals fitness plus freshness." Optimal TSB for racing depends on event type:
- Short anaerobic events (track, BMX, <5 min): TSB +5 to +25 (highly positive freshness needed).
- Longer aerobic events (road race, TT, stage race): TSB −10 to +25 (fitness dominates; some carry-over fatigue acceptable).
- Personal bests across all durations occurred most frequently at TSB −5 to +15.

**CTL management guidelines:**
- "Optimal" training range for most serious racers: CTL 100–150 TSS/day.
- Safe CTL ramp rate: 3–7 TSS/day/week. Exceeding 7 TSS/day/week for >4 consecutive weeks risks non-functional overreaching.
- Tour de France hardest stages: TSS 200–300 per stage.

**ATL time constant adjustment by age and recovery context:**

| Athlete Context | Suggested ATL τ |
|----------------|----------------|
| Young rider, below CTL ceiling | 5 days |
| Default | 7 days |
| Masters athlete, near CTL ceiling | 10 days |
| Older masters (55+), targeting short anaerobic events | 10–14 days |

### Periodization Structure (Case Study Evidence)

The book presents a "3 weeks on, 1 week off" as a common default but also presents a "5 weeks on, 1 week off, 1 week on, 1 week off" variant for athletes with limited time. The periodization framework is classical (base → build → peak → taper) but driven by TSS/CTL rather than arbitrary hour targets. The Performance Manager Chart provides the macro view; individual workout execution provides the micro view. Both are required.

### Specificity Principle

Quadrant Analysis is used to verify that training neuromuscularly matches race demands. A triathlete who only races criteriums to prepare for Ironman will not be ready neuromuscularly (too much QI, not enough QIII/QIV). A mountain biker who trains only on road will underemphasize QII (high force, low cadence) that MTB racing requires. The authors provide explicit QA comparisons between training files and race files for MTB, road, criterium, and track events.

## Athlete Assessment & Testing Protocols

### FTP Testing Protocol (Hunter Allen 20-Minute Test)

**Recommended test frequency:** Every 6–8 weeks, same road/trainer, same time of day, same weather, post-rest week.

| Segment | Duration | Intensity | Notes |
|---------|----------|-----------|-------|
| Warm-up | 15 min | Level 2 (~65% FTP) | |
| Fast pedaling × 3 | 3 × 1 min | 100 rpm+; not maximal power | 1 min recovery between |
| Easy spin | 5 min | Level 2 | |
| All-out effort | 5 min | Maximal; not quite all-out in first 30 sec | "Opens legs," depletes FRC for cleaner 20-min test |
| Recovery | 5 min | Easy | |
| 20-minute time trial | 20 min | Absolute maximal sustainable effort | Stay even or negative-split; start conservatively |
| Cooldown | 10–15 min | Level 1 | |

**FTP calculation:** Average 20-min watts × 0.95 = estimated FTP.

Qualifying notes: The 5% deduction compensates for anaerobic contribution during a 20-minute effort that does not exist during a true 60-minute FTP effort. Riders with high anaerobic capacity may need to subtract 7%+; purely aerobic riders may need only 2–3%.

### Power Profile Test (Ch. 4)

Performed on same road on the same day or across multiple sessions (4 days for bilateral testing). Requires flat or same section each time.

| Test | Duration | Protocol |
|------|----------|---------|
| Neuromuscular Power | 20 sec sprint × 3 | Out of saddle; all-out; 3 min between; best 5-sec avg extracted |
| Anaerobic Capacity | 2 × 1 min all-out | Standing start; 5 min between |
| VO2max | 1 × 5 min all-out | Maximum sustainable effort |
| FTP | 20 min TT minus 5% | As above |

Results expressed as W/kg and compared to Power Profile table benchmarks (World Class → Novice categories).

### Pedaling Asymmetry Hill Climbing Test (Ch. 7)

Four identical test days on same course, same start location. Three 5-min efforts per day at Level 5 (~113–115% FTP):

| Day | Protocol |
|-----|---------|
| 1 | Natural climbing; no emphasis on either leg |
| 2 | Emphasize the leg that released less GPR on Day 1 |
| 3 | Emphasize left leg only for all 3 efforts |
| 4 | Emphasize right leg only for all 3 efforts |

Analyze GPR (Gross Power Released) and GPA (Gross Power Absorbed) Mean Maximal Power Curves after each day. Determines whether discrepancy is muscular, positional, or movement-pattern-based.

## Nutrition & Recovery

### Kilojoule-to-Kilocalorie Estimation

The authors use a direct 1:1 approximation (kJ = kcal) for practical purposes. Exact thermodynamic efficiency (20–25%) makes the true conversion 4.184 kJ/kcal ÷ 0.20–0.25 ≈ 1.0 kJ/kcal as a net approximation. "Although the exact relationship between kJ and kcal is not one to one, it probably is not worth worrying about any error this assumption creates."

### Using TSS and kJ for Nutrition Planning

Downloaded kJ expenditure is used to plan in-race and post-ride caloric intake. The case of Sami Srour demonstrates the protocol: Hunter calculated kJ per segment of a metric century, established per-segment intake goals for carbohydrates plus electrolyte replacement, and built a post-ride recovery protocol matching carbohydrate, protein, and fat to measured expenditure to accelerate glycogen repletion.

### Glycogen Conservation in Triathlon

Quadrant Analysis is used to verify race execution. Spending excessive time in Quadrant II (high force, low cadence) depletes muscle glycogen faster due to greater Type II fiber recruitment and higher per-contraction glycogen cost. Athletes targeting Ironman should aim for >70% of pedaling time in QII + QIII (low force), with Variability Index 1.04–1.07.

### CTL-Based Overtraining Guidance

If ATL is greater than CTL for too long without recovery, the athlete enters non-functional over-reaching and eventually overtraining syndrome. The authors define the risk threshold as sustained CTL ramp rates >7 TSS/day/week for >4 weeks. Signs include: TSB chronically negative with no performance improvement, declining mean maximal power across durations, persistent fatigue even after rest days.

## Special Populations & Conditions

### Masters Athletes (40+)

- VO2max declines ~0.5 ml/kg/min/year for men from age 30; ~0.35 ml/kg/min/year for women.
- Muscular strength and power maintainable until ~age 50, then decline more rapidly.
- The authors recommend against separate age-adjusted Power Profile tables; apply Table 4.1 as-is but interpret within age context.
- ATL time constant adjustment for age: masters riders (45–55) should use τ = 10 days; older masters τ = 14 days. Recovery from equivalent training stress takes longer.
- Target CTL and ramp rates should be reduced for older masters approaching their ceiling.

### Triathletes

- Triathlon is predominantly slow-twitch aerobic sport. Level 7 training has limited value except on highly variable courses (e.g., Ironman Wisconsin's 100+ hills).
- The 20-minute FTP test is conducted on the triathlon bike in aerobars since bike-leg performance is position-specific.
- Running TSS (rTSS) is calculated on a scale where 45 min of running at FTp ≈ 100 rTSS (more stressful than cycling per unit time due to musculoskeletal impact loading).
- Performance Manager Charts are built separately for cycling, running, and combined. All three must be consulted to plan a triathlon peak.

### Cyclocross

- Average power in CX races typically 20–40 W below FTP due to coasting, running sections, and traction loss on loose terrain.
- Quadrant Analysis of CX races shows primarily QII (high force, low cadence from dismounts and remounts) and QIII/QIV (recovery phases).
- The "30-30-30" workout directly addresses CX's unique demand profile.
- FTP intervals (4 × 10 min, 3 × 15 min, or 2 × 20 min per week) remain essential base work alongside the 30-30-30.

### Track Cycling

- NP is difficult to interpret for events shorter than a few minutes; TSS calculation is valid but requires deleting coasting/non-pedaling time to avoid inflation.
- CdA field testing on the velodrome is recommended; reproducibility <2% is achievable under low-wind conditions.
- Gear selection during standing-start training: within broad limits, gear ratio does not affect AEPF at any given cadence. Forces are primarily determined by muscle contractile properties, not gear choice.
- Team pursuit exchange analysis: dropping too early (overlapping third rider's wheel) is no more costly energetically than a perfect exchange. Dropping too late (losing draft and needing to accelerate back on) is significantly more costly.

### Ultra-Endurance Mountain Biking

- The "Allen Effect": creating a distance gap in early laps accumulates because descending speed differences are exponentially larger than ascending speed differences. Going harder than sustainable pace for the first 4–6 hours in a 24-hour race is tactically sound.
- Data from 24-hour pro Dave Harris confirms consistent NP across the first 4 laps before gradual decline; establishing a 45-minute gap by hour 6 typically holds for remaining 18 hours.
- Jeremiah Bishop's Shenandoah 100 record: 6 hrs 51 min, NP 274 W, TSS 356, FTP 390 W (IF 0.70). Comparable TSS to a Tour de France mountain stage.

## Terminology Glossary

| Term | Author's Definition | First-Occurrence Context |
|------|---------------------|--------------------------|
| Functional Threshold Power (FTP) | "The highest power that a rider can maintain in a quasi–steady state without fatiguing. When power exceeds FTP, fatigue will occur much sooner (generally after approximately one hour in well-trained cyclists), whereas power just below FTP can be maintained considerably longer." | Ch. 3 |
| Normalized Power (NP) | The fourth-root of the time-averaged fourth power of 30-second rolling average power; "the power that your body thought it was doing" over a variable effort; estimates metabolic cost as if power had been perfectly constant. Calculated: (1) 30-sec rolling average; (2) raise to 4th power; (3) average all values; (4) take 4th root. | Ch. 7 |
| Intensity Factor (IF) | Ratio of Normalized Power to FTP (IF = NP / FTP). Quantifies relative intensity of any ride or segment relative to the athlete's own threshold. | Ch. 7 |
| Training Stress Score (TSS) | A score for each workout accounting for both duration and intensity: TSS = [(s × W × IF) ÷ (FTP × 3,600)] × 100, where s = seconds, W = Normalized Power. A 1-hour ride at FTP = 100 TSS. | Ch. 7 |
| Variability Index (VI) | Normalized Power ÷ Average Power. Quantifies power variability; higher VI indicates more stochastic effort. Coined by Charles Howe. | Ch. 7 |
| Chronic Training Load (CTL) | Exponentially weighted 42-day moving average of daily TSS; represents accumulated fitness from chronic training. | Ch. 9 |
| Acute Training Load (ATL) | Exponentially weighted 7-day moving average of daily TSS; represents recent fatigue from acute training. | Ch. 9 |
| Training Stress Balance (TSB) | CTL minus ATL; represents the balance between fitness and freshness; "form." | Ch. 9 |
| Power Profile | A method of characterizing an athlete's relative strengths and weaknesses across four test durations (5 sec, 1 min, 5 min, FTP) expressed as W/kg and plotted against category benchmarks from Novice to World Class. | Ch. 4 |
| Phenotype | "A physical expression of a genetic characteristic." In power-meter context: the pattern of the Power Profile characterizing a cyclist as all-rounder, sprinter, time trialist, or pursuiter. | Ch. 4, Ch. 8 |
| Power Duration Curve (PDC) | A mathematically fitted curve of best-fit through the Mean Maximal Power data across all durations; models the complete power-duration relationship using non-linear curve-fitting. | Ch. 4, 8 |
| Mean Maximal Power (MMP) | "Your best average power for each second of the ride"; a plot of maximum average power across all test durations from raw ride data. | Ch. 6 |
| Pmax | "The greatest amount of power that can be generated for a very short period of time with a minimum of one full pedal revolution with both legs." The PDC-derived maximal neuromuscular power metric. | Ch. 8 |
| Functional Reserve Capacity (FRC) | "The total amount of work that you can do above your FTP." Expressed in kilojoules; represents the anaerobic/non-oxidative energy reserve available above FTP. Analogous to a battery: finite charge, depletes faster at higher supra-FTP intensities. | Ch. 8 |
| Modeled FTP (mFTP) | The power level at which the Power Duration Curve flattens horizontally; the software-derived FTP estimate from the PDC model. May correspond to 30–75 min maximal effort depending on the individual. | Ch. 8 |
| Time to Exhaustion (TTE) | The maximal duration the mFTP power level can be sustained; varies between ~30 min (low TTE) to >60 min (high TTE). Predicts mFTP improvement when approaching or exceeding 1 hour. | Ch. 8 |
| Stamina | "A measure of resistance to fatigue during prolonged-duration, moderate-intensity exercise." Expressed as a percentage; refers to the flatness of the tail end of the PDC beyond mFTP. Higher % = more fatigue-resistant below FTP. Most athletes: 75–85%. | Ch. 8 |
| Quadrant Analysis (QA) | A scatterplot of Average Effective Pedal Force (AEPF) vs. Circumferential Pedal Velocity (CPV), divided into four quadrants by the AEPF and CPV values at FTP and self-selected cadence. Reveals neuromuscular demands and fiber-type recruitment patterns. | Ch. 7 |
| AEPF (Average Effective Pedal Force) | Force (Newtons) tangentially applied to the crank over 360°: AEPF = (P × 60) ÷ (C × 2 × π × CL), where P = power, C = cadence, CL = crank length. | Ch. 7 |
| CPV (Circumferential Pedal Velocity) | Speed (m/s) at which the pedal moves around its circular path: CPV = (C × CL × 2 × π) ÷ 60. Proxy for muscle shortening velocity. | Ch. 7 |
| Match | "An effort in which one goes over threshold power by at least 20 percent and holds it there for at least 1 minute." Longer durations require proportionally less excess above FTP. Finite number available per ride; timing of match burning is tactical. | Ch. 6 |
| Maximal Power Available (MPA) | Xert's real-time fatigue-tracking metric representing how much power a rider can generate at any given moment during a ride; declines during supra-threshold effort and recovers during sub-threshold periods. | Ch. 6 |
| Gross Power Released (GPR) | "The gross (muscular + inertial + gravitational) power released by one leg (left or right), primarily on the downstroke." Bilateral metric from Ch. 7. | Ch. 7 |
| Gross Power Absorbed (GPA) | "The gross (muscular + inertial + gravitational) power absorbed by one leg (left or right), primarily during the upstroke." | Ch. 7 |
| Kurtotic Index (KI) | "A measure of the 'peakedness' of the pattern of force, torque, and power application during the power-producing phase." Higher KI = more of a "stomper." | Ch. 7 |
| Strength Endurance Training | Big-gear intervals at abnormally low cadence (45–75 rpm) at moderate-to-high power. The authors demonstrate via Quadrant Analysis that AEPF during such efforts remains below 50% of velocity-specific maximal force, insufficient to cause meaningful muscular hypertrophy. | Ch. 7 |
| rTSS (Running Training Stress Score) | Developed by McGregor; a TSS-equivalent for running using normalized graded pace (NGP) and functional threshold pace (FTp). Approximately 45 min running at FTp = 100 rTSS (more than cycling equivalent due to musculoskeletal impact). | Ch. 12 |
| Allen Effect | In ultra-endurance MTB, the phenomenon whereby a gap created on an ascent amplifies exponentially on the descent due to high speed differentials; attacking hard in the first 4–6 hours creates a gap that accumulates and is essentially impossible for trailing riders to close even as the leader slows later. | Ch. 14 |
| Power Calibration | A training protocol for triathletes to anchor RPE to specific power outputs through repeated 10-min intervals at race-specific intensities. "Power calibrates perceived exertion, and perceived exertion modulates power." | Ch. 12 |
| Phenotypic Map | A 2D scatter plot of Pmax/FTP (X-axis) vs. FRC/Pmax (Y-axis) for characterizing an athlete's phenotype with greater nuance than the 1D Power Profile. Allows visualization of athlete movement between phenotypic categories over time. | Ch. 8 |

## Detected Internal Inconsistencies

**1. FTP duration definition vs. mFTP duration variability**
Chapter 3 defines FTP as "the highest power that a rider can maintain in a quasi–steady state without fatiguing" with a practical standard of one hour. Chapter 8 states the mFTP may correspond to 30–75 minutes depending on the individual, and the TTE metric specifically quantifies this variation. The book resolves this partially by arguing the one-hour standard is a population average and the PDC model's mFTP is the more accurate individualized expression, but coaches using the Coggan Classic framework are operating under an implicit 60-minute assumption that the Power Duration Model disavows.

**2. The 5% subtraction from 20-minute test**
Chapter 3 states that subtracting 5% from the 20-minute test is "a general rule" and provides the caveat that high-anaerobic athletes may need 7%+ and purely aerobic athletes only 2–3%. The 5% figure is simultaneously presented as universally applicable (it appears in all worked examples) and as an approximation that varies substantially by individual. The downstream coaching project must determine appropriate subtraction per athlete.

**3. Level 4 (91–105%) vs. Sweet Spot (88–93%) boundary overlap**
The iLevels section adds Level 4a (Sweet Spot) at 88–95%, which overlaps with both the top of Level 3 (76–90%) and the bottom of Level 4 (91–105%). The overlap at 88–90% means an effort in this range is simultaneously classified as upper Level 3 in the Classic system and as Sweet Spot / lower Level 4a in the iLevel system. Both cannot be primary without creating a gap or overlap in zone assignments.

**4. Strength endurance and Type II fiber recruitment**
Chapter 7 argues that strength endurance training (big-gear low-cadence intervals) generates AEPF below 50% of velocity-specific maximum force, implying insufficient Type II fiber recruitment to provide meaningful overload. Chapter 5, however, lists "big-gear power efforts" as a prescribed remedy for Bob Rider's neuromuscular power weakness, explicitly targeting muscular power improvement. The theoretical critique of strength endurance training and the practical recommendation to do big-gear intervals are in tension and not explicitly reconciled.

**5. Winning road racers pedal less vs. optimal work rate**
Chapter 1 asserts that "the racers who consistently win are also the ones who do not pedal as much as the rest of the peloton." Chapter 5 and Chapter 13 both emphasize maximizing pedaling time and creating breakaways through sustained high-wattage efforts. These perspectives are tactically compatible (save energy by not pedaling in the pack; go hard when it counts) but the Ch. 1 framing is presented as a general fact without the crucial "when to pedal" qualifier that Chapters 5 and 13 supply.

## Cross-Reference Notes

- **FTP and TSS accuracy dependency:** TSS = duration(hrs) × IF² × 100, and IF = NP/FTP. TSS scales as the inverse square of FTP. A 4% FTP underestimate → ~8% TSS overestimate, which compounds into CTL/ATL/TSB miscalculations. Ch. 9 warns coaches to watch for IF > 1.05 on ~1-hour races as an indicator that FTP needs updating.

- **Normalized Power algorithm:** The four-step calculation (30-sec rolling average → 4th power → mean → 4th root) is presented in Ch. 7. The choice of the 4th power is justified by the curvilinear (non-linear) relationship between exercise intensity and physiological responses such as glycogen utilization and lactate production.

- **PDC vs. MMP Curve distinction:** The MMP Curve is raw best-power data; the PDC is the smoothed mathematical curve of best fit. Both are needed for training analysis. A "dip" in the MMP below the PDC indicates either a physiological weakness or a gap in test data for that duration. The authors recommend specific testing to distinguish the two.

- **iLevels are software-dependent:** iLevels (and mFTP, FRC, Pmax, TTE, Stamina) require TrainingPeaks WKO4 or equivalent PDC-modeling software. They cannot be calculated from manual testing alone. The Coggan Classic levels require only FTP and can be applied with any power meter and basic spreadsheet.

- **Triathlon power calibration and race execution:** The mathematical pacing formula (Ch. 12) requires knowing FTP from a bike-position-specific test and using Normalized Power (not average power) as the race target because course variability would cause average power to undershoot NP pacing goals on hilly courses.

- **Aerodynamic testing methods:** Three field methods for CdA determination described in Ch. 13 (constant-speed, regression, virtual elevation/Chung method). Precision under ideal conditions is <2% coefficient of variation, comparable to wind-tunnel testing. Requires windless conditions, typically early morning.

- **ATL time constant customization for event type:** Ch. 9 explicitly states that athletes targeting short anaerobic events should use longer ATL τ (10–14 days) to ensure true fatigue dissipation is captured. Athletes targeting long aerobic events should use shorter ATL τ (3–5 days) to train closer to race day without over-tapering.

- **rTSS integration for triathlon PMC:** Three Performance Manager Charts are required for triathlon: cycling-only, running-only, and combined. Swimming is not currently quantifiable in this framework (acknowledged in Ch. 12). Peak performance coordination requires all three sport-specific PMCs to be analyzed simultaneously.

## Chapter 6 — Data Interpretation: Expanded Coverage

### Interval Shape Analysis

The shape of a power line during an interval encodes pacing information and physiological capacity. The following patterns are explicitly described with accompanying case material:

**Twin-peak interval:** Power high at start, dips at ~1 minute, rises at end. Indicates conservative but correct pacing — athlete started at sustainable pace, discovered reserve, finished strong. "This is a textbook example of creating your best average watts in an interval."

**Front-heavy collapse:** Power starts very high, drops rapidly, never recovers. Indicates start was too fast, anaerobic work capacity depleted early. Athlete "blows up." Characteristic of Interval 2 in the Canadian track racer case study.

**Flat line with final kick:** Power is consistent throughout, small terminal surge. The ideal well-paced interval for threshold or VO2max work.

**Mid-interval power hole:** Power drops noticeably at a specific time mid-interval (e.g., at 1:17 into a 3-minute effort), then partially recovers. Indicates the energy system transition from anaerobic capacity to VO2max oxidative metabolism. The "hole" occurs when fast-twitch fibers partially fatigue before slow-twitch fibers can fully compensate. Training prescription: extend interval duration to push the transition point later; practice pacing to smooth through the transition.

**Late-interval power fade:** Power gradually declines from a sustainable start. Indicates accumulated glycogen depletion or rising muscle acidosis. Common in longer threshold intervals when rider is not fully recovered or has incomplete glycogen stores.

### Canadian Track Racer Case Study (Ch. 6) — Pedagogical Extract

Seven 3-minute intervals on indoor trainer. Goal: VO2max power (315–325W for this athlete). No wattage target given intentionally:

| Interval | Shape | Interpretation |
|----------|-------|----------------|
| 1 | Twin peak; conservative start, strong finish | Too cautious but correct structure; clearly had more reserve |
| 2 | Hard start, sharp collapse | Overconfident from Interval 1; anaerobic capacity depleted |
| 3–6 | Consistent twin peaks with power "hole" at ~1:17 | Correct pacing found; hole indicates fast-twitch fatigue at transition |
| 7 | Similar to Interval 1; conservative, large reserve | Could have done more intervals; did not reach exhaustion threshold |

Coaching conclusion: (1) Practice pacing from the start; (2) focus specifically on the 1:00–2:00 window of 3-minute efforts to eliminate the power hole; (3) the athlete had good repeat power (low interval-to-interval drop-off), good overall pursuit power, but insufficient depth at the energy system transition zone.

The temporal evolution of time-in-power-levels reveals whether periodization is executing correctly:

| Season Phase | Expected Power Distribution Pattern |

| Off-season / Base | Predominantly Levels 1–2; long flat bars in lower watts |

Time-in-level overstates the training effect of intermittent high-power efforts; Normalized Power is a more accurate physiological load indicator.

## Chapter 11 — Tracking Changes in Fitness: Expanded Coverage

### Mean Maximal Power Periodic Chart

The MMP Periodic Chart plots peak power for a given time period (e.g., 5 sec, 1 min, 5 min, 20 min) as a single data point per ride across a season or multi-season span. When smoothed to 7-day rolling peaks, trends emerge:

- **Early-season peak in 5-second power:** Appears consistently in the Jack three-year case study approximately four weeks after completion of winter weight training. The authors note this as a recurring empirical pattern worth tracking.
- **Double peaks in FTP:** Common when an athlete has two competitive goals separated by illness, crash, or a deliberately planned off-period mid-season.
- **Altitude compensation:** Races at altitude will produce lower wattage than sea level performances at equivalent effort. Adjust comparisons accordingly (the book provides a worked pursuit comparison: same pacing at 8,000 ft elevation = equivalent performance to sea level after aerodynamic and aerobic corrections).

### MMP Curve Shape Reading (Ch. 11)

| Curve Feature | Physiological Interpretation |
|--------------|------------------------------|
| Steep initial slope (0–5 sec), then flatter | Strong neuromuscular power; typical sprinter |
| Very flat from 1–8 min | High VO2max relative to overall power curve; characteristic all-rounder or pursuiter |
| Clear slope change at ~2–3 min | Transition from anaerobic capacity domain to VO2max domain |
| Plateau or "hump" at 3–6 min | Specific VO2max power strength |
| Dip (lower wattage at one duration than adjacent durations) | Data gap: no maximal effort collected at that specific duration; not a true weakness |
| Flat tail from ~45 min onward | High fatigue resistance; strong stamina |

**MMP vs. PDC distinction:** The MMP curve represents actual best data with day-to-day noise. The PDC is the smoothed mathematical best-fit. A dip in MMP below PDC at a given duration indicates either a true physiological weakness or a test gap — specific testing is required to distinguish the two cases before prescribing targeted training.

**Normalized Power as the upper MMP line:** Beyond ~60 minutes, mean maximal NP exceeds mean maximal average power (because NP accounts for non-pedaling time). At these durations, the NP line better represents the athlete's true aerobic capacity, since average power is depressed by coasting and soft-pedaling inherent in longer mass-start ride files.

### WAC Score (Ch. 10)

The WAC (Williams, Allen, Coggan) score assigns the rider a percentage of the world champion's best wattage for each duration, corrected for age. Expressed as a percentile or absolute score:
- Bill Masters peak WAC score: 74 (at ~22:48 duration; performs at 74% of world champion at that duration in his age category)
- Bill Masters WAC at 5 hours: drops to 31 (43% degradation from peak), confirming endurance as his primary limiter for a long gran fondo

The WAC score serves a similar function to the Power Profile but with age correction and expressed relative to absolute world-best rather than categorical benchmarks.

## Chapter 13 — Racing Applications: Expanded Coverage

### Criterium: Sit-In and Pacing Strategy (Ch. 13)

The criterium case study demonstrates how FTP knowledge governs in-race decisions. Key scenario: athlete in a breakaway realizing he is producing 500W on pulls and 400W in the recovery line, both above his 350W FTP:

"He reasoned that if he was just sitting in on the break and over his FTP, then his time in the break was limited." Decision: stop pulling entirely, sit on, preserve FRC for a sprint. This is presented as tactically optimal even under social pressure from breakaway companions.

Post-prime solo: athlete at ~45 seconds off the front held ~350W (100% FTP) for the remainder of the race. "He inched it up to the 360–370 range for the finish" — approximately 103–106% FTP for the last 4–5 minutes, which falls at the edge of Level 4/5 and represents the typical maximal sustainable effort for a well-trained rider over a 3–5 min terminal push.

**Practical rule stated:** Riding in a breakaway at or above FTP unsustainably → sit on immediately rather than burning matches that will be needed for the sprint.

### Time Trial — Headwind/Tailwind Asymmetry (Ch. 13)

On a course with symmetrical headwind and tailwind legs, the majority of time advantage comes from the headwind leg. The text provides a worked example:

| Scenario | Rider A (FTP 340W) | Rider B (FTP 320W) |
|----------|---------------------|---------------------|
| Tailwind 20km speed | 32.5 mph | 32.1 mph |
| Tailwind time difference (20km) | +17 sec for Rider A | — |
| Headwind 20km speed | 22 mph | 20.5 mph |
| Headwind time difference (20km) | +194 sec for Rider A | — |

Conclusion: "In fact, the time trial could be won in the headwind." Riders with better FTP should expend proportionally more energy on headwind sections; both riders effectively equalize on tailwind sections regardless of fitness.

### Individual Pursuit — Aerobic/Anaerobic Ratio Analysis (Ch. 13)

By comparing the area under measured power output versus theoretical maximal aerobic power output (from lab VO2max and efficiency data), the split between aerobic and anaerobic contribution can be estimated:

| Rider | Event | Aerobic % | Anaerobic % | MAOD Estimate |
|-------|-------|-----------|-------------|---------------|
| Rider A (masters road racer, poor sprint) | 3km pursuit | 80% | 20% | 3.36L O2 equivalent |
| Rider B (elite female pursuiter) | 3km pursuit | 72% | 28% | 5.27L O2 equivalent |

Despite Rider B having 4% lower VO2max than Rider A, her anaerobic capacity is ~57% larger. She produces 4% more power over the pursuit. "If Rider B were as aerodynamic as Rider A, the additional power that she could generate anaerobically would enable her to complete the 3 km in approximately 3 minutes and 43 seconds, or approximately 4 seconds faster."

**Training prescription from this analysis:**
- Rider A's limiter: anaerobic capacity → prescribe Level 6 intervals in pre-competition phase
- Rider B's limiter: VO2max and FTP → prescribe Levels 3–5 in off-season and base; avoid over-emphasizing AC work that is already superior

The "quasi-plateau in power" that occurs after 1.5–2.5 minutes of a well-paced pursuit marks the depletion of anaerobic capacity; all subsequent effort is purely aerobic. This real-world marker can be identified in post-race data without lab testing.

## Chapter 14 — Special Disciplines: Supplementary Detail

### Track Cycling — Standing Start Gear Selection

Scientific data collected as part of USA Cycling's Project 96 (1996 Olympics preparation): within broad limits, varying the gear ratio for standing-start efforts does not affect force applied to the cranks at any particular cadence. The force-velocity relationship is determined primarily by the rider's muscle contractile properties, not by gear choice.

"Within reason, varying the gear ratio has no effect on the force that a rider's muscles can produce at a particular speed. It only determines how quickly the rider will 'hop, skip, and jump' down his or her maximal force-velocity line."

Implication: track cyclists using oversized training gears for "strength overload" during standing starts are not generating greater pedal forces — they are simply spending more time at the low-cadence, high-force portion of their existing force-velocity curve. The effective overload is not meaningfully greater than standard training gears.

### Track Cycling — Team Pursuit Exchange Data

From Project 96 (Jeff Broker, University of Colorado):
- Second, third, and fourth position riders in team pursuit require only 64–71% of the power needed by the lead rider.
- This draft savings varies significantly with skill; novice pursuers may require substantially more than 64–71% due to poor drafting technique.
- "Dropping back too early" (overlapping third rider then drifting back) has **no measurable extra energetic cost** vs. a perfect exchange.
- "Coming down too late" (losing draft and having to accelerate back on) **is significantly more costly** than either a perfect exchange or an early drop-back.

### Cyclocross — Power File Characteristics

CX race power files characteristically show:
- Average power 20–40W below FTP despite race-level effort intensity
- Cause: coasting on technical descents, off-bike running sections, traction loss on mud/sand reducing effective force transfer
- Quadrant Analysis: primarily QII (slow cadence, high force; accelerating from remounts, climbing in heavy terrain) with QII/QIII/QIV all represented
- "Matches" in CX are described as "bursts of flames coming up from an already raging fire" — the athlete is often already at FTP when a supra-threshold effort is required, rather than ramping up from recovery as in road racing

CX-specific training principle: because average race power underestimates metabolic cost, TSS calculation may underestimate CX race load. Coaches should add a qualitative correction based on RPE and post-race recovery behavior.

### Ultra-Endurance MTB — Dave Harris 24-Hour Data

| Lap | NP | Duration |
|-----|-----|----------|
| 1 | ~224W | 1:07–1:09 |
| 2 | ~224W | ~1:08 |
| 3 | ~224W | ~1:08 |

## Aerodynamic Testing Methods (Ch. 13 supplemental detail)

### Three Field Methods for CdA Determination

**Method 1 — Constant Speed (or Constant Power):**
Rider performs repeated passes (both directions, to cancel wind) on a flat, windless course of known distance at constant speed while recording power (or constant power while recording speed). Minimum 500 m per pass. Data corrected for: kinetic energy changes (select identical start and end speed points), potential energy changes (if not perfectly flat), drivetrain efficiency (crank-based meters only; PowerTap hub measures "at the road" and needs no drivetrain correction). Provides rough CdA estimate.

**Method 2 — Regression Method:**
Six to nine passes at varying speeds (5–15 m/s range). Plot corrected power vs. speed; fit to: Y = aX + bX³ where a = rolling resistance (Crr, in Newtons) and b = (air density / 2) × CdA. Or linearize: plot power/speed on Y-axis vs. speed² on X-axis; intercept = Crr, slope = (air density / 2) × CdA. **Advantage over Method 1:** Separately identifies CdA and Crr, enabling tire/wheel discrimination. More precise due to multiple measurements. Disadvantage: more complex; requires environmental recording for air density.

**Method 3 — Virtual Elevation Method (Robert Chung):**
Rider makes repeated passes over a stretch of road at natural pace, collecting power and speed. Data are used with total mass to solve for apparent elevation profile; CdA and Crr are adjusted until repeated passes at each point show consistent virtual elevation (i.e., the plot "levels"). Can work on non-flat roads. Half-pipe course (moderate hills at each end) is optimal: eliminates braking, provides speed variation to separate CdA from Crr. Braking data points must be excluded.

**Precision achievable:** <2% coefficient of variation under ideal conditions — equivalent to wind tunnel. Requires: no wind, no vehicle traffic (a passing car disturbs air for several minutes), typically early morning.

**Key equation reminder (power = force × velocity):**
The PowerPod/AeroPod (Velocomp) uses the inverse: it measures opposing forces (aerodynamic drag, grade, rolling resistance, acceleration) and derives power, rather than measuring applied crank force directly. Newton's Third Law: resistive forces = applied forces. This is the only commercially available "opposing forces" power meter; all others are direct-force power meters.

## Cross-Reference Notes (continued from Part 1)

- **FRC as kJ capacity, not watts rate:** FRC is expressed in kilojoules (total work capacity above FTP), not watts (rate). A rider with 20 kJ FRC can sustain 666W above FTP for 30 seconds, or 167W above FTP for 120 seconds, or 11W above FTP for 30 minutes. As the duration above FTP grows, the rate-of-FRC-draw diminishes exponentially, explaining the characteristic shape of the PDC above FTP.

- **TTE as leading indicator of FTP improvement:** A TTE approaching or exceeding 60 minutes often precedes a measured FTP increase. When TTE resets to a lower value after an FTP jump, it means the athlete has not yet demonstrated sustained capacity at the new FTP power level. Training to extend TTE at the new FTP is the next priority.

- **mFTP vs. 20-min test FTP:** The WKO4 mFTP (modeled FTP) depends on the quality and recency of data across all durations. If the athlete has not produced a maximal effort near 20–60 minutes within the last 90 days, mFTP may underestimate true FTP. "If a best effort falls off on day 91, mFTP might take a surprisingly large drop on a single day." Always triangulate mFTP against testing-derived FTP.

- **Bilateral power analysis limitations:** The ANT+ power balance metric (standard left/right %) does not account for negative power (absorbed force during upstroke). GPR/GPA analysis is more accurate but requires compatible hardware and software. Three published studies (Coyle et al. 1991; Edwards et al. 2009; Korff et al. 2007) all indicate that deliberately modifying pedaling pattern to "pull up" more reduces efficiency and that elite cyclists already minimize upstroke absorption naturally. Modification is only warranted when GPR asymmetry exceeds 10% or GPA from either leg exceeds 35W while seated.

- **Noncircular chainrings and power meter accuracy:** Crank-based and pedal-based power meters that measure angular velocity at the crank will overestimate power by 10–20W average when used with noncircular (oval) chainrings, because the rings intentionally speed the crank through the low-power portion of the stroke, inflating the angular velocity measurement. Hub-based (PowerTap) and pedal-based power meters are unaffected.

- **Smart recording on Garmin head units:** Garmin's default "smart recording" mode reduces GPS data points on straight paths but also reduces power sample frequency in practice. Always change to 1-second recording for power meter use. "There is nothing worse than coming home from a ride where you put out more power than you ever have, to download the data and realize you forgot to zero your power meter."

## Banister Impulse-Response Model — Mathematical Formulation

The impulse-response model is presented in Ch. 9 as the scientific foundation that the Performance Manager simplifies:

```
p(t) = p₀ + ka × Σ[w(s) × e^(-(t-s)/τa)] − kf × Σ[w(s) × e^(-(t-s)/τf)]
```

Where:
- p(t) = predicted performance at time t
- p₀ = initial performance level
- ka = gain factor for positive adaptation (fitness)
- kf = gain factor for negative fatigue effect
- τa = time constant for fitness effect decay (longer; adaptation persists)
- τf = time constant for fatigue effect decay (shorter; fatigue dissipates faster)
- w(s) = daily training dose at time s (TSS or TRIMP)
- Constraint: ka < kf; τa > τf (fatigue magnitude is larger but shorter-lived than fitness)

**Performance Manager simplification:** By setting ka = kf = 1 (removing gain factors), the complex integrals reduce to exponentially weighted moving averages:

```
CTL(t) = CTL(t-1) × e^(-1/42) + TSS(t) × (1 − e^(-1/42))
ATL(t) = ATL(t-1) × e^(-1/7)  + TSS(t) × (1 − e^(-1/7))
TSB(t) = CTL(t) − ATL(t)
```

The 42-day CTL time constant and 7-day ATL time constant are based on scientific literature values for τa and τf respectively. The authors note the model is "not very sensitive to changes in the CTL time constant" but is sensitive to the ATL time constant, which is the primary parameter coaches should adjust for individual athletes.

**6. CTL optimal range stated but Tour de France data contradicts upper bound**
Chapter 9 states the "optimal" CTL range is 100–150 TSS/day, and that "few, if any, athletes seem to be able to sustain a long-term average of more than 150 TSS/day." Chapter 7 presents extensive evidence that strength endurance training at 45 rpm generates insufficient AEPF for meaningful muscular adaptation. ## Cross-Reference Notes (continued)

- **Single-leg drills (SubLT-W4) and bilateral power analysis:** The single-leg drill prescription (Ch. 10 appendix) is the practical training response to GPR asymmetries identified in Ch. 7 bilateral power analysis. Coaches should use bilateral GPR/GPA data to determine whether to prescribe SubLT-W4 and which leg to emphasize in additional reps. The drill is contraindicated if the asymmetry is positional rather than muscular — the hill climbing test (Ch. 7 testing protocol) distinguishes these cases.

- **Sweet Spot and FTP testing interaction:** The authors recommend 6–8 rounds of Sweet Spot workouts before advancing to pure Threshold work: "He should do at least six to eight of these workouts before moving to specific threshold work." This periodization guideline implies Sweet Spot serves as a preparatory phase for FTP interval training and should be reflected in the mesocycle structure before formal FTP testing cycles.

- **TSS accuracy for CX and MTB:** The authors note that coasting and off-bike running in cyclocross, and traction-limited sections in mountain biking, reduce recorded wattage below actual metabolic cost. TSS calculated from raw power files will underestimate training stress for these disciplines. Coaches should apply a qualitative multiplier (author does not specify an exact factor) or add a manual TSS estimate for the non-pedaling time when building CX/MTB Performance Manager Charts.

- **Garmin "Smart Recording" and MMP integrity:** Because the MMP Curve depends on the best power for every second of a ride, a 2-second or 3-second recording interval will cause MMP to miss true 1-second peaks, particularly relevant for Level 7 (sub-10-second efforts). If a Garmin user has operated in Smart Recording mode for historical data, their MMP and PDC for short durations will be systematically underestimated. This degrades iLevel accuracy for Pmax and iLevel 7a.

- **rTSS scaling rationale:** McGregor's choice of 45-min running at FTp = 100 rTSS (rather than 60-min = 100 rTSS as in cycling) is rooted in the additional musculoskeletal loading of running vs. cycling. The ground-reaction forces in running impose structural stress (tendons, bones, fasciae) that has no cycling equivalent. This means a triathlete's combined PMC will appropriately weight running as a more costly training modality per unit time at equivalent relative intensity.

## Critical Power Model (Ch. 3, Mathematical Modeling FTP Method)

Critical Power (CP) is presented as one of six methods to estimate FTP, positioned as appropriate for riders who "are more mathematically inclined." The CP approach predates and conceptually underpins the PDC model of Ch. 8.

### Protocol

Perform a series of relatively short (3–30 min), all-out efforts. For each effort, record:
- Duration in seconds (t)
- Total work performed in joules (W = power × time)

Plot total work (Y-axis) against duration (X-axis). Fit a straight line:

```
W = CP × t + W'
```

Where:
- **CP (Critical Power)** = slope of the line; "the power that can be sustained for a very long time without fatigue" (Monod and Scherrer, 1965 definition)
- **W' (W prime)** = Y-intercept; the anaerobic work capacity above CP (unit: joules; directly analogous to FRC in the PDC model)

**Equivalence to FTP:** "Provided that you adhere to these guidelines — which are based on the scientific literature, and in particular the recommendations of those that originally developed the model — the value you obtain for CP will be essentially equal to FTP."

**Critical qualification:** If CP is calculated from efforts shorter than 3 minutes, CP will be **higher** than FTP and will overestimate true maximal metabolic steady-state intensity (as measured by blood lactate levels, hormonal responses, and ventilatory data). The minimum effort duration for a valid CP estimate is 3 minutes; the maximum is approximately 30 minutes.

### CP vs. FTP Equivalence Conditions

| Condition | CP vs. FTP relationship |
|-----------|------------------------|
| Efforts 3–30 min, all-out | CP ≈ FTP |
| Efforts <3 min only | CP > FTP (overestimates) |
| Efforts >30 min | CP may drift lower than FTP |
| Sustainable duration: Monod/Scherrer definition | "Very long time without fatigue" → corresponds to ~40–75 min in trained cyclists |

### W' and FRC Relationship

W' (from CP model) and FRC (from PDC model) are conceptually identical: both represent the total finite capacity for work above the sustainable threshold. The PDC model extends this concept across the full power-duration spectrum rather than as a single intercept value. W' expressed in kilojoules can be directly compared to FRC in kilojoules.

## TRIMP — Training Impulse (Formal Definition)

TRIMP is the heart rate–based training load quantification method developed by Dr. Eric Banister, which served as the conceptual predecessor to TSS. Referenced throughout Ch. 7 and Ch. 9 as the original input to the impulse-response model.

**TRIMP formula (Banister, 1975):**

```
TRIMP = duration × ΔHR × e^(b × ΔHR)
```

Where:
- Duration = exercise duration in minutes
- ΔHR = (HR_exercise − HR_rest) ÷ (HR_max − HR_rest); normalized heart rate reserve fraction
- b = a weighting constant (1.92 for men; 1.67 for women) accounting for the non-linear physiological response to increasing exercise intensity
- e = base of natural logarithm

The exponential weighting term (e^(b × ΔHR)) is what makes TRIMP non-linear: it disproportionately weights high-intensity efforts relative to low-intensity efforts at the same duration. This is the mathematical analogue to TSS's IF² term.

**TSS vs. TRIMP comparison:**

| Parameter | TSS | TRIMP |
|-----------|-----|-------|
| Input metric | Power (watts) | Heart rate |
| Non-linearity factor | IF² (= NP²/FTP²) | e^(b × ΔHR) |
| Reference anchor | FTP (1 hr at FTP = 100 TSS) | Not fixed to absolute event |
| Cross-athlete comparability | Yes (via W/kg-normalized FTP) | Limited (requires individual HR parameters) |

The impulse-response model works "equally well regardless of precisely how training has been quantified" — meaning CTL/ATL/TSB calculations produce equivalent quality outputs whether driven by TSS or TRIMP, provided consistent input data.

## Auto-Phenotyping and Phenotypic Map (Ch. 8 — Full Detail)

### Phenotypic Map Axes

The Phenotypic Map uses two ratio axes derived from PDC model outputs:

**X-axis: Pmax / FTP**
- Low ratio → high FTP relative to Pmax → time trialist / climber
- High ratio → high Pmax relative to FTP → sprinter

**Y-axis: FRC / Pmax**
- For riders with low Pmax/FTP (left side of map): FRC/Pmax distinguishes time trialists (low FRC/Pmax) from pursuiters (high FRC/Pmax)
- For riders with high Pmax/FTP (right side of map): the FRC/Pmax axis becomes less discriminating

### Four Canonical Phenotypes on the Map

| Phenotype | Map Location | Pmax/FTP | FRC/Pmax | Characteristics |
|-----------|-------------|---------|---------|-----------------|
| Pure time trialist | Lower-left | Low | Low | Very high FTP; low Pmax; low FRC; minimal fast-twitch; loses all sprints |
| Pursuiter | Upper-left | Low | High | High FTP; low Pmax; high FRC; poor absolute sprint but outstanding relative to other time-trial types |
| All-rounder | Center | Moderate | Moderate | Balanced; competitive across event lengths |
| Sprinter | Right | High | Variable | High Pmax; lower FTP; large fast-twitch proportion |

The time trialist / pursuiter distinction is the most practically important for coaching: both have low Pmax/FTP, but in a sprint between two time trialists, the pursuiter (higher FRC) wins. This is why Bradley Wiggins and Geraint Thomas (Grand Tour TT champions who were also elite track pursuers) serve as the exemplar pursuiter archetype.

### Auto-Phenotyping Algorithm Basis

The phenotyping calculation is described as "a statistical calculation" producing a label (e.g., "sprinter") or a coordinate on the map. The specific algorithm is implemented in TrainingPeaks WKO4. The authors describe the process as "objective ('hard') instead of subjective ('soft') determination of an individual's phenotype," as distinct from the visual interpretation of the one-dimensional Power Profile.

**Training implications of Phenotypic Map movement:**
An athlete can move on the map over time through training. The authors give this explicit example:
- A training period emphasizing FTP → moves rider leftward (lower Pmax/FTP ratio)
- A training period emphasizing AC and NP → moves rider rightward (higher Pmax/FTP ratio)
- De-training from FTP → also moves rider rightward
This dynamic is invisible on the Power Profile (which shows static percentile ranks) but visible on the Phenotypic Map as a directional movement between seasons.

## Chapter 2 — Hardware Detail (Extended)

### Strain Gauge Placement and Measurement Logic

All direct-force power meters use strain gauges to measure deformation of metal under load. The deformation is proportional to force applied. The specific placement determines what forces are captured:

| Placement | Bilateral? | Measures | Key limitation |
|-----------|-----------|---------|----------------|
| Spider (both legs) | Pseudo-bilateral only | Total torsion in spider | Cannot independently measure L/R; left-upstroke negative forces not captured |
| Crank arm — single (left) | Left only | Left-leg force | ~5% discrepancy from assuming symmetry; left-dominant riders overestimate FTP |
| Crank arm — dual (both) | True bilateral | Independent L and R | Most complete bilateral data when combined with net force calculation |
| Bottom bracket axle (left) | Left only | Left-side torsion | Same limitation as single crank arm; compatible with many cranks |
| Chainring/additional spider | Pseudo-bilateral | Total torsion | Cannot independently measure L/R |
| Hub (rear wheel) | Total only | Power after drivetrain losses | 5–10W lower than crank measurement due to drivetrain friction; easiest multi-bike transfer |
| Pedals | True bilateral | Force at point of transfer | Most accurate bilateral; easiest bike-to-bike transfer |
| PowerPod / AeroPod (opposing forces) | Total only | Opposing aerodynamic + gravitational forces via sensors | Not a direct-force meter; power derived from Newton's Third Law |

### ANT+ vs. Bluetooth Low Energy (BLE) Comparison

| Feature | ANT+ | Bluetooth Low Energy (BLE) |
|---------|------|---------------------------|
| Transmission rate | Up to 4 messages/second (3 repeated; effectively ~1 unique/sec) | Higher; can transmit full bilateral metrics at useful rates |
| Proprietary control | Owned by Garmin; universally licensed | Open standard; implemented by all smartphones |
| Head unit pairing | Any ANT+ head unit with any ANT+ meter | Pairs to smartphone; can also pair to BLE head units |
| Firmware updates | Manual/manufacturer tool typically | Easy over-the-phone app updates |
| Data richness | Constrained by protocol message definitions | Supports radial/tangential force vectors, higher-resolution bilateral |
| Interoperability | Industry-standard; SRM with Garmin with Wahoo all work | Less universal across head units historically; improving |

**Practical guidance:** "Most cyclists do not need to worry about which protocol their head unit uses. However, for anyone wanting to get the most out of bilateral data or advanced metrics, BLE provides a more capable channel."

### Noncircular Chainring Power Inflation (Detailed Mechanism)

Purpose of noncircular (oval) chainrings: accelerate the crank through the dead spots (0° and 180°) so more of each revolution is spent in the power phase (0–180°). This speeds the crank within a single revolution.

Power meters measuring angular velocity at the crank assume constant angular velocity across the full revolution and sample only once per revolution. Because the oval ring speeds the crank in the dead-spot phase, the single-revolution angular velocity measurement is inflated. When multiplied by applied force to calculate power, the result is 10–20W above true power.

**Meters affected:** Crank-based (spider, crank arm) and pedal-based (which inherit crank angular velocity).

**Meters unaffected:** Hub-based (PowerTap) measures torque and angular velocity at the hub, after the drivetrain; the chainring geometry does not affect this measurement.

| Kurtotic Index (KI) | A measure of the "peakedness" of the power application pattern during the power-producing phase of a pedal stroke. High KI = one leg stomps; low KI = smooth application. | Ch. 7 |
| Phenotypic Map | A two-dimensional scatter plot of Pmax/FTP (X-axis) vs. FRC/Pmax (Y-axis) that characterizes a rider's phenotype with greater nuance than the 1-D Power Profile. Allows tracking of phenotype movement across seasons due to training. | Ch. 8 |
| Stamina | "A measure of resistance to fatigue during prolonged-duration, moderate-intensity exercise." Expressed as a percentage; describes the flatness of the PDC tail beyond mFTP. Score of 100% = never fatigues; most trained riders: 75–85%. | Ch. 8 |
| Time to Exhaustion (TTE) | The maximal duration the mFTP power level can be maintained; varies between ~30–75 min by individual. A TTE approaching 60 min often precedes an mFTP increase. | Ch. 8 |
| rTSS | Running Training Stress Score. Developed by McGregor; based on normalized graded pace (NGP) and functional threshold pace (FTp). ~45 min running at FTp = 100 rTSS (vs. 60 min cycling at FTP = 100 TSS) due to additional musculoskeletal loading. | Ch. 12 |
| FTp | Functional threshold pace. The running equivalent of FTP: "the highest pace that a runner can maintain in a quasi-steady state without fatiguing." Used as the reference for rTSS calculation. | Ch. 12 |
| NGP | Normalized Graded Pace. The running equivalent of Normalized Power; adjusts pace for grade to give a "flat equivalent" effort that can be compared across varied terrain. | Ch. 12 |
| Allen Effect | In ultra-endurance MTB racing: the compounding gap created when a leader exploits differential speed on descents following an ascent attack. The gap grows geometrically even as absolute power output later equalizes. Going harder than sustainable pace in the first 4–6 hours is tactically sound in events over 6 hours. | Ch. 14 |
| Power calibration | Triathlon-specific training protocol: repeated 10-min intervals at race-specific intensities to anchor RPE to power output. "Power calibrates perceived exertion, and perceived exertion modulates power." | Ch. 12 |
| CdA | Aerodynamic drag coefficient × frontal area. The product of drag coefficient (dimensionless) and frontal area (m²); the key variable in aerodynamic field testing. Measurable via three power-meter field methods (constant speed, regression, virtual elevation) to <2% reproducibility. | Ch. 13 |
| Crr | Coefficient of rolling resistance. A dimensionless number representing tire-road friction losses. Separable from CdA via the regression method of aerodynamic testing. | Ch. 13 |
| Virtual elevation method | Robert Chung's method for estimating CdA from non-structured power and speed data; adjusts CdA and Crr until repeated passes at each road point show consistent apparent elevation profile. | Ch. 13 |
| AEPF threshold for Type II recruitment | The average effective pedal force at which significant fast-twitch muscle fiber recruitment begins. Approximately equal to the AEPF at FTP and self-selected cadence; better represented as a downward-sloping line on the QA plot (decreasing with increasing CPV) rather than a horizontal cut-off. | Ch. 7 |
| Power Duration Curve (PDC) | "A mathematically calculated curve of best fit along the Mean Maximal Power Curve." Generated by non-linear curve-fitting to raw MMP data; produces smooth continuous estimates of performance capacity at every duration; basis for all PDC model metrics (Pmax, FRC, mFTP, TTE, Stamina). | Ch. 4, 8 |
| Mean Maximal Power (MMP) | "Your best average power for each second of the ride." Raw performance data; contrasts with the smoothed PDC. The MMP Curve is the actual data; the PDC is the model fit to it. | Ch. 6 |
| Modeled FTP (mFTP) | "The power level on the Power Duration Curve at which the curve flattens horizontally." Software-estimated FTP from PDC fitting; may correspond to maximal effort of 30–75 min depending on individual TTE. | Ch. 8 |

## Chapter 14 — Remaining Track Cycling Detail

### Evaluating Track Workouts with Power Meters (Ch. 14)

The book provides four distinct applications of power meters in track cycling beyond pacing:

**1. Determining whether rest intervals are adequate in AC workouts:**
For a "2,000-meter flying-start workout" (approximately 2 minutes of maximal effort), adequate rest must be confirmed by checking that subsequent efforts maintain the same power curve shape and wattage. "Cumulative fatigue can cause the workout to deteriorate into a suboptimal VO2max training session." The CP approach (see Critical Power section above) can be used to track whether anaerobic capacity is genuinely improving across training blocks.

**2. Evaluating positional changes:**
Power-meter data collected during repeated standing-start efforts (at high frequency: ≥1 sample/second) before and after a positional adjustment (hip position relative to crank, handlebar height) can confirm or refute whether the change improves power output. The force-velocity line (AEPF vs. CPV plot) shifts upward if the new position genuinely increases force production at any given cadence.

**3. Assessing draft quality:**
Power required to hold second/third/fourth wheel in team pursuit at a given speed decreases as the rider improves drafting skill. Comparing power-to-speed ratio for the non-lead positions across multiple training sessions reveals skill development objectively.

**4. Altitude comparison of equivalent efforts (Table 14.1 example):**

| Event | Location | Time | Power | Adjusted Time (sea level equivalent) |
|-------|---------|------|-------|--------------------------------------|
| 3km pursuit | Sea level (concrete velodrome) | ~3:48 (inferred) | ~395W | 3:48 |
| 3km pursuit | Altitude (~8,000 ft) | ~3:42 (~6 sec faster) | Same power | ~3:48 (adjusted) |

When air density reduction and corresponding aerobic power reduction are both accounted for, the two performances are equivalent. "The two performances are therefore essentially equivalent, despite the significant difference in time required to cover the same distance." Without power data, the altitude improvement would appear to be a genuine fitness gain.

### Track Cycling — Key Training Principle Summary

The central track-specific training principle the authors state: "The exact conditions under which the test (i.e., the standing start) is performed are far less important [than the rider's muscular properties]." This means:
- No benefit to using oversized training gears for "overload" standing starts
- Gear choice determines acceleration *rate* along the maximal AEPF-CPV curve, not the forces achieved at each cadence
- More productive than gear manipulation: increasing motivation through pairing, using handicapped starts, or increasing dwell time at high force by using extremely large gears (not for overload but to slow acceleration so more time is spent at low CPV / high AEPF)

## Remaining Chapter 9 Supplementary Content

### Seeding the Performance Manager Without Historical Data

When a rider starts using a power meter without historical TSS data:

1. Estimate typical training intensity: most riders average 50–75 TSS per hour of training.
2. Multiply by average daily training hours over a normal week.
3. Assign the same value to both CTL and ATL (setting TSB = 0 as starting assumption).
4. Allow ~90 days for CTL to stabilize before treating PMC outputs as reliable.

"Assign the same value to both CTL and ATL (with TSB assumed to be zero). Over time your CTL will become evident, in which case you may want or need to go back and revise these initial estimates."

**Example seed calculation:**
- Rider trains 10 hours/week, mostly outdoors, moderate structure → 55 TSS/hr
- Daily training = 10 × 55 / 7 = ~79 TSS/day
- Seed both CTL and ATL at 79 TSS/day; TSB = 0

### Handling Missing TSS (Race Without Power Meter)

Three methods, listed in order of author preference:

| Method | Approach | Recommended? |
|--------|---------|-------------|
| Library matching | Compare to similar previous workouts | Preferred |
| Manual IF estimation | Estimate IF from type of ride; calculate TSS = hours × IF² × 100 | Acceptable |
| HR-derived NP → TSS | Use heart rate data to estimate NP; calculate TSS | "No reason to recommend over other approaches" for experienced users |

"Any error introduced as a result of poorly estimating the true TSS for one or two missing workouts is likely to be minimal. However, if you were missing a large amount of data (for example, more than 10 percent of all files for a particular block of time), then the output of the Performance Manager calculations during and after that period should be interpreted with considerable caution."

## Chapter 11 — Fitness Tracking: Remaining Detail

### Cadence Changes Over Time

Cadence monitoring across a full season reveals:
- Whether an athlete is successfully implementing a cadence change prescription (e.g., the pro triathlete's 80 rpm → 90 rpm shift)
- The mean max cadence for 30-sec, 20-min, and 60-min durations as separate tracking metrics
- Trend direction of average weekly cadence bar chart

"With enough data, you should be able to see the difference when you begin to change your cadence." The confounding factor: average cadence across a full ride includes all zero-cadence coasting time, which depresses the average artificially. Use mean max cadence at specific durations as the cleaner metric for trend analysis.

### Comparing the Same Race Year-Over-Year

Methodology for valid year-over-year race comparison:
1. Identify the same race event on the same course in different years
2. Select identical time-range boundaries (same start landmark to same finish landmark)
3. Overlay power, heart rate, cadence, and speed simultaneously
4. Identify sections where speed differs but power is equivalent → aerodynamic or positional change
5. Identify sections where power differs → fitness or tactical change

The case study in Ch. 11 (same TT course, two years): identical power and cadence, slightly different speed. Year 2 was 10 seconds slower despite equivalent watts. Analysis revealed: the athlete failed to exploit a slight downhill section as aggressively in Year 2 (speed line comparison), and power output dropped slightly in the final 3 minutes. "The lack of focus in Year 2 resulted in a time that was 20 seconds slower than the time achieved in the first year, despite nearly identical watts."

This type of analysis is the power meter's ultimate coaching value: separating fitness (watts) from execution (speed), from tactics (distribution), and from external conditions (wind, grade).

## Remaining Training Principles Not Previously Captured

### The Stochastic Nature of Cycling (Ch. 5) — Practical Implications

The authors describe cycling power output as "stochastic" — appearing random but containing analyzable patterns. The dominant sources of variability are terrain, wind, traffic, and the proximity of other riders. Practical prescriptions derived from this reality:

- "It is important not to become discouraged if you are unable to hold a perfect 300 watts while doing, for example, a Lactate Threshold interval." Target a range, not an exact wattage.
- When doing Tempo intervals on rolling terrain: going over the target on uphills and under-target on downhills does not produce the intended training stimulus — the result is actually a hill-repeat session, not a Tempo session. Match terrain to intent.
- "If you live in such an area [only hilly terrain] and you need a rest, ride on an indoor trainer so you can more easily adhere to the recovery power guidelines."
- The normalized watts (NP) of a session — not average power — is the correct way to characterize Tempo rides with variable terrain.

### Race vs. Training: Different Effort Contexts

From Ch. 13: "The best data will often come from races where the competition drives you to go harder than you would in routine training." This is a fundamental principle that directly affects PDC accuracy:
- Lab-equivalent efforts only emerge under competitive pressure
- Training best-efforts underrepresent Pmax, FRC, and supra-threshold capacity
- Racing regularly is not optional for complete PDC modeling — it is a data source requirement
- "Your best data will come from races, your best efforts will come in races, and you stand to learn the most from your race data."

This directly motivates the PDC model's design philosophy (Ch. 8): it was developed precisely because real-world race data doesn't conform to formal test protocols, and a useful model must extract information from ecologically valid, uncontrolled efforts.

### The Minimum Effective Interval Duration Principle

For each training level, there is a minimum duration below which no meaningful training adaptation occurs in the target system:

| Level | Minimum Effective Duration | Basis |
|-------|--------------------------|-------|
| 5 (VO2max) | 3 minutes | Below 3 min: insufficient time to elicit VO2max stimulus |
| 6 (AC) | 30 seconds | Below 30 sec: insufficient glycolytic and PCr system stress |
| 7 (NP) | 1 full pedal revolution (Pmax); practical minimum ~5 sec | Even 1-sec sprint has explosive utility; diminishing adaptation below 5 sec for training |
| 4 (LT) | 8–10 minutes | Below 8 min: insufficient time at threshold for robust FTP adaptation |
| 3 (Tempo) | 20+ minutes continuous | Short Tempo segments have limited adaptation value |
| 2 (Endurance) | 45+ minutes | Mitochondrial and capillary adaptations require prolonged sub-threshold stimulus |

These minimums interact with the intervals-to-exhaustion principle: you must be able to complete the minimum duration at the required intensity for the interval to count as a training stimulus.

### The "Least Amount of the Most Specific Training" Applied Mechanically

The authors operationalize Joe Friel's philosophy through the power meter as follows:
1. Determine your goal event and its specific physiological demands (via QA and PDC comparison with race data)
2. Identify your weakest energy system relative to those demands (Power Profile / PDC analysis)
3. Determine the minimum number of intervals at the correct wattage and duration that produces adaptation in that system (intervals-to-exhaustion rule)
4. Stop when the drop-off indicates further work is counterproductive
5. Accumulate CTL progressively; TSB governs when quality work is accessible

"With your power meter you can now rest assured that you are training all of your systems optimally." The mechanical precision of watt-based training replaces the traditional coach's intuition about "enough but not too much."

## Additional Internal Inconsistency Notes

**9. The isopower TT strategy and variable pacing literature**

Chapter 13 states the default time trial strategy is isopower (ride at FTP throughout, increase in final 3–5 min). The text simultaneously acknowledges that "variable pacing might be beneficial" and spends several paragraphs on headwind/tailwind and hill-specific pacing modifications. The performance physiology literature contains substantial debate about whether variable pacing (positive split, negative split, J-curve, even split) is optimal. The authors do not resolve this debate, instead providing the isopower default and modifications, then leaving "complex" variable pacing underdeveloped. Coaches should not treat the isopower prescription as experimentally settled.

**10. Triathlon no-sprint prescription vs. course-specific sprint prescription**

Chapter 12 explicitly states: "It's not that important for most triathletes to train at the Neuromuscular Power level (Level 7), the Anaerobic Capacity level (Level 6), or even for VO2max (Level 5)." Yet the same chapter provides a specific AC workout for courses with "over 100 hills." The boundary condition (how many hills, of what length, on what course) for when AC training becomes necessary for triathletes is not formally defined, leaving the prescription underdetermined for courses of intermediate variability.

**11. Hub-based power meters read 5–10W lower — implications for FTP testing**

Chapter 2 states hub-based (PowerTap) meters read 5–10W lower than crank-based meters due to drivetrain losses. Chapter 3 and throughout the book, FTP is treated as a universal metric comparable across athletes. An athlete who tests FTP on a PowerTap will have a 5–10W lower FTP value than if they tested on an SRM — not because of genuine fitness differences, but because of measurement location. This creates a systematic inter-athlete comparison bias when athletes use different meter types. The book does not formally address cross-meter FTP comparison protocols; the downstream coaching project should record which meter type was used for each FTP test.

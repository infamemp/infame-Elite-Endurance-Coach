## C01 Overview & Scope

### [WKOC-C01-001] Overview & Scope

**What the book is.** A collection of eight webinars (late 2016 to about 2021) by Tim Cusick, TrainingPeaks WKO product leader and master coach at Velocious Endurance Coaching. The talks were delivered to WKO4 and WKO5 users and teach how to apply the power duration (PD) model (Pmax, FRC, mFTP, TTE, stamina, phenotype, iLevels, optimized intervals) to training design, from individual physiology to the annual plan.

**For whom.** Coaches and self-coached endurance athletes, mostly cyclists, who use a power meter. Some content applies to running and triathlon. The author says coaches should test his system against their own athletes' data, not treat it as law.

**Problem it solves.** Generalized zones and rules come from a population bell curve. Cusick estimates only about half of athletes fit them, so everyone else trains sub-optimally. Without individual physiology, "we don't know what we don't know". The books gives a method to measure the athlete's own physiology from power data, then prescribe and track training from it.

**Central thesis.**
- Individualize training from the athlete's live PD curve instead of fixed %FTP zones.
- FTP is the biggest determinant of success, and lack of fatigue resistance is the biggest cause of failure.
- Build FTP by extending TTE (extensive) first, then pulling power up (intensive). Raise VO2max when FTP nears its ceiling (the fitness house), then return to FTP.
- Periodization is a neutral organizing construct. The process (diagnosis, manipulation of volume and intensity, modality, fitness/freshness management) produces the peak.
- In base, progress time in zone, not watts. In the short peak, progress intensity ("+1 principle") with falling volume.
- Power is the ultimate predictor of performance. Metrics explain why.
- Everything is cost-benefit.

**Organization.**
- Part I, the model: Chapter 1 (metrics, iLevels, optimized intervals).
- Part II, physiology and development: Chapter 2 (FTP/TTE/stamina, WKO5 2019), Chapter 3 (same topic, WKO4 2017, partly superseded), Chapter 4 (aerobic engine: chronic vs acute), Chapter 5 (fatigue resistance).
- Part III, annual planning: Chapters 6 to 8 (early base; mid/late base; late base to peak).
- Appendix A (currency notes) and Appendix B (glossary and Intervals.icu equivalences).

**Does NOT cover.**
- Nutrition and fueling detail, strength training, injury rehab, and medical conditions.
- A single secret workout. The author says pros do about 6 to 10 unique workouts a year and that the final phase is "art".
- Full phase-by-phase moto-pacing protocols (deliberately omitted as dangerous).
- A method to identify LT1 in WKO (not available at the time).
- Raw training impact score formulas.
- Sports other than endurance (mostly cycling). Specific prescriptions are the author's generalizations and must be individualized.

- Uses: performance strategy (event type selection, race strategy, race tactic) and training strategy (training load, training content, training targets, dose and response tracking). The PMC covers load; the new metrics cover content, targets and dose-response.

## C02 Philosophy & Core Principles

### [WKOC-C02-001] Core principles (summary)

**What the author optimizes for:** velocity and power at the event (not metrics, CTL or watts for their own sake), peak performance at the chosen time, and the highest odds of success through a defensible process.

**Principles, with reasoning**
1. **Individualize.** Zones from a population bell curve fit about half of athletes. Use the athlete's own PD curve (iLevels, optimized intervals, mFTP, TTE).
2. **Ability of the rider meets demand of the event.** This is the performance statement and the diagnostic starting point.
3. **Cost-benefit governs everything.** A specific dose gives a specific response (Pmax vs FTP, FTP vs FRC, stamina vs sprint). Track metrics each 4 to 6 week cycle to see what a block cost.
4. **FTP is the greatest, not the only, driver of success.** FTP, LT2, MLSS and OBLA move together. Lack of fatigue resistance is the greatest cause of failure.
5. **Extend before raise.** Push the curve right (extensive, TTE) and up (intensive, power) as separate emphases. Mixing both tends to give "the worst of both worlds".
6. **Fitness house.** FTP grows toward the VO2max ceiling. When it nears it (mFTP about 81 to 85% of VO2max, or flat for 2 to 3 weeks), raise the ceiling, then grow FTP again.
7. **Volume determines how much you adapt, intensity defines which adaptation.** Time-capped athletes need volume investments (about 20 to 25% jumps) for big gains.
8. **Progression always.** In base progress time in zone, not watts. "Always be pushing". Repeat a workout at most a couple of times. Power follows.
9. **Aerobic foundation first (stacking).** A deeper base allows more work in build and a higher peak. Avoid longer cycles that produce "permafit" without a peak.
10. **Strategy, not rigid plan.** Phases are fuzzy time ranges with metric-based exits ("don't go till you need to go, but know when to leave"). Use strategy over plan, and keep structure minimal when not needed.
11. **Progressive overload with managed fatigue.** G.A.S.: overreach, recover, higher overreach. Fatigue comes first, fitness later.
12. **Short, specific peak.** 3 to 6 weeks, most gains in 3 to 5, volume down, intensity progression up, polarized for events under about 4 hours.
13. **Power data informs the response, metrics explain it.** Combine with subjective data (RPE, sleep, HRV, motivation). Athletes often sense the move-on time first.
14. **Simplicity and communication.** FTP is used instead of lab thresholds because it is easy to track and explain. Avoid over-precision.
15. **Mental toughness matters.** Most athletes quit well before true exhaustion.

**What the author rejects or distrusts**
- FTP as "the power you can hold for an hour", and 95% of 20 min as universal.
- The "20-minute revolution" (everything in intervals under 20 minutes, always adding watts).
- Stopping intervals when power drops 4% for max efforts.
- Reverse periodization (except ultras), polarized all year or "80/20 as a religion", and long slow distance as the driver.
- Machine-learning coaching ("10 to 20 years away").
- Over-structured 52 to 57 week plans, and junk or ceiling-edge zone 2 miles.

**Non-negotiables**
- Keep the PD model alive with maximal efforts or unstructured testing.
- Ride in the middle of zone 2.
- Full recovery for max sprints and FRC work (7 to 12 minutes).
- At least 48 hours between HIIT sessions in the acute phase.
- Never intervals under 10 minutes when building FTP.
- Individualize.

### [WKOC-C02-002] Why generalized zones fail: the bell curve problem

- Over roughly 20-25 years endurance training became very systematized, so athletes were trained more alike than they should be. Reason: without a way to see the performing athlete's unique physiology, training could not be fully individualized (professionals with lab access could; the masses could not).
- Generalized training relies on general rules; targets are based on a bell curve of the population; response tracking has been through a limited lens. "We don't know what we don't know."
- Intensity systems (time and intensity) were designed simply: smart scientists/coaches took ONE point (FTP) and, from observational data, assigned percentages (e.g. ~110% taxes VO2max, ~135% taxes anaerobic capacity). Coggan classic zones and Friel zones are examples. They are good observational science, not as mystical or scientific as assumed.
- Bell curve gives only general functional rules. Author's observation (from ~4-5 years of individualized approach): only about half the population fits the bell curve; the other half sit at the extremes, and following general rules means they do not maximize results.
- Percentage-based zones give no better guidance than the average of the population.

### [WKOC-C02-004] FTP as biggest determinant; FTP, LT2 and MLSS move together

- FTP is the single biggest determinant of racing success (not the only one). If an athlete could focus on only one element: raise threshold. Common denominator across successful athletes (classics rider, MTB world champion, stage racer, sprint tri champion, Ironman champion) is a high FTP.
- FTP, LT2, anaerobic threshold and MLSS tend to move in conjunction when trained (a 5% FTP rise implies about 5% rise in the others). Minor variations do not matter for day-to-day training of ~99% of athletes; the debate among them is "a lot of hype".
- Your FTP is aerobic: the 20-minute power on the curve includes anaerobic contribution; the PD curve = aerobic (green) + anaerobic (blue) contributions. Use the aerobic plateau as FTP, not the 20-min value.
- Coaching philosophy: no secret workouts; WorldTour pros do about 6-10 unique workouts a year, with excellent timing and rhythm, volume, natural gifts, and tolerance for boredom ("bread and butter"). Have a training strategy, not a rigid plan; hire a coach early to shorten the learning curve; annual strategy is outlined but not filled in, adjusted by tracked metrics and dose-response.

### [WKOC-C02-005] Cost-benefit principle for training and metrics

- Every idea, workout or strategy a coach implements has a cost; the benefit must outweigh it. At first cost seems low and benefit high, but the curves intersect, where cost can begin to outpace benefit. Implement within the range of balanced cost-benefit.
- Cost-benefit is a "versus" analysis: focusing on FTP may give up Pmax or FRC; focusing on FRC may give up FTP; Stamina vs Sprint; Power vs Fatigue Resistance. A specific dose or intensity tends to elicit a specific response; not everything improves at once. Metrics let you track that.
- Cusick's caution: webinars are generalist; do not over-correct to a more precise level than presented.

### [WKOC-C02-007] Aerobic training: challenges and the progression-over-time principle

Challenges of aerobic training:
- Very time-consuming when done right (maximal aerobic fitness just takes time).
- Harder to maximize than most think: of 100 random mid-category or gran fondo riders, aerobic training is done incorrectly more often than anaerobic training. It takes discipline to ride in a tight range, not too hard, not too easy; many riders have more junk/trash miles than they think, or ride too hard. Dose and target seem easy but are more complex.
- Diminishing returns: continuing to overreach needs more time.
- More than one system and opinion (from long slow distance to everything high intensity).
- Key limitation of most riders is TIME: average category racer or gran fondo rider trains about 10-14 hours/week. Hence efficiency of training matters.
General formats: high-intensity; high-volume low-intensity; threshold (zone 3 / sweet spot); polarized (e.g. 80/20); pyramidal (similar to threshold). Question: which is best for me?
Author's view: debates make it static (a point on a continuum) and a right/wrong argument. The answer is PROGRESSION OVER TIME; multiple solutions can be explored; TRACKING is key (the reason WKO4 was built). Individualized by physiology and time constraints (e.g. 7 h/week athlete vs unlimited time athlete get different answers). Timing is an individualized progression and transition. Do not lose sight of progress and overreaching in the process; do not over-complicate.
Polarized is not the same as block periodization: polarized = some very hard work plus much easy work (like 80/20); block/front-loading are other systems.

### [WKOC-C02-008] Core principles of fatigue resistance training

- FTP is the single greatest factor related to performance success in endurance sports, but lack of fatigue resistance is the greatest factor in performance failure.
- Not just more watts: prefer an athlete with greater fatigue resistance over five more watts.
- The author opposes the 20-minute revolution: every interval, submaximal effort and FTP test kept under 20 minutes. It removes longer efforts, which holds athletes back psychologically (learning not to suffer more than 20 min) and physiologically.
- Mental toughness is required: most athletes never truly reach exhaustion; the psychological side is the umbrella over all fatigue.
- Good training = optimal response for the least amount of fatigue.
- In base, progression is time in zone (TiZ), not watts; power follows naturally.
- Coach should overreach almost every workout if adaptation is to continue (progression principle).
- Aerobic sport: a deeper aerobic base allows more work in build, which raises the race peak.

### [WKOC-C02-009] Mental toughness lesson: Marcora and Staiano test and That Guy

Test (Marcora and Staiano, Italian exercise physiologists): 10 male, non-highly-competitive cyclists on Wattbikes.
1. Max power test: 3 × 5 s with good rest = baseline.
2. Ride to exhaustion at 90% of VO2max (exhaustion = cadence under 50 rpm): average 12 minutes at 242 W.
3. Immediately repeat 3 × 5 s max test: 9 of 10 had a 30% reduction against baseline.
The tenth (That Guy) had a 90%+ reduction in power. Author: that guy was the best, the one he would want to train: the other nine were not truly exhausted (still within 30% of peak), he was. Very few athletes truly go to exhaustion. Author uses this with athletes as a mental-toughness lesson. He cites Navy SEALs research: when people quit they are physically typically only about 40% done (the 40% rule), and suggests inspirational videos for an athlete who fails this test. Being able to go deeper is an important part of FR.

### [WKOC-C02-010] Periodization is a neutral construct; velocity and power as the output goal

- Elite performance = integration of muscular, cardiovascular and neurological factors that transfer aerobic and anaerobic ATP turnover into velocity and power (Joyner and Coyle, Endurance exercise performance: the physiology of champions). Train multiple systems to work together; the goal is velocity and power, not more watts or more work.
- Periodization = systematic planning of training, progressive cycling of training aspects, aiming at the best performance in the most important competition. Built on progression and overload as building blocks (base block, then faster/stronger blocks on top).
- Periodization is only a system of organization and, like training load, a neutral construct. It is not a training process or modality. Following a periodized schedule does not guarantee a peak.
- Origin: single-peak focus (one or two big events); in practice it has morphed into multiple peaks (dual, triple, many).
- Cycles: macrocycle = training year; mesocycles = larger cycles with specific focus; microcycles = weeks.
- Timing: coaches over-structure huge long blocks (e.g. 57 weeks). Periodization is the beginning of the peaking process. Do not go somewhere till you need to go (resist organizing early, because once the fuse is lit it burns at its own rate), but know when it is time to leave.
- Load progression is not a constantly rising CTL. Follow G.A.S.: overreach, a little recovery, a higher overreach, recovery, another overreach (planned recovery steps).
- Classic linear model: volume (quantity) starts high and falls; intensity (quality) starts low and rises; performance peaks after taper; technique peaks at the most important time.

### [WKOC-C02-011] Volume vs intensity: what each does

- Volume = amount of work performed; determines how much you adapt (depth of adaptation). Author's opinion: volume is probably the greatest driver of improvement, until it is not (diminishing returns).
- Intensity = amount of specific quality performed; defines the adaptations. Example: gap between 250 W and desired 350 W 20-min ability; volume drives depth, intensity specificity.
- Clients are time-capped; adaptation limited, but can be improved by more specific adaptation.
- Volume must be in the right range (Goldilocks): too little = no gain or loss; too much = too much acute/chronic fatigue, reduced performance. No sudden jumps (e.g. from 3 h/week to 25 h/week for 8 weeks).
- Increasing capacity = progression of time in a training zone; increasing power = progression of higher intensity. Not mutually exclusive: working on one improves the other; it is a matter of focus. Both move the power duration curve right and up; both should be scheduled.
- Stacking: build one system on top of another. Endurance is an aerobic sport (Coggan). A better, broader aerobic foundation (capacity, power, lactate onset moved right, fat burning) allows better anaerobic, speed, power and repeatability on top. The foundation is the driving block of success.
- Indoor vs outdoor: indoor is more time-efficient if the athlete coasts a lot outdoors (e.g. 25% coasting); if the athlete stays on the gas (about 5% coasting), little difference. Always cost-benefit analysis.

### [WKOC-C02-012] Periodization is an organizing system, not the plan; strategy over plan

- Purpose of all training: more velocity and power (not training for its own sake or a higher CTL). Performance needs muscular, cardiovascular and neurological factors working together (Joyner and Coyle).
- Performance determinants are complex ("butterfly effect": small changes ripple downstream), so coaches need organizing systems.
- Periodization: macrocycle = year; mesocycles = phases; microcycles = small chunks. It schedules progression and planned recovery (G.A.S., the principle of progression). No science proves a periodized plan works better, but practical experience says it does.
- Periodization is "only a system of organization" and, like training load, a neutral construct, not a training process or modality. The coach must define the process and modality inside it.
- Phases are fuzzy, not rigidly time-bound; the athlete's adaptation has its own rate. Need "a strategy versus a plan". Asterisked periods are flexible (e.g. a 4-week block may take 6-8 weeks to show a response).
- A schedule is a guide, not an absolute: if 60-70% of it works on schedule it is a pretty good plan (illness, life stress, injury, non-adaptation, coach error, COVID).
- Every coach/athlete should have their own planning grid of important factors before building plans. Self-coached athletes are an N of one; a coach's grid comes from tens/hundreds of athletes. For self-coached athletes the author can only give general answers.
- Athletes cannot peak for 4-5 A events; roughly 1-2 absolute peaks per year. Wanting to be awesome for everything means being pretty good for everything.

### [WKOC-C02-013] Core principles of the peak phase

- Performance is the goal: elite performance integrates muscular, cardiovascular and neurological factors that turn aerobic and anaerobic ATP turnover into velocity and power (Joyner and Coyle). Analytics must translate into velocity, power and wins.
- Periodization is only an organizing system and a neutral construct, like training load. It is not a training process or modality. The author states there is no scientific proof it works. He believes in it because it creates manageable chunks, micro-goals and compartmentalized work on strengths and weaknesses.
- He is not a firm believer in progression by time. Time estimates are guides. The peak must still land at the right time, but plans must be flexible, not rigid.
- Volume and intensity play separate roles. Volume (quantity of work) determines how much you adapt. Intensity (quality of work performed) defines the adaptations. Specificity matters most (Coggan's top learnings after 25 years of power: specificity, specificity, specificity).
- Significant performance gains for the average cyclist usually need a significant volume jump (about 20–25%). Elite riders with 10–15 years of riding may have little volume room left.
- Data does not decide. The coach's brain, wisdom and process decide. The author states machine-learning coaching is not feasible because of too many variables and too little data, and is 10–20 years away if it happens.
- All training specificity has a cost: focusing on one thing loses something elsewhere.
- Base and build are just training to train: build a linear foundation so the athlete can do more work in a short peak window. More work in that window means a higher peak.
- Power is the best indicator and greatest predictor of performance. Metrics explain the why, but you win on the actual power you put out.
- Quality is the goal, not tolerance: the athlete should absorb the work and want more.
- The author says to develop and write out your own process, because the process increases the odds of success.

### [WKOC-C02-014] Training focus evolution: weaknesses, limiters, general and specific demands

Focus progresses through the year:
1. General weaknesses of the athlete (anything they are weak at).
2. Specific limiters of the athlete (early to late base). A limiter is a weakness that can stop you from winning. Example: a time-trial and stage-race specialist will never win a sprint finish; that is a weakness but not a limiter. Do some work on it so it does not become a barrier, but do not chase it.
3. General demands of the event, then 4. specific demands of the event (late base to peak).
Examples: a series goal (win the crit series) stays more general. A national crit championship is specific (course features, a small hill, the place where separation happens).
Analysis tool: a needs analysis (general / specific / focused) done in depth once at the start with the athlete. "Focused" means the little mental and physical things to get right in the race (e.g. pace maintenance, positioning).

## C03 Integral Model

### [WKOC-C03-001] Integral Model

**1. Chain from beliefs to decisions**
- *Beliefs:* athletes differ; aerobic fitness is the foundation ("it is an aerobic sport"); FTP is the biggest success driver; fatigue resistance prevents failure; every emphasis has a cost; progression drives adaptation; power is the final predictor.
- *Mechanisms* (see C04 entries: Elements of FTP and trainable mechanisms; Fitness house model; FTP breakdown aerobic vs anaerobic contribution; Accumulation and depletion hypotheses):
  - aerobic power plus anaerobic reserve (FRC) sum to the PD curve;
  - FTP is the aerobic plateau, capped by VO2max;
  - FTP training builds fat oxidation, mitochondrial and capillary density, and lactate handling;
  - fatigue is central or peripheral, by accumulation or depletion.
- *Methodology:* extensive then intensive FTP building; chronic then acute aerobic work; stack periodization (base, build, race); polarized peak.
- *Decisions:* what interval, what power, how many, when to move on, how much volume.

**2. Variables the author manipulates, in priority order**
1. Diagnosis: ability of the rider and demand of the event. Includes phenotype, muscle fiber type, PD profile, strengths and limiters, and capacity vs power.
2. Volume (ramp rate in TSS/day over 7 and 28 days; see Ramp rates by athlete training age).
3. Intensity (specific quality; time in zone and power targets).
4. Neuromuscular manipulation (cadence).
5. Modality (rhythm: HVLIT, pyramidal, polarized, threshold).
6. Fitness/freshness (CTL, ATL, TSB, cycle length).
7. Outcome: peak.

**3. How the tools fit**
- **Model and metrics** (Pmax, FRC, mFTP, TTE, stamina, VO2max) diagnose and track. They need maximal efforts (baseline test: 5 s, 1 min, 5 min, 20 min, each on separate days; then unstructured testing every 4 to 6 weeks).
- **iLevels** describe training and cover the whole curve. **Optimized intervals** prescribe FTP-to-Pmax work. Below threshold, classic and iLevel zones coincide (levels 4 and below).
- **Curve-under-the-curve targeting** (85 to 94% of PDC at or just beyond TTE for extensive; 95 to 105% for intensive) is the author's personal method for sub-max/threshold work.
- **Phases:** early base (HVLIT, volume, 5 to 6 days, one long day, one hard day every 8 to 14 workouts) → mid/late base (pyramidal, tempo and sweet spot 88 to 93%, about 3 days a week, cycle 2 to 3 weeks, managed ramp 3 to 5 TSS/day) → build/peak (polarized, volume down, +1 principle, 3 to 6 weeks) → race.
- **Exit rules** use history-based targets: mFTP % of VO2max (rises toward 81 to 85%, 84 to 85% in mid base), EF trend, power at HR-estimated LT1, cluster tightness, plus subjective data.
- **FTP-build sub-cycle** (Chapter 2): extensive 4 to 8 (up to 12) weeks → intensive 4 → VO2max raise 4 → FTP speed 4. It runs inside or alongside the annual plan.

**4. Decision flow for a new athlete**
1. Baseline test and wait 30 to 90 days for the model to flesh out (Q&A: When to trust the phenotype model).
2. Diagnose: see "Diagnosing the ability of the rider", phenotype is the shape of the curve, and the capacity vs power equation.
3. Ask the athlete how they win and how they lose (fatigue resistance often appears).
4. Set the strategy backwards from the A event. Do not start structure early.
5. Early base: see "Early foundation phase: the Perfect World Principle" and "Base miles: ride the middle of zone 2". Expect initial performance decline (3 to 5 weeks).
6. Progress time in zone (SDT rule "extend, don't go harder"). Check ramp rate and training age tables.
7. Move on when transition metrics are met (see "When to leave early base" and "mFTP as % of VO2max: transition rule").
8. Mid/late base: tempo, sweet spot, TTE-scaled workouts. Protect glycolytic capacity with a higher-intensity day every 8 to 14 days.
9. If FTP hits the VO2max ceiling: max aerobic work (optimized intervals, 15 to 20 min total, time at VO2max 7 to 10 min), then FTP speed.
10. Peak: decide capacity vs power, specificity (iLevels override optimized intervals when demand is far from optimum), 2 to 3 hard days, polarized under about 4 hours, pyramidal over about 4 hours.
11. Manage ATL, cluster tracking, feelings; spend CTL down slightly; taper.
12. Racing all season: expect two true peaks a year at most; otherwise a perma-peak a few percent lower.

**5. Tensions and acknowledged trade-offs**
- FTP vs FRC/Pmax: raising one can lower the other.
- Extensive vs intensive: choose, don't blend.
- Volume vs time cap: ramp rate must slow when intensity enters.
- Intervals are broken up only to prepare for longer efforts.
- Aerobic vs anaerobic emphasis at peak: cost of specificity.
- Structure vs flexibility (athlete budget; unstructured time).
- Sweet spot is efficient but monotone with "sneaky fatigue" and biased feedback.
- Optimized intervals vs event specificity.
- Models vs lab: the author claims the model is comparable (VO2max ±4 to 6% vs lab ±5 to 8%).
- Author warns against over-precision and says all prescriptions are opinion.

### [WKOC-C03-002] Periodization concepts: G.A.S., types, time-bound construct

- Periodization rests on the general adaptation syndrome (G.A.S.) with three phases. Alarm: new stimuli alarm the body. Resistance: the body works against the stimulus and adapts. Exhaustion: stagnation or decline. The cycle is: ring the alarm, raise the resistance, rest so you do not stagnate or exhaust, then repeat higher (stair-step progression with planned recovery).
- Forms of periodization: linear/block, reverse, undulating, others.
- Hierarchy: macrocycle (training year), mesocycles (Preparation: general and specific; Competition: pre-comp and competition; Transition), microcycles (weeks).
- Example phase timeline shown (slide 13): PREP 2 weeks, BASE 1 4 weeks, BASE 2 4 weeks, BASE 3 4 weeks, BUILD 1 4 weeks, BUILD 2 4 weeks, PEAK 4 weeks, RACE (no duration given).
- The author's own simplification is two phases: a training phase (base and build) and a performance phase (build and peak).

### [WKOC-C03-003] The author's annual planning process: diagnosis, manipulate, via, manage, outcome

Process flow:
1. Diagnosis: ability of the rider and demands of the event.
2. Manipulate: volume and intensity.
3. Via: training modality, meaning how the manipulation is delivered (polarized, pyramidal, threshold, undulating, etc.). Modalities are different forms of implementing the volume/intensity manipulation to create adaptation through a superior adaptive signal and management of the fitness–freshness relationship.
4. Manage: fitness and freshness (performance management; CTL and ATL measure training load, not necessarily fitness).
5. Outcome: peak performance, defined as the state of performing to maximum ability with confidence, effortlessness and total concentration.

Phase-by-phase use of the process:
- Early base: aggressive volume-based ramp rate (CTL and ATL are the drivers; a little intensity maintained). Modality HVLIT. Manage with 3- to 5-week cycles. Outcome: initial performance declines for the first 3–5 weeks, because fatigue comes first and fitness follows later.
- Mid/late base: progressive addition of time in intensive aerobic training (sweet spot or tempo, between LT1 and LT2) and TSS accumulation. Modality pyramidal. Manage with 2- to 3-week cycles (shortened to deal with fatigue). Outcome: rising ATL causes inconsistent performance.
- Late base to peak: progression of intensity with a volume decrease. Modality polarized with some customization. Manage with 2- to 3-week quality-managed cycles. Outcome: higher ATL means inconsistency; athletes are hypersensitive.
- The author notes that cycles in weeks are a generalization; a cycle could be 2.5 weeks with a half week of rest.

### [WKOC-C04-002] The power duration model: origin, fit and logic

- Developed by the WKO team; physiologist behind it Dr. Andy Coggan. Must be predictive, reliable, quantitative.

### [WKOC-C04-006] Elements of FTP and trainable mechanisms

Three elements of FTP: (1) VO2max: ceiling is a genetic gift, but 90%+ of athletes are not at it (a WorldTour pro training 25+ h/week probably is; someone training 10-12 h/week is not close). (2) Maximal lactate steady state: where maximal lactate accumulation equals maximal clearance; trainable on both sides (produce less, clear more). (3) Efficiency: link between ATP turnover and external power.
Four trainable mechanisms of FTP training: 
- Improved fatty acid oxidation: more aerobic energy, less lactate produced at a given power.
- Higher mitochondrial density: better resynthesis/reuse of lactate as energy (author notes simplified).
- Greater capillary density: more oxygen in, better removal of by-product.
- Improved dealing with lactate: shuttling proteins, buffering, resynthesis; prevents lowering of muscle pH, improving fatigue resistance.

### [WKOC-C04-007] Fitness house model (VO2max ceiling)

### [WKOC-C04-009] Threshold concepts and why Cusick uses FTP only

- Threshold = point or intensity where the fuel source changes significantly and measurably.
- VO2max: maximum oxygen the body can use in a period of intense exercise; the cap, not a threshold.
- OBLA: intensity where lactate starts to continuously accumulate. MLSS/LT2: highest intensity where lactate production and clearance are balanced. AT/LT1: point where lactate first rises above baseline (slightly before LT2).
- OBLA and MLSS/LT2 are treated as the same. Functionalized (Coggan's insight), they equal the highest power a rider can maintain in a quasi-steady state without fatiguing = FTP.
- LT1 and LT2 typically move together with metabolic fitness (up and down at about the same rate), so tracking both adds little; only at very high specificity might it matter (not for 98–99% of people).
- Cusick tracks only VO2max (the cap) and FTP: simpler, testable on the bike, easy for athletes to understand; a coach's job includes communication, motivation and education, so he prefers a simple construct over one half a percent more accurate but harder to grasp.

### [WKOC-C04-010] The fitness house (VO2max ceiling) model

- Growth spurt/ceiling: with continued intensive aerobic focus FTP runs out of room; a taller house is needed.

### [WKOC-C04-012] Generating power and endurance performance models

Quote (Joyner & Coyle): elite athletic performance involves integration of muscular, cardiovascular and neurological factors cooperating to transfer energy from aerobic and anaerobic ATP turnover into velocity and power.
Model: Performance velocity or power = (Performance VO2 (aerobic) + Performance O2 deficit (anaerobic)) x Gross mechanical efficiency. Performance VO2 relates to lactate threshold VO2 and maximal oxygen consumption; anaerobic term to total buffering capacity. Morphological components: muscle capillary density, stroke volume, max heart rate, hemoglobin content, aerobic enzyme activity, distribution of power output, % slow-twitch fibers, anthropometry and elasticity. Primary driver: VO2 cardiovascular work, stored energy, cardiac autonomic stress. Secondary: anaerobic glycolytic contribution, neuromuscular load, musculoskeletal strain. ### [WKOC-C04-013] Types of cycling fatigue and where it occurs

Two dominant conditioning scenarios: short-term intense exercise (about 0 to 2-3 min) and prolonged submaximal (everything beyond), with max aerobic (about 3 to 8-10 min) as a unique space between. A psychological factor sits over both: people surrender; most never truly fatigue.
Where fatigue occurs:
- Central/neural (CNS): proximal to the motor unit (muscle fibers plus nerve); limitation of the nerve's ability to generate a sustained signal; closely related to mental toughness (desire to quit is a precursor).
- Peripheral/metabolic: within the motor unit; reduced ability of the muscle fiber to contract.

### [WKOC-C04-014] Accumulation and depletion hypotheses of fatigue

Origin of fatigue is exercise-dependent: accumulation, depletion, or both.
Accumulation: buildup of metabolic by-products in the muscle fiber: lactate, hydrogen ions (H+), ammonia, inorganic phosphate. Lactate is the common marker, but at high intensity H+ accumulation interferes with force production (the burn); author says it is not simply lactate.
Depletion: (a) neural depletion of acetylcholine, released by nerve cells to signal other cells; (b) depletion of energy substrates: ATP, phosphagen/creatine phosphate, glycogen.
Substrate chain: one sprint depletes ATP; several sprints use CP; sets of sprints use glycogen, which replenishes ATP/CP.

### [WKOC-C04-016] Chronic fatigue (not overtraining): substrate, CNS, microtrauma

Substrate depletion: one sprint: ATP drops, fast recovery; several sprints: CP drops, several minutes; multiple sets of sprints: glycogen drops (used to replenish ATP/CP), hours to days, can take several days based on diet. If you train hard enough (volume, intensity, frequency) you are unlikely to replete 100% of glycogen each week, particularly on a lower-carb or hypocaloric diet; week after week often leads to lower glycogen, repeatedly linked to performance decreases.
CNS/neuroendocrine: Acute: testosterone increases; sympathetic and parasympathetic both increase. Chronic: cortisol increases, testosterone declines; sympathetic increases (fight or flight), parasympathetic decreases (recovery and regeneration).
Microtrauma: acute increases but heals; chronic accumulates.
Causes of fatigue list: cardiovascular/anaerobic; energy supply/depletion; neuromuscular; muscle trauma; biomechanical; thermoregulatory; psychological/motivational.

### [WKOC-C04-018] Intensive aerobic: definition, why it builds a bigger engine

### [WKOC-C05-003] Aerobic capacity

The ability to take in, transport, and use oxygen to sustain prolonged periods of aerobic/submaximal work. Aerobic capacity is the core of aerobic development.

### [WKOC-C05-004] Aerobic power

The ability to utilize oxygen in the production of energy to power muscular contractions (the "use" part of aerobic capacity). ### [WKOC-C05-006] AWC vs FRC

| Anaerobic work capacity (AWC) | Functional reserve capacity (FRC) |
| Total work that can be performed using only stored energy sources in the muscle (ATP, phosphocreatine, glycogen, oxygen bound to myoglobin) | Total work that can be done during continuous exercise above FTP before fatigue occurs; kJ or J/kg |
| Based on critical power | Based on functional threshold power |
FRC equals AWC plus the small aerobic contribution (the athlete is still breathing); "functional" = all power above threshold, a little aerobic and a lot anaerobic: the whole battery.

### [WKOC-C05-007] Central vs peripheral components of aerobic fitness

Central: ability of lungs, heart and vascular system to deliver oxygen to muscles via the bloodstream. Peripheral: ability of muscle cells to extract oxygen from blood and use it to make ATP, the fundamental unit of energy. Both need training; central drives the boat, peripheral is a secondary training demand. SIT in particular works the peripheral side.

### [WKOC-C05-008] Chronic aerobic fitness vs acute aerobic fitness

Chronic: building aerobic fitness from below; multi-system focus (aerobic, metabolic, neuromuscular); "depth" of fitness; growth TO VO2max. Acute: pulling up from above, increasing the ceiling ("raise the roof"); multi-capability focus (also some anaerobic and neuromuscular gains); "sharpness" of fitness; growth OF VO2max (the driver of the phase). Credit on slide: Arthur Lydiard.
Three formats mapped: Chronic foundation = high volume, lower intensity; Chronic = threshold training (higher volume, medium intensity); Acute = high-intensity training. Polarized and pyramidal are set aside as end results of executing tactics, not drivers.

### [WKOC-C05-010] Fatigue (two definitions)

1. An exercise-induced progressive decline in maximal force production (e.g., a maximal effort from the driveway: power duration curve falling from about 2,200 W).
2. The point when you can no longer maintain a given output of force (e.g., 242 W riding until cracked after 1 to 3 hours).
One is a relationship over time, one is a point in time.

### [WKOC-C05-011] Fatigue resistance (two definitions)

1. To resist or slow the rate of exercise-induced progressive decline in maximal force production (raise the curve, sustain more of it).
2. The ability to delay (extend) the point when you can no longer maintain a given output of force (e.g., hold 242 W for 90 minutes instead of fatiguing at an hour).
Linked to raising the power duration curve and extending it to the right (Pmax, FRC, FTP, stamina).

### [WKOC-C05-012] Functional reserve capacity (FRC)

FRC: the total amount of work that can be done during continuous exercise above FTP before fatigue occurs. Units: kJ or J/kg. Analogy: the "anaerobic battery". It starts full; going over threshold drains it, the harder the faster; Pmax is maximal drain. Once drained, rest must occur, and rest must occur under FTP; the further under FTP the faster it recharges, though it generally does not recharge totally (maybe once or twice, then stops filling back up). FRC is a measure of continuous exercise (common confusion point).

src: Ch 1 | Functional Reserve Capacity

### [WKOC-C05-013] Functional threshold power (FTP) and quasi-steady state

FTP: the highest power a rider can maintain in a quasi-steady state without fatiguing. Quasi-steady state: roughly under a 5% range (author flags the number as a loose illustration); e.g., FTP 300 W means about 293-307 W. FTP is aerobic: the maximal sustained aerobic steady state. It was created by Dr. Andrew Coggan as a field (power meter) functional estimate of LT2 / anaerobic threshold / maximal lactate steady state (originally a ride of about a 40K time trial as hard as possible).
What FTP is NOT (slide, not discussed): highest power for one hour; normalized power for a hard one hour; 95% of a 20-minute maximal power test.

- **Modeled VO2max**: VO2max estimated from the power duration model. (Ch. 1)
- **Phenotype**: The athlete's strengths and limiters as shown by the shape of the power duration curve. (Ch. 1, 6)

### [WKOC-C05-016] Glossary B.1 — Training level and interval terms (iLevels, Optimized intervals, Classic levels, Extensive, Intensive, TiZ/TTZ, SST, TAN, Edge interval, The Grind)

- **Edge interval**: An interval that uses power and time as the edge of a zone (for example one minute at the upper edge of FRC/FTP). (Ch. 3)
- **The Grind / long tempo**: Long tempo-pace sessions used in the aerobic foundation phase. (Ch. 3)

### [WKOC-C05-017] Glossary of mid/late base terminology (Ch 7)

- **Intensive Aerobic Training**: Training situated physiologically between LT1 (lactate threshold 1) and LT2 (lactate threshold 2 / FTP), predominantly encompassing tempo and sweet spot (88–93% FTP). Its primary objective during mid/late base is to increase the size of the aerobic engine with high TSS/CTL efficiency and high repeatability before formal threshold and VO2max blocks.
- **SDT (Self-Determined Training)**: An individualized operational rule assigned to the athlete for each mesocycle governing how to capitalize on days when they feel exceptionally good. In mid/late base, the SDT rule mandates expanding time in zone (TIZ), never increasing power or intensity.
- **ABP (Always Be Pushing)**: The coaching heuristic dictating continuous advancement of training load, duration, or time in zone at the fastest rate the athlete can absorb, recover, and adapt. Heuristic check: performing the same workout more than three times indicates insufficient progression or miscalibrated thresholds/iLevels.
- **The Slow Wave**: The progressive, gradual transition of training intensity distribution from high-volume low-intensity training (HVLIT) toward pyramidal training, steadily expanding Zone 2 (LT1 to LT2) and Zone 3 volume while maintaining overall volume progression.
- **Plate Spinner**: The coaching metaphor for simultaneously managing multiple interacting physiological capacities during mid/late base (e.g. aerobic efficiency, threshold, and glycolytic capacity/VLamax), ensuring secondary qualities do not decay excessively while focusing on primary aerobic engine development.

### [WKOC-C05-022] Lactate thresholds: LT1/AT, LT2/MLSS/OBLA, VO2max, efficiency, TAN

- LT1 / AT (aerobic threshold): point where lactate first rises above baseline (inflection; line turns upward).
- LT2 / MLSS / anaerobic threshold: highest exercise intensity at which lactate production and clearance are balanced (maximal lactate steady state). OBLA: intensity at which lactate starts to continuously accumulate; tends to be slightly before MLSS. Author treats them as generally similar, reflecting the second inflection point.
- VO2max: maximum amount of oxygen the body can use during a specified period of usually intense exercise; power at VO2max is maximal aerobic power.
- Efficiency: the link between ATP turnover and external power output (energy in, power out). End goal is velocity, not power.
- TAN: Tough As Nails (name from Dr. Andy Coggan): longer mixed-pace tempo ride.
Physiological zones: three (Zone 1 below LT1, Zone 2 LT1-LT2, Zone 3 LT2 to VO2max); training starts at about 50% VO2max.

### [WKOC-C05-033] Pmax

Pmax: the maximal power that can be generated for a very short period. Units: watts or W/kg. It is the maximum power over at least a full pedal revolution with both legs.
Why not max 1 s: noise and data spikes (e.g. a 1,800 W one-second spike when the rider cannot repeat ~1,100 W is likely a spike); at 120 rpm devices record once a second so data timing within a revolution is uncertain. Why not max 5 s: fatigue shows in a 5 s sprint. Pmax uses a modeled full revolution (multiple model points), avoids spikes, balances recording-rate issues, and is not affected by fatigue. It can also help identify power spikes.

### [WKOC-C05-034] SAID, GIGO, perma-peak, limiter, SDT (terms as used in Chapter 8)

- SAID: specific adaptation to imposed demands; a rapid SAID response is expected from quality intensive work.
- GIGO: garbage in, garbage out; poor-quality work yields poor adaptation.
- Limiter: a weakness that can stop you from winning (distinct from a mere weakness).
- Perma-peak: holding a slightly sub-maximal peak all season.
- SDT: self-determined training, what the athlete adds on a good day (at peak: more watts, not more intervals).
- Booster: the weekly long easy ride that maintains aerobic capacity.
- Plus-one principle: each hard day does a little more than the last.
- Capacity vs. power equation: the coach's decision about where the athlete sits at peak start.

### [WKOC-C05-039] Stress vs strain

In power training:
- **Stress:** An external mechanical measurement of workload performed, calculated as force applied to the pedals multiplied by pedaling cadence (measured in watts).
- **Strain:** The internal physiological response and biological cost experienced by the body during that workload.
During maximal repeat intervals, as fatigue sets in and watts drop, external stress decreases while internal strain remains extremely high due to the progressive removal of anaerobic work.

### [WKOC-C05-041] Time to exhaustion (TTE)

The maximum duration for which a power equal to FTP can be maintained (when power at threshold starts dropping off irreversibly). Read it together with mFTP; with a very flat model it can be tricky.
Two ways to improve: (1) Increase FTP: lift the PD curve (more power in quasi-steady state); this typically also raises TTE. (2) Extend TTE: push the curve to the right (maximum duration at FTP power).
Author's point: TTEs of 30-35 min are common because modern training is mostly 2x20 and few intervals are hard beyond 20 min. To extend TTE apply specific overload, e.g. 1x40, 1x60 or 2x40 instead of 2x20 of SST (specificity principle).

### [WKOC-C05-045] VO2max

The highest rate of oxygen consumption attainable during maximal or exhaustive work; the absolute cap (ceiling) of aerobic capability. In the fitness house metaphor, VO2max is the ceiling: training grows FTP toward the ceiling in the "aerobic house"; "raise the roof" = pull the ceiling up via extensive-to-intensive anaerobic work.

### [WKOC-C06-021] Training intensity definition and three-zone model

Training intensity = level of effort relative to maximum. Training intensity distribution (TID) is determined from the percentage of time at low (zone 1, below LT or VT), moderate (zone 2, between LT and MLSS or RCT) and high (zone 3, above MLSS or RCT) intensity. Three-zone: Zone 1 below LT1; Zone 2 between LT1 and LT2; Zone 3 above LT2. Mapped to WKO zones: Zone 1 up to LT1/VT1 (~50% VO2max in the diagram), Zone 2 between LT1/VT1 and LT2/VT2/MLSS, Zone 3 above MLSS to 100% VO2max.
Classic levels vs iLevels (alignment table): 1 Recovery; 2 Endurance (aerobic endurance, extensive); 3 Tempo; 4 Lactate threshold (FTP) / iLevel 4a Sweet spot and 4 Threshold (FTP); 5 VO2max / iLevel 5 FRC/FTP; 6 Anaerobic capacity / iLevel 6 FRC; 7 Neuromuscular power / iLevel 7a Pmax/FRC and 7 Pmax. Training targets: aerobic endurance (extensive), aerobic power (intensive), anaerobic endurance (extensive), anaerobic power (intensive), maximal power.
Author's use: three-zone is easier to manage, but he plans time in zone and uses both iLevels and the three-zone model together. Zone 1 has a small role in early base; spending much time in Zone 1 outside active recovery is too little. He believes in time around LT1.
Review monitoring charts: Polarized Training Monthly (<65%, 65–85%, >85% VO2max), Time at or over FTP (% time over 105% and 90% FTP; example avg 3.7% and 12.0%; lots of mid-range yellow time all year is a recipe for stagnation).

### [WKOC-C06-022] Training modalities (TID shapes) and three core modalities

Eight TID shapes across Zones 1-3: A high volume–low intensity (HVLIT); B high intensity (HIT); C pyramidal; D inverse pyramidal; E threshold (peak in Zone 2); F polarized (peaks Zone 1 and 3, trough Zone 2); G inverse polarized; H even/equal/uniform. Many confuse threshold with pyramidal.

Three modalities at the core:
| | Threshold | Pyramid | Polarized |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Hard-easy daily format | 60–70% hard / 30–40% easy | 40–50% hard / 50–60% easy | 20–30% hard / 70–80% easy |
| Hard | ~Threshold, 4–5 workouts a week | Tempo/sweet spot, 2–4 workouts a week | HIIT, 1–3 workouts a week |
| Easy | Steady but easy | Sub (close to) LT1, 2–4 workouts a week | Sub (close to) LT1, 2–4 workouts a week |

Weekly rhythm examples (intensity score): Polarized Mon 2, Tue 10, Wed 4, Thu 4, Fri 2, Sat 10, Sun 4 (80/20; one to two very hard days a week, rest at or near LT1). Threshold: 2, 8, 8, 8, 2, 8, 4 (four fast group rides a week approximate this). Pyramidal: 2, 7, 4, 7, 2, 7, 5 (two to three days not as hard, using Zone 2 between the two LTs).
"Which is best?" "All roads lead to Rome": very individualized and highly affected by timing, hence modalities are used within a periodized plan. Author is a big fan of polarized but has issues with it, being refined by science.

### [WKOC-C06-024] Sweet spot 88-93% definition and rationale

Sweet spot (Dr. Coggan) = 88% to 93% (of FTP). Others expand to 85-90% and more; author targets 88-93% but accepts day-to-day range. Coggan chart: training effect peaks around L3/L4, physiological strain rises exponentially, maximum duration falls with intensity; sweet spot ellipse about 85-95% FTP.

## C08 Methodology & Progression

### [WKOC-C08-001] Optimized intervals: concept and use

iLevels describe; optimized intervals PRESCRIBE. The PD model has specific points of exertion (high points and transitions in energy-system contribution: ATP-CP, anaerobic glycolysis, aerobic/oxidative) that relate to how energy is made; these points are used to prescribe interval duration and power. Optimized intervals are given only from FTP up to Pmax (below threshold the numbers are close, no need). Energy system sequence: ATP-CP dominates first seconds (gone by ~15-20 s), glycolysis peaks ~25-35 s, oxidative takes over ~45-50 s and plateaus ~88% from ~90 s (simplified chart).
Guidance: be careful of over-precision (e.g. 2:44 can be prescribed as 2:45 or 3:00). Reps/time/recovery estimates come from a coaching consultation (Dean Golich and author). Progression is key: assign what the athlete can do correctly (e.g. 5 reps; then 6; 6 again; then 7, 8; or 2 to 3) and progress the workload; do not chase a mythical target.

### [WKOC-C08-002] Two ways to build FTP: high-frequency zone 2 vs low-frequency zone 3

Zone 2 (physiological; tempo and sweet spot, iLevels 3 and 4a): high-frequency, 3-5 times a week, progressive TiZ then power. Zone 3 (LT2 to VO2max; polarized/80-20 style): low-frequency, 1-2 times a week (2 for a mature athlete, one with 1+ years of training history), progressive power then time. Both have a role; don't call one good and one bad: the role is determined by time and periodization. Plain riding in zone 2 (classic endurance) alone also improves FTP (even 25-30 h/week would raise it a lot) but is not recommended as a strategy if time is limited, since time can be used better.

### [WKOC-C08-005] Interval modality and progression of TiZ

Modality = how total time in zone is assembled (intervals vs steady). Start with at least two intervals and progress toward one. Example: 2x20 SST -> 3x15 with 5 min rest -> 4x15 -> then reduce rest (3 min, then 2 min: 60 min with only 6 min rest), or 2x30 with 5-7 min rest. Always progress: start just beyond TTE, never start at the 200% target. Choose by event demand and rider ability: long steady events -> 2x30 or 1x45; riders facing 10-15 min hard efforts (road racing) -> shorter repeated intervals (e.g., 3x10, 4x10, 5x10); 40K TT specialist -> 2x20, 2x30, then 1x50. Don't repeat the same workout stagnantly: never more than two workouts at the same time range, typically one.

### [WKOC-C08-007] Building FTP: four-phase periodized progression

FTP is built as a periodized progression (progression of the work, not reverse/linear annual periodization; it may live inside an annual plan). Phases overlap, each starting before the previous ends:
1. FTP aerobic foundation / extensive aerobic training: base miles, tempo, long tempo.
2. Intensive aerobic training: TAN tempo, SST, FTP.
3. Extensive to intensive anaerobic training: FTP/FRC work, FRC work.
4. Intensive FTP / FTP speed: FTP speed work, moto pacing, training races, racing.
Rationale: physiological cost rises across phases (aerobic/threshold work costs less, above-threshold anaerobic work costs more). Maximize cheap gains first, do a quick push of anaerobic work to raise the ceiling, then return to FTP work to use the new room. Cusick considers polarized/80-20 and HIT-heavy approaches high-risk, medium-reward ("a crapshoot"); progression best increases odds of success (though they can be effective).

### [WKOC-C08-008] TTE development: intensive (up) vs extensive (out)

- Fitness does not improve in a uniform curve shift; the curve is sigmoidal, pushing one area can lower another.
- Intensive = push a point on the curve up (more power); also moves the connection point to the right. Extensive = push a point out (same power for longer; fatigue resistance, sustaining power longer).
- Ask for each workout: am I doing this intensively (up) or extensively (out)? Mixing both usually yields the worst of both worlds (80–90% of the time).
- Extending TTE (extensive) affects mFTP and stamina positively; FTP and stamina are highly correlated.

### [WKOC-C08-009] Building stamina

- Stamina correlates highly with FTP; raising FTP is among the primary drivers to improve stamina (slowing the downward decline of the power duration curve beyond 1 hour). Dr. Coggan predicted this close coupling, suggesting as an illustration that a 5% increase in FTP might yield roughly a 4% increase in stamina.
- Prescriptive strategy: build FTP in a manner that extends TTE, emphasizing the extensive side (long steady rides, fat-burning rides, TAN rides, and extended tempo).
- Cost-benefit caution on volume: "riding long" builds stamina, but eight-hour rides impose massive physiological fatigue. Coaches must balance volume versus recovery cost.
- Specific populations (ultra-endurance, Ironman, ultra-MTB): Cusick questions the cost-benefit of high-intensity roof-raising work for these athletes; he recommends prioritizing extensive aerobic work, TAN rides, and extended tempo.
- Stamina predictive utility: stamina models and predicts how well an athlete can sustain power beyond their longest ride duration on file (e.g. extending from a 4-hour historical ride to a 6- or 7-hour event).
- Practical anecdote: Cusick's participation in the Diabolical Double (125 miles, 17,000 ft climbing, 7 timed climbs, ~7:40 duration) demonstrated that a massive endurance ride drives stamina upward, but severe fueling lapses lead to cracking around 6.5 hours.
- Status of norms: population norms for stamina were not yet established during the WKO4 rollout.

### [WKOC-C08-014] Prescribing the acute phase: progression, recovery, fatigue management

- Progression principle rules: acute phase = short, hot, intense period of overreach (3-5 weeks); drive everything to the max.
- Risk: non-functional overreaching, overtraining, mental burnout; never extend to 6 weeks; 8 at absolute most.
- Rapid gains (numbers move every interval workout) can offset mental fatigue; show athletes results.
- Very physically and mentally demanding: break up with fun things.
- Recovery crucial: at least 48 hours between HIIT sessions to perform maximally (high intensity must be truly high).
- Vary quantities and format but progress quickly (short cycle).
- Benefit from high-volume low-intensity "filling": at least one ride a week slow, easy, low intensity, 5-6 hours; athlete reports five scenic things seen, to refresh the brain.
- The 3-5 weeks INCLUDE the off/recovery week (stress -> fatigue -> rest -> adaptation); not locked to calendar weeks.

### [WKOC-C08-015] Stack periodization: deeper base instead of longer cycles

Common mistake: to improve, athletes with capped hours (e.g., 10 to 12 h/week) extend cycles: base 12 becomes 20 weeks, build 6 becomes 12 weeks, starting earlier each year. Result: no increase in peak (permafit).

Aerobic FR = taller house (ceiling-height analogy of the FTP/stamina webinar); anaerobic FR = higher ceiling. Benefits: physiologically, psychologically and tactically prepared (athletes not fit enough cannot implement tactics when on the rivet).

### [WKOC-C08-018] Interval progression examples: tempo, SST, FTP (time in zone driver)

Numbers are illustrative; scale to training age. Break into intervals only to prepare the athlete mentally for the next, longer step, then return to the longer effort. Under threshold, 2 × 20 (short break of a couple of minutes) vs 1 × 40 steady gives no real physiological difference; the difference is psychological. Avoid the 20-minute-revolution trap of 2 × 20 to 3 × 15 to 6 × 10.
- Tempo: 1×30 → 2×20 → 1×45 → 2×30 → 1×60 → 2×45 → 1×90 min.
- SST: 1×15 → 2×20 → 3×15 → 2×25 → 1×50 → 4×15 → 3×20 → 2×30 → 1×60 min.
- FTP: 3×10 → 3×15 → 5×10 → 2×25 → 6×10 → 3×20 → 1×60 min.
Rate: progress really quickly; most coaches go too slow; keep trying to find what is too quick. Power progresses naturally, not driven (ABP, always be pushing). In base no reason to raise the curve; push it to the right. Longer efforts drive central/mental adaptation.

### [WKOC-C08-019] Max aerobic intervals: duration and progression

Max aerobic (formerly VO2max intervals): you are limited by VO2max, going as hard as possible aerobically. Research: best gain around 15 minutes and a little more; author rarely prescribes beyond 20 min of work, since most benefit comes at 15 (credit to Dean Golich's influence). Target at least 15 min of quality work (about 16 min total including roll-in/out); 15-20 min. Little time progression: progression is go harder, because the target is max (e.g., 5×3 min → 5×3 min go harder → 5×4 min still go harder). Don't start with a sprint in the first 30 s; find the natural watermark. Do not extend much past 20 min; just go harder.

### [WKOC-C08-021] Fatigue resistance strategy: four core coaching applications

Four ways to apply the fatigue resistance philosophy to athlete development:
1. **Annual Training Plan (ATP):** Incorporate fatigue resistance into seasonal planning rather than merely extending base/build durations.
2. **Training tactics and workouts:** Build sessions specifically targeting the accumulation and depletion aspects of fatigue (e.g., EPD SST intervals, crisscross SST, lactate stacking).
3. **Data-driven carrot-and-stick motivation:** Motivate athletes by demonstrating where their power drops off (TTE, PD curve intersection points) rather than obsessing solely over adding raw watts. Time and fatigue extension provide measurable targets.
4. **Stack periodization:** Deepen base fitness so the athlete can complete more work in the build phase ("the more you train, the more you can train").
*Practical illustration:* In fast group rides, riders who drop off after 15–20 minutes did not lack the power (they matched the pace initially); they lacked the fatigue resistance to maintain it.

### [WKOC-C08-024] Ramp rate as key measure of volume uptake

- Performance improvement is more triggered by the trend/rate of increase of volume than by pure volume alone (the two are related). A 1% increase per week gives slow gains; the most aggressive rate the athlete can recover from and adapt to gives faster gains.
- Find each athlete's progressive rate; ramp rate is the key driver, and is underutilized in planning and tracking.
- Look at 7 days and 28 days at all times; do not go beyond 28 days. Use the same fixed weeks (e.g. Monday to Sunday).
- Track on the WKO Performance Manager Chart with Insights (ramp rate line).
- Look at the athlete's history (CTL Compare with day counts, e.g. days over CTL 105, 90, 75, 60). Many days at high CTL (example: 222 days over 105 in the last year, rising each year) signals potential stagnation.
- Think in a 2-4 year cycle: e.g. peak CTL 100 in year one, 120 in year two, 140 in year three, rather than jumping from 50 to 150. This yields a higher peak by year 3 than jumping straight to the number, even if the athlete could handle the load. Over-volume uptick is a common error.

### [WKOC-C08-027] Early foundation phase: the Perfect World Principle

Dose: volume plus neuromuscular manipulation (cadence work). Response: increased mitochondrial density, muscle glycogen storage, aerobic efficiency, fat as fuel; the foundation of harder work to come.
- Manipulate: volume way up, intensity down; aggressive volume-based ramp rate that must fit the athlete's life.
- Modality: HVLIT. Low intensity = time at and around LT1 (fuzzy gray area, do not be over-precise).
- Manage: G.A.S. cycles of roughly 3–5 weeks, lower intensity, smart volume progression; not rigid "three on, one off"; microcycles can be longer depending on maturity and prior rest; in build/peak may do 1 on, 1 off.
- Outcome: with an aggressive ramp, performance probably declines for the first 3–5 weeks (fatigue comes first; fitness takes 4–8 weeks to layer in). Warn athletes.
Foundation volume distribution: progressive up to 10%, driven by volume ~@LT1; TSS measured; ramp rate 4–7 RR per training week (low/mid CTL), 3–5 RR per training week (high CTL); G.A.S. cycles 2- to 5-week; intensity control = microcycle extension (extend weeks on when intensity is low). Many athletes entered 2021 with higher CTL than ever (kept training through 2020), so adjust what counts as aggressive ramping.

### [WKOC-C08-028] Early base intensity: the slow wave and foundation intensity distribution

- Start with HVLIT (mostly Zone 1 / at or near LT1). Later in early base moving to mid-late base, evolve toward pyramidal ("the slow wave"): no reduction in volume (volume increases), part of the increase moves into Zone 2 (aerobic power → tempo → sweet spot), transitional prep for pyramid. ### [WKOC-C08-029] Neuromuscular manipulation (cadence work) in early base

- Watts = torque × angular velocity. Indoor platforms (Zwift, RGT) turn us into cadence drones (e.g. about 90–95 rpm), narrowing the range at which we make power. Outdoor/gravel needs power at a range (e.g. 65, 85, 105 rpm). Goal: make watts across a range of cadences. Efficiency/economy gains are marginal for experienced cyclists, but no reason not to get them.
- Prescription: add cadence work to workouts and cadence targets to intervals; early, mostly high cadence, with some low cadence to widen range. Rollers (intra-workout fast-pedal periods).
- Rationale: keep strain on the cardiovascular system (cardiac preload) and avoid muscular fatigue/metabolic strain now; high cadence = more contractions at lower contractile force. Example: 5 × 3 min at 110% of threshold at 95–100+ rpm.
- Left/right balance: check with left/right meter (bilateral PD curves, gross power released/absorbed); typical response is also high-cadence roller riding and high-variance cadence days.
- Track: cadence by iLevel heat map over 28- or 35-day cycles; goal is for the spread to condense ("accordion") with neuromuscular work.

### [WKOC-C08-033] Early base recap (perfect world principle) vs mid/late base process

Early base: large volume, low intensity; aggressive volume-based ramp rate; HVLIT; manage fitness/freshness in 3- to 5-week cycles; initial performance declines (athlete is tired, growing).
Mid/late base:
- Manipulate: volume stays (probably not reduced overall unless forced) but shrinks as % of focus; intensity increases, mostly aerobic intensity between LT1 and LT2 (tempo, sweet spot); keep a small amount of high intensity every 8-14 workouts depending on limiters, weaknesses and goals; fatigue resistance work (progressive workouts started in early base, more here); progressive addition of time in intensive aerobic training; continued TSS accumulation (earned increasingly in the LT1-LT2 space).
- Neuromuscular: early phase more high-cadence work; now more big-gear/force pedaling.
- Via: pyramidal.
- Manage: cycles shorten from 3-5 weeks to 2-3 weeks (depends on athletic maturity, training history, current CTL, age; author at 54 cannot always hold 3-week cycles).
- Outcome: higher ATL, ATL ramp rate picks up, more fatigue, inconsistent performance (a great Tuesday then a bad Wednesday). Normal growth, improvement is not linear. Good fitness/freshness management is needed because "inconsistency can disguise bad pretty well".

### [WKOC-C08-034] Volume: ramp rate from aggressive to managed in mid/late base

- Early base: aggressive ramp rate through volume (about 7 or more TSS/day per week; track 7- and 28-day from same starting day).
- Mid/late base: reduce progressive ramp rate as intensity is added; targets decline from aggressive to managed (3-5 TSS/day), history matters (guideline, not absolute).
- Reasons: (1) acute fatigue/ATL rises with intensity (ramp rate predicts ATL); high-volume low-intensity gives a strong adaptive signal because the athlete is not beaten down, while tempo/sweet spot accumulate fatigue and damage, so lighten progression to leave capacity to send and respond to the adaptive signal. Too much sweet spot (4-6 days/week) with aggressive ramp dulls the response. (2) Time cap: with e.g. 12 h/week you cannot cheat the duration limit by doing more/harder sweet spot daily; large steps need volume investment; otherwise control CTL ramp.
- Exceptions: athletes who have done it for years and adapt to higher numbers may ramp more; use one or two weeks as an adaptation test; use historical data.
- Convince athletes to invest 1-2 more hours/week during this roughly 8-week period; it lets volume keep progressing.

### [WKOC-C08-035] Load density and frequency: HVLIT to pyramidal weekly evolution

- Early phase: one good long day, one semi-long day, some okay long days, maybe active recovery. Small intensity every day.

### [WKOC-C08-039] Manipulation rule: time and intensity distribution from mid/late base into peak

### [WKOC-C08-040] 3–6 weeks at the core of the peak phase

- Core duration of intensive peak manipulation: 3–6 weeks (may stretch to 3–8 if the athlete can only do two weeks on then needs rest, then two weeks on). Most of the gain occurs in the first 3–5 weeks.
- Mistake: 12 weeks of VO2max intervals. Continuing may yield 1–2% more but adds fatigue that reduces performance on the day.
- It is an intensive approach: significant increase in intensity load, a loud adaptive signal, and a rapid specific adaptation to imposed demands (SAID) if quality work is accomplished. Fear: garbage in, garbage out (GIGO).
- Athletes must be mentally and physically ready (a good foundation). Quality work includes falling off 10 W on the last interval while still refusing to quit.
- Bad-workout disease: over-prescribed work the athlete cannot do. If garbage appears in the 3–5 weeks (wrong capacity/power choice), pull back, rest about a week, reset and start again.
- Precursor to response (credit Kolie Moore): intensive VO2max or FTP blocks do not produce a response unless aerobic capacity is already high. Otherwise the alarm phase of G.A.S. overwhelms the body and the adaptive signal falls on deaf ears. Balance aerobic power/capacity; the author's opinion is that systems progress linearly.
- Example: an athlete who rides only Mondays and Thursdays and comes to a coach six weeks before nationals; five weeks of intensive VO2max can still fail without a foundation.

### [WKOC-C08-041] The +1 principle (progression at peak)

- Whatever hard work was done in a day, the next hard day does plus one, and so on. Progression becomes more important, not less, in the compact peak window. Do not stagnate; steadily increase time in zone (TIZ) and power outputs.
- The pace of progression depends on what that athlete can manage. Use the maximal progression the athlete can handle.
- Example: 7 × 3 min on / 3 min off VO2max = 21 minutes of work, maybe 11–12 minutes above 90% (or 95%) of VO2max, depending on the athlete. The next workout must have more. Options: first three intervals at 3:15 and the rest at 3:00 (+45 s), add a fourth for about +1 minute, or change to 6 × 4 minutes. This is not simply 6 × 3 to 8 × 3 or 9 × 3. The author's text is internally inconsistent in the example (7 × 3 versus 6 × 3), recorded as spoken.
- Power tracking is key: compare with what you did last time. The biggest influence on today's workout is yesterday's workout (performance, feel, RPE, recovery, sleep). The plan is written in advance but the next workout is tweaked based on feedback.

### [WKOC-C09-001] Cost/benefit of training and tracking each cycle

Principle: specific training gains somewhere and loses elsewhere; coach/athlete must judge whether the cost is worth it for the target event (e.g. a sprint gain that costs threshold may stop a road racer making breaks). Because the PD model is live, patterns can be seen in 4-week cycles (6-8 weeks in) with unstructured testing, no formal test needed; measure every training cycle and tweak or keep strategy early rather than waiting until season end.

### [WKOC-C09-002] Why a higher-anaerobic athlete does longer max aerobic intervals

Jane (more anaerobic, steeper curve) does max aerobic intervals of ~5:02 (round to 5:00) at 316–296 W, versus Joe 2:44 at 320–300 W: slightly lower power, longer time. Reasoning (admitted oversimplification): her anaerobic battery carries more charge, so she must burn it down in the first minute or two (30 s if very hard) so the aerobic/oxidative system is forced to supply the rest. The battery does not fully restore between intervals (hence tracking dFRC), so in intervals 3-5 the aerobic system is strained more. To build maximum aerobic capability, burn off the anaerobic side to stress the aerobic system, even if you are a pursuiter/sprinter.
Caution: prescriptions like "go home when power drops 5%" can make athletes leave the most important intervals undone.

### [WKOC-C09-004] Using the PD curve for race power and pacing

The PD curve does not predict performance; it estimates it, and is excellent for pacing. Time trial: estimate the duration (e.g. ~40 min), read the curve at that duration for a starting average power. Better: break the course into segments (e.g. long flat section then three hills in about 18-20 minutes), analyze each segment against the PD curve (how energy will be made and what it costs) and plan the whole. Road race: no single answer; phenotype gives initial insight; ask how you will win and what could make you lose, given your physiology.

### [WKOC-C09-005] When to progress between phases: mFTP as % of VO2max

Track VO2max and mFTP (chart Compare VO2max to mFTP as % - Cycling, Basic Cycling view, Power Duration Curve dashboard). Core measures of aerobic fitness: VO2max, threshold, and their relationship (HR-based decoupling and efficiency are only color commentary).
Rules:
- If mFTP is below ~81% of VO2max, keep doing extensive work; at least reach 80-81% before progressing.
- Flat VO2max and percentage for 2-3 weeks (stagnation) is a potential transition point; for trained athletes plateaus occur between 81 and 85%. 85% is about the maximum seen.
- At the VO2max ceiling, go to phase 3; VO2max rises and the percentage falls; phase 4 then pulls FTP up to its highest.
- Use the athlete's own history (highest VO2max and FTP percentage) as the target. Masters athletes: same percentages (81-85) as others; VO2max declines with age but FTP can still be trained to a good fraction of it. Exceptions: reasons such as an upcoming race/century may justify progressing earlier.

### [WKOC-C09-008] What FTP is not: estimation methods and their limits

- FTP is not necessarily: highest power for one hour; normalized power for a hard hour; 95% of a 20-minute maximal power test. These are estimates (Coggan's "seven deadly sins" of estimating FTP). Estimates are fine absent a better method.
- 1-hour power and hard-hour NP work for roughly 50% of people (bell curve); the rest do not fit.
- 95% of 20-min test works for about 50 to 60% of people; 95% is an average decline; an individual may be 93 or 97.
- Advice: if using 20 minutes as a fitness indicator, state it as maximum 20-minute power and base intervals on that, rather than inflating it via 95% to set a higher FTP.

### [WKOC-C09-009] Power vs TTE: the 2x20 trap and overstating FTP

- "More, more, more" power focus leads to a high-power approach to intervals: shorter total time in zone and lower-quality workouts. Cusick estimates (admittedly made-up) 60–70% of athletes fall into the "two times 20 rule": always 2×20 with progression only by more watts.
- Problem 1: getting good at ~40 minutes while declining after it (short TTE) means the real FTP is lower than the 2×20 power suggests.
- Problem 2: setting FTP too high (e.g. 2×20 at 290 W → FTP 280) makes SST targets (about 255–260 W) unachievable: first interval on the edge, second cracks (e.g. 248, 245 W); overstating FTP ruins workout quality.
- Prescribe progressive intervals: 2nd 20 min a little stronger than the 1st, 3rd a little stronger than the 2nd; at most the same or a 1–2% decline depending on level. If an athlete cannot hold the prescribed power (iLevels, classic levels, threshold or CP targets), the underlying number is wrong.
- Fatigue resistance is more often how races are won: prefer an athlete who is more fatigue resistant over one with 5 more watts at threshold or 50 more in the sprint.

### [WKOC-C09-011] When to transition from chronic to acute (tracking and judgment)

No single magic analytic: combine quantitative data with wisdom, intuition and the process designed from the athlete's diagnostic; track to a point of diminishing returns ("analytic-based intuition"). There is no set number; targets come from the athlete's own history (predictive analytics = using the past to predict).
Tools used:
1. VO2max/mFTP and MMP review chart (WKO4 chart library): relationship of VO2max and mFTP with peak performance numbers (top 10 EFs, greatest all time, 5-min MMP top 20). A "chronic target" = a historical point where the athlete was most aerobically fit / ready to move on. Example: Peak 20 EF; mFTP 344 W; VO2max 5.11 L/min; mFTP % VO2max 82.3%; 5-min MMP top 20 379 W; Avg Top 10 EF 1.98; Greatest 10 All Time EF 2.17; Chronic Target EF 2.06; Max VO2max All Time 5.42 L/min; VO2max range 5.16; Chronic Target VO2max 5.26 L/min. Review by weeks for plateau/diminishing returns.
2. Aerobic Power Review chart (EF and power-to-HR decoupling PWHR trend, e.g. EF 1.44, PWHR 8.37%, trend lines): trend-check across ranges (last week, 30 days, base); as the EF trend line flattens/diminishes, time to move on.
3. VO2 distribution tracking (TiZ vs VO2max): see table; transition targets relate to volume (23% of time means very different hours at 25 h/week vs 10-12 h/week); customize per athlete and look at where they transitioned before.

### [WKOC-C09-015] Q&A rules: race-specific work, TTE after mFTP update, lactate stacking, HR

- FRC intervals need long rest but races give none: FR extension is one tool; mimicking the race pattern is also most important. Mix the long-rest supermaximal workouts with race-specific controlled on-off work (lactate stacking intervals).
- Lactate stacking intervals target 85–90% of the duration/power; should not drop under 85%; start with four, then five, six.

- Heart rate: author sees little role; it is a trailing indicator; power is the driver. He records HR and HRV with athletes but never finds the connection wanted (too variable).
- Coaching: ask athletes in the initial interview how they win and how they lose; losses often describe fatigue resistance. Data targets (TTE, intersection points) can motivate as carrot or stick.

### [WKOC-C09-017] Self-determined training (SDT) and time-in-zone over watts in foundation

SDT = what the athlete adds to the prescription when feeling great (the coach is not there each workout). In foundation (prep): feeling great, increase volume. In Base 1/2: increase time in zone (TiZ), not watts. Example: 2 × 20 min SST, feeling great → do 2 × 25, not +10 W. Reason: improves conditioning and fatigue resistance, builds a platform; foundation is to train so you can train really hard later; resiliency, fatigue resistance, depth and breadth. Extra watts now builds fatigue that is not needed; preserve chronic fatigue until build/peak, then increase power ("throw it down harder"). In race: RPE / fatigue.
Transition targets (power first, since power is the greatest predictor of performance; metrics to understand). Prep/base: progression of LT1, close gap between LT1 and LT2/FTP, push lactate curve to the right while raising power and building VO2max; natural FTP growth. If low-volume work does not move VO2max or FTP, do more (not enough stimulus). Build/peak: power and clusters (repeatability), generally and specifically.

### [WKOC-C09-022] Pros and cons of intensive aerobic training; timing of sweet spot

Pros: efficient stress/strain versus time (high bang for the buck); lower fatigue, higher repeatability; superior response when timing is right (athlete prepared, rested, fed).

### [WKOC-C09-026] Capacity vs. power equation at the start of peak

Entering the peak, analyze where the athlete sits in the capacity versus power quadrant:
- High power, low capacity.
- High power, high capacity (good, though maybe reached too quickly).
- Low power, low capacity (bad training).
- Low power, high capacity (the author corrected the slide typo live).
High capacity means the physiological engine is well built. Example: an athlete returning from injury with controlled training had huge aerobic capacity but could not access power (heart rate not high, breathing fine, fuel fine, just no power). Her capacity was wasted unless she now trained power. Decision: build intensity and power, delivered to exploit that capacity, with no more capacity building.
- You must make the decision because there is not enough time to do both.
- Both capacity and power are needed at peak.
- Growth is not linear between the two. Humans respond uniquely, with ups and downs.
- Use power data versus power-duration metrics to place the athlete: VO2max (best indicator of cardiovascular fitness), threshold (best indicator of metabolic fitness), power at VO2max, FRC, Pmax. Compare actual power outputs with physiological metrics. In the example, five weeks (four for her) remained before racing, so the plan was to go all-in on power.
Capacity measurement (Q&A): capacity is how long the athlete can sustain a time at power (a form of time to exhaustion). Repeatability tests are also a form of capacity. The author uses history plus testing plus metrics, with a test workout or two at the end of base. He gives no single rule because event type changes everything (12-hour gravel, 45-minute crit, 90-minute XC, 30-minute TT).

### [WKOC-C09-030] Transition rules from Q&A: sweet spot to FTP, entering a VO2max block, VO2max time ceiling

- Sweet spot to FTP work: it depends (gut feel). Switch to power-based work when aerobic capacity is full and returns diminish (threshold is not moving, physiological stagnation). Example: holding 2½ hours at 3.6 W/kg in sweet spot on Zwift with a 4.0 W/kg threshold.
- Ready for a VO2max block: when progress stagnates (FTP or 60-min power was rising roughly linearly then flattens, even with other work). Physiologically, threshold becoming a higher percentage of VO2max (close to 85%) usually shows this, but not always. At about 4 hours a week and 75% this does not apply cleanly. He gives no single answer without athlete data.
- VO2max block completion: if the athlete consistently achieves 15 to 18 minutes above 95% of VO2max in workouts, returns diminish (the author says this is about the maximum load the body adapts to; anything over is more than needed). Then the athlete has capacity at maximum aerobic power but may lack power to go harder; shorten the interval, drop max aerobic targeting, shift to more power (e.g. FRC-style). Do not stagnate. Example: a time trialist with a huge system but little fast-twitch muscle needs some hard muscular work before VO2max work can be effective.
- mFTP as % of VO2max is not a capacity/power measure. VO2max indicates cardiovascular fitness; FTP indicates metabolic fitness (fuel in, byproduct out). It informs modality in mid-base.
- Aerobic capacity early: push LT1 as close to LT2 as possible.
- Tracking VO2max time: noisy because consecutive time is not known. He reviews workout by workout, records it in notes and tags workouts. VI can help describe workouts.

### [WKOC-C12-001] Stamina: use, building and population notes

### [WKOC-C12-004] Racing all season, perma-peak and maintenance (Q&A)

- If trained optimally, expect at best two true peaks a year. A perma-peak is possible all summer but about 3%, 5% or 7% below the true peak.

### [WKOC-C13-003] Cautions: acute high-intensity aerobic phase and chronic progression

- WHAT: HIIT acute aerobic block. LIMIT: at least 48 hours between HIIT sessions to perform maximally; acute phase duration 3–5 weeks typical, never extend past 6 weeks, 8 weeks absolute maximum (author prefers 4–5 weeks). WHO: athletes in acute / late build-to-race phase. WHY: rapid accumulation of heavy chronic physical fatigue, cardiac autonomic stress, and severe mental burnout; risk of non-functional overreaching and overtraining. EXCEPTION / mitigation: include a scheduled recovery/rest period within the 3–5 weeks; include at least one weekly 5–6 hour slow, easy, low-intensity "filling" ride to refresh the mind; show athletes rapid quantitative improvements to offset mental fatigue.
- WHAT: Recovery duration during max long intervals. LIMIT: do not extend recovery interval beyond 5 minutes (even when extending rest from 3 or 4 minutes to offset power degradation); stop the workout when there is a significant power drop-off (wattage drops naturally, but once true strain is lost, cease effort).
- WHAT: Passive recovery on climbs during VO2max intervals. LIMIT: avoid repeatedly performing max aerobic intervals uphill followed by coasting down (passive recovery). WHY: passive coasting drops residual VO2max and alters lactate buffering kinetics; active recovery (pedaling at ~1/3 of interval watts on flat or low grade) is necessary to sustain residual VO2max when recoveries are longer.
- WHAT: Repeated sprint training (RST) in highly fit athletes. LIMIT: do not rely on RST as a primary acute VO2max builder for well-trained athletes. WHY: fit athletes recover rapidly between micro-bursts (5–30 s), slowing lactate accumulation and delaying aerobic demand ramps, resulting in only 10–50% of work time spent at VO2max; RST can quickly lead to plateaus and diminishing returns without a deep chronic foundation.
- WHAT: Chronic aerobic phase extension. LIMIT: cap chronic phase at 10–16 weeks (author's recommendation; physiologists may suggest 20+). WHY: beyond 16 weeks, marginal aerobic gains are outweighed by prolonged chronic fatigue ("permafitness").
- WHAT: Off-season detraining. LIMIT: avoid allowing athletes to become excessively unfit in the non-competitive season. WHY: author's noted coaching mistake—rebuilding requires excessive catch-up training volume and delays structured progression.

### [WKOC-C13-004] Caution on low-carbohydrate or hypocaloric diets during hard training blocks

- **What:** Low-carbohydrate or hypocaloric diets combined with high-volume, high-intensity training.
- **Limit:** Avoid chronically restricting carbohydrates or calories during heavy training phases; ensure complete glycogen replenishment.
- **Who:** Endurance athletes undergoing substantial volume, intensity, and frequency to drive physiological adaptations.
- **Why:** Multiple sprint and interval workouts deplete muscle glycogen to restore ATP and creatine phosphate. When training hard enough for adaptation, an athlete is unlikely to replete 100% of glycogen each week if adhering to a low-carb or energy-restricted diet. Week-over-week glycogen deficits compound, causing chronic substrate depletion, neuroendocrine disruption (cortisol increases, testosterone drops), and persistent performance declines (chronic fatigue).
- **Exceptions:** None noted by author.

### [WKOC-C13-005] Cautions, limits, and contraindications from Chapter 6

- WHAT: Sudden volume spikes (e.g., jumping from 3 h/week to 25 h/week for 8 weeks). LIMIT: Prohibited; adhere strictly to training-age ramp rate tables. WHO: All athletes. WHY: Excessive volume causes acute and chronic fatigue overload, diminishing returns, and reduced performance.
- WHAT: Stacking baseline maximal tests (5 s, 1 min, 5 min, 20 min). LIMIT: Never perform multiple maximal baseline tests on the same day; each test must be executed on a separate, dedicated day. WHO: Any athlete establishing or updating a baseline power duration model. WHY: Baseline tests require true maximal efforts to generate an accurate PD curve.
- WHAT: Outdoor coasting time. LIMIT: Keep coasting under 8% of total workout duration (target range 5–10%); exceeding 10% coasting represents wasted, unproductive training time. WHO: Outdoor cyclists. WHY: High coasting percentages degrade training density and volume uptake; ~80% of excess coasting is poor habits/focus rather than terrain.
- WHAT: Ramp rate tracking duration. LIMIT: Do not evaluate ramp rate over time windows longer than 28 days; track only 7-day and 28-day windows across identical calendar periods (e.g., Monday–Sunday). WHO: All coaches and athletes. WHY: Longer windows mask acute load progression and physiological uptake dynamics.
- WHAT: Increasing wattage instead of time in zone during Foundation/Base. LIMIT: Prohibited during self-determined training progressions in early base; extend interval duration (e.g., turn 2 × 20 min into 2 × 25 min), never increase interval target power. WHO: Base/foundation athletes feeling exceptionally good. WHY: Adding intensity consumes acute and chronic fatigue reserves prematurely; foundation fatigue must be conserved so the athlete can train intensely during build and peak phases.
- WHAT: Two-a-day training sessions. LIMIT: Prescribe strictly during Base 1 and Base 2 only, and only if the cost-benefit analysis is positive; discontinue immediately if sleep is compromised, sympathetic/parasympathetic balance is disrupted, or fatigue becomes unmanageable. WHO: Highly disciplined athletes with exemplary recovery and restoration protocols. WHY: Induces heavy systemic and autonomic strain.
- WHAT: Condensing base training for physiologically immature athletes when races are imminent (e.g., 5 weeks away). LIMIT: Do not force an accelerated base-to-race progression in athletes lacking deep training maturity. WHO: Athletes with low training age or irregular training background. WHY: Extremely dangerous; high injury and burnout risk.
- WHAT: Sustained chronic training load (CTL stagnation). LIMIT: Avoid spending excessive continuous days at high CTL (e.g., >200 days/year over 105 CTL). WHO: Highly trained or self-coached riders. WHY: Drives physiological staleness and training stagnation; load must undulate via General Adaptation Syndrome (G.A.S.) overreach-and-recovery cycles.
- WHAT: Initial performance drop during early base volume ramping. LIMIT: Anticipate a temporary 3–5 week performance decline when initiating aggressive volume ramps. WHO: Athletes undergoing volume-driven base training. WHY: Fatigue precedes fitness; aerobic adaptation requires 4–8 weeks to consolidate. Coaches must explicitly warn athletes in advance.

### [WKOC-C13-006] Cautions: ramp rate, sweet spot overuse, chronic fatigue, feedback bias, FTP overestimation

- Do not keep an aggressive ramp rate when adding sweet spot/tempo; limit to managed (3-5 TSS/day) unless history proves otherwise. WHY: accumulating fatigue/damage weakens the adaptive signal; ramp rate predicts ATL.
- Avoid sweet spot 4-6 days/week; about 3 days/week (impulse rhythm). Dulls response.
- Do not start base with large sweet spot volume before foundation.
- Do not accumulate chronic fatigue in base for no benefit; athletes feeling good will push threshold 5-10 W too high.
- Athlete feedback during sweet spot may understate fatigue (sneaky fatigue).
- Indoor/Zwift riding can pull athletes to ride too hard near threshold; indoor FTP can be about 20 W higher.
- Do not repeat same workout over 3 times (not pushing) or progress too slowly; but do not move to FTP work too soon.
- Polarized for more than 6-10 weeks tends to plateau.
- No medical contraindications are given in this unit.

### [WKOC-C13-007] Cautions in the peak phase (Chapter 8)

- WHAT: intensive peak blocks (VO2max or FTP) without an aerobic foundation. LIMIT: avoid. WHO: athletes with low aerobic capacity or little base. WHY: the system cannot handle the stimulus, so there is no adaptation, only suffering.
- WHAT: peak block longer than about 3–6 weeks (up to 8 only with individualized on/off). WHY: most gains come in the first 3–5 weeks; more adds fatigue that reduces performance on the day.
- WHAT: adding intervals beyond prescription on a good day. WHY: over-fatigue and disrupted progression; add watts instead, without large jumps (1 W, not 10–20 W).
- WHAT: increasing or maintaining volume at peak. WHY: removes freshness needed for quality work.
- WHAT: starting with too much time at intensity. WHY: workouts become tolerated rather than absorbed. Start lower if unsure.
- WHAT: peak in this phase is a gamble (all chips on the table); monitor every workout and respond to fatigue, because the athlete can be pushed over the edge quickly.
- EXCEPTION: pull back about a week and reset if poor-quality workouts appear.

### [WKOC-C14-003] Endurance performance equation and acute aerobic estimation formulas

### 1. Joyner & Coyle Model of Endurance Performance Velocity / Power
$$\text{Performance Velocity or Power} = \left(\text{Performance } \mathrm{VO}_2\text{ (aerobic)} + \text{Performance } \mathrm{O}_2 \text{ deficit (anaerobic)}\right) \times \text{Gross Mechanical Efficiency}$$
- **Performance $\mathrm{VO}_2$ (aerobic):** determined by lactate threshold $\mathrm{VO}_2$ and maximal oxygen consumption ($\mathrm{VO}_2\mathrm{max}$). Morphological drivers: muscle capillary density, stroke volume, max heart rate, hemoglobin content, aerobic enzyme activity, % slow-twitch (Type I) muscle fibers.
- **Performance $\mathrm{O}_2$ deficit (anaerobic):** determined by total buffering capacity and glycolytic turnover.
- **Gross Mechanical Efficiency:** converts biochemical energy turnover into external work/speed; influenced by biomechanics, anthropometry and muscle elasticity.

### 2. Time at $\mathrm{VO}_2\mathrm{max}$ Estimation Formula: Max Long Intervals
$$\text{Estimated Time at } \mathrm{VO}_2\mathrm{max} \approx 0.60 \text{ to } 0.70 \times \text{Prescribed Work Interval Duration}$$
- *Condition:* power target properly anchored ($\ge 90\text{--}110\% \text{ v}\mathrm{VO}_2\mathrm{max}$) and intervals completed.
- *Mechanism:* it requires 1 to 2 minutes of sustained suprathreshold effort for the aerobic engine and oxygen uptake kinetics to ramp to true $\mathrm{VO}_2\mathrm{max}$.

### 3. Time at $\mathrm{VO}_2\mathrm{max}$ Estimation Formula: Repeated Sprint Training (RST)
$$\text{Estimated Time at } \mathrm{VO}_2\mathrm{max} \approx 0.10 \text{ to } 0.50 \times \text{Prescribed Work Duration}$$
- *Condition:* highly dependent on athlete training status; highly fit athletes recover faster between bursts and trend toward the lower bound ($10\%$).

### 4. Chronic Time-in-Zone (TiZ) Manual Progression Rule
$$\text{Target Duration}_{\text{new}} = \text{Target Duration}_{\text{previous}} \times 1.05 \quad (+5\%)$$
- *Application:* applied manually to tempo and SST durations rather than waiting for trailing WKO4 optimized interval recommendations to update.

### [WKOC-C14-004] Mechanical cycling power formula

$$ \text{Watts} = \text{Torque} \times \text{Angular Velocity} $$

Where:
- $\text{Watts}$ = total mechanical power output.
- $\text{Torque}$ = force applied to the pedals (how hard you pedal, contractile force per revolution).
- $\text{Angular Velocity}$ = rotational speed of the cranks (how fast you pedal, cadence in radians/second or rpm).

Applied principle: Producing power at high angular velocity (high cadence, e.g., 95–100+ rpm) reduces contractile torque and muscular strain while shifting metabolic demand to the cardiovascular system (maintaining cardiac preload). Conversely, indoor training drones riders into a narrow cadence band (e.g., 90–95 rpm), impairing their ability to generate torque across the broad range of cadences required in outdoor/gravel racing (e.g., 65–105 rpm).

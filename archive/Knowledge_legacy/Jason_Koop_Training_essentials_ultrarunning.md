## Methodological Framework & Core Philosophy

Koop's training philosophy is built on evidence from ultramarathon-specific research, research borrowed from analogous endurance disciplines (cycling, cross-country skiing, triathlon), and direct coaching observation. No stock training plans are provided; the book teaches athletes to construct individualized programs. The core claim is that "ultrarunning is not a longer marathon — it is a different sport altogether," and that performance optimization requires attending to a broader set of limiting factors than those governing marathon performance.

**Block periodization** is the preferred macro-structure: training is organized into sequential phases each targeting one primary intensity (RunningIntervals → TempoRun → SteadyStateRun/EnduranceRun), rather than mixing intensities week to week ("mixed-intensity micro-periodization"). This approach is favored because most ultrarunners are experienced athletes for whom concentrated stimuli produce greater adaptation, and because ultramarathon races are performed in a narrow, low-intensity band that rewards deep development of aerobic and sub-threshold physiology.

**Key philosophical postulates:**

> "Develop things that are the most specific to the event closest to that event, and develop the things least specific furthest away from it."

> "At some point during the season, incorporate each of the three critical workouts: SteadyStateRun, TempoRun, and RunningIntervals."

> "Work strengths closer to the race and weaknesses further away."

**Koop's Hierarchy of Ultramarathon Training Needs** (bottom to top = most to least fundamental):
1. Total volume of workload
2. Adequate rest
3. Wholesome nutrition (mostly plants, periodized)
4. Developed mental skills
5. Building a team (support network)
6. High-intensity training and distribution
7. Long- and short-range planning
8. Train the gut
9. Terrain-specific training
10. Training stimuli enhancements (altitude, heat, strength)
11. Taper

The hierarchy is explicitly designed to counter "chasing marginal gains" before foundational elements are optimized.

**CTS (Carmichael Training Systems)** nomenclature is used throughout; Koop is a CTS coach and the workout names (RecoveryRun, EnduranceRun, SteadyStateRun, TempoRun, RunningIntervals) carry precise definitions standardized across their coaching staff.

## Physiological Foundations

### Energy Systems

The book introduces three energy systems operating simultaneously, not in isolation — Koop terms this the **"Endurance String Theory"**: improving one part of the continuum lifts the entire string, but with proportionally decreasing upstream/downstream effects.

**ATP-PCr system:** Supports efforts under 8–10 seconds. Used for explosive movements (creek jumps, boulder bounds). Adequately developed through normal training in most endurance athletes.

**Lactic acid system (glycolytic system):** Described as "the high-speed express lane." Labeled "anaerobic" in common parlance, but the text clarifies this is a misnomer: energy demand outstrips aerobic supply, not that oxygen use ceases. Glycolysis converts glucose to lactate rapidly, delivering less total ATP per molecule but faster. 75% of lactate enters the bloodstream and is shuttled to mitochondria as fuel; 25% is sent to liver for gluconeogenesis. Lactate is not the cause of fatigue — it merely coincides with it. "The best way to utilize lactate is to reintegrate it back into normal aerobic metabolism."

**Lactate threshold:** The intensity at which lactate production outstrips clearance. One primary outcome of endurance training is raising the pace/power at which this occurs. Koop observes that elite ultrarunners commonly test with lactate threshold at 95–97% of VO2 max — interpreted not as elite fitness but as evidence of chronically under-developed VO2 max.

**Aerobic system:** "A flex-fuel engine." Burns carbohydrate, fat, and protein simultaneously; can adjust the mix by fuel availability and demand. Only by-products are water and CO2. Contains the mitochondria — "a muscle cell's power plants."

**VO2 max:** Maximum aerobic capacity, expressed in mL/kg/min. "One of the indicators (but not the only indicator) of your potential as an endurance athlete." Elite ultrarunners (male) test at approximately 60–70 mL/kg/min; elite female competitors approximately 55–65 mL/kg/min. "Raising the roof" of VO2 max is described as essential before investing in submaximal intensity work.

**Aerobic Deficiency Syndrome refutation:** Koop dismisses ADS (coined by Phil Maffetone) as scientifically unsupported. "As of this book's publishing, the term 'aerobic deficiency syndrome' appears nowhere in any scientific literature." Since endurance efforts beyond ~2 minutes derive nearly 100% of energy aerobically regardless of intensity, the premise that high-intensity work depletes the aerobic system is characterized as "complete and total nonsense."

### Five Fundamental Principles of Training

1. **Overload and Recovery** — The body must be stressed beyond current capability, then allowed to recover. Adaptation (plasma volume expansion, mitochondrial biogenesis, red blood cell volume increases) occurs during recovery, not during exercise. "When you kick up your feet, sleep, hydrate, and provide your body with adequate and proper nutrition, that's when your fitness improves."

2. **Progression** — Training must progressively advance. Koop critiques the common weekly-linear-progression model (each week harder than the last) as structurally flawed: it places the hardest workouts at the highest accumulated fatigue. He prefers phases where volume of intensity *decreases* across a block from week 1 to week 3, then resets after a recovery week.

3. **Individuality** — No two athletes respond identically to the same stimulus. Muscle fiber type distribution, genetic coding, psychological response to training all vary. No stock training plan is provided in the book.

4. **Specificity** — Training must resemble the target activity. For ultrarunning, this means matching the grade, surface, terrain type, and environmental conditions of the goal race, particularly in the final weeks.

5. **Systematic Approach** — All four preceding principles must be integrated simultaneously.

### Female Athlete Physiology

This section was authored by co-author Corrine Malcolm. Key differences relevant to ultrarunning performance:

**Substrate utilization:** Endurance-trained females exhibit 24–56% greater rates of fat oxidation compared to males, even at rest. They reach maximal fat oxidation at higher intensities. This is attributed to greater expression of genes coding for fatty acid metabolism (including CD36 protein) and the role of estrogen in enhancing lipid oxidation and downregulating glucose utilization. "One of the reasons 'fat-adapted' strategies are generally ineffective for females is because they are already efficient fat burners."

**Fatigability and muscle structure:** Females naturally have a higher percentage of Type I (fatigue-resistant, oxidative) muscle fibers. Males have larger fiber diameters, resulting in less blood flow per individual fiber during submaximal contractions, making them more fatigable. Females showed less peripheral fatigue than males after a 100K mountainous race (Temesi et al. 2015).

**GI distress:** Females report greater propensity for GI distress. Females have ~10% smaller stomachs, slower transit times, and slower gastric emptying rates. Mechanisms not fully understood but thought to involve estrogen/progesterone interactions.

**Decision-making/pacing:** Males tend to overestimate their abilities and have greater pace decline over the course of a race, possibly related to testosterone and associated impulsiveness and risk-taking behavior.

**Menstrual cycle:** Negative symptoms most likely during the early follicular phase (lowest estrogen). Estrogen protects against muscle damage, regulates glycogen use, and increases neural pathway activation. Current recommendation: approach training and competing independent of cycle phases in as personalized a manner as possible, by tracking individual cycle and logging feel/performance at different phases.

**RED-S (Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport):** Extends the former Female Athlete Triad concept to males as well. Low energy availability depresses metabolism, reproductive function, and bone health. Athletes can be in low energy availability without disordered eating — training demands are dynamically changing and easy to under-fuel.

**Menopause considerations:** Perimenopause typically begins mid-forties. VO2 max declines at 5–10% per decade after age 30. Key recommendations: prioritize sleep hygiene, add ergogenic strength training, watch for heat sensitivity and dulled thirst response, allow more recovery, increase protein timing postexercise.

### Overtraining / Under-Recovery

Koop uses the term "unexplained underperformance syndrome" (UUPS) rather than overtraining syndrome, to direct attention toward recovery deficits and to rule out confounds (RED-S, anemia, hypothyroidism, illness). Symptoms include unrelenting fatigue, acute performance decline, sleep disturbances, elevated resting heart rate, low HRV, mood disturbances, loss of appetite, frequent minor injuries.

## Intensity Model & Zone System

Koop uses a 5-level RPE system anchored to specific physiological targets. The primary measurement tool is **Rating of Perceived Exertion (RPE)** on a 1–10 scale, where the number × 10 ≈ % of VO2 max.

| Zone | Name | Physiological Anchor | RPE | Breathing / Talk Test | Notes |
|------|------|----------------------|-----|-----------------------|-------|
| 1 | RecoveryRun (RR) | Below aerobic threshold; active circulation only | 4–5 | Brisk-walk breathing; "story time" — full sentences | Not a true intensity zone; restorative only |
| 2 | EnduranceRun (ER) | Aerobic base; predominantly fat oxidation; ~50–65% VO2 max | 5–6 | Deep, rhythmic; comfortable conversation with pauses | "Forever pace"; race zone for most ultras |
| 3 | SteadyStateRun (SSR) | High aerobic; below lactate threshold; ~65–75% VO2 max | 7–8 | Labored but controlled; 2–3 sentences max | Continuous climbs > 20 min naturally fall here |
| 4 | TempoRun (TR) | At or near lactate threshold; ~75–85% VO2 max | 8–9 | Short, controlled; ~5–7 words per sentence | "All-out time trial pace for 40–90 min" |
| 5 | RunningIntervals (RI) | VO2 max intensity; ≥90% VO2 max consumption | 9–10 | No conversation; short/rapid breathing | Must exceed 10 total minutes ≥90% VO2 max per session |

**Why not heart rate?** Heart rate is confounded by core temperature, caffeine, excitement/nervousness, hydration, altitude, and fatigue. Critically, fatigue suppresses heart rate — causing athletes who train by HR to push harder when most depleted. Cardiac drift further elevates HR independently of effort. Koop concludes RPE is the "only accurate information you have" during a trail ultra.

**Running power meters** are criticized as producing algorithms rather than direct force measurement, unlike cycling power meters. "The differences are so great that even the phrase 'running power' is essentially meaningless." Their surface-insensitivity is a disqualifying flaw for trail running.

**Heart rate variability (HRV):** Acknowledged as a useful trend indicator (low HRV = poorly recovering autonomic nervous system) but not actionable in isolation. Requires at minimum 4-week baseline. Peaking/tapering and illness can both produce uncharacteristic readings. Performance data remain the primary recovery indicator.

## Training Principles & Periodization

### The Long-Range Plan (LRP)

The LRP is a season-long overview specifying which intensity blocks occur in which weeks, when volume peaks, what environmental/terrain adaptations to pursue, and when recovery weeks occur. It is structured by three guiding principles (applied in order):

**Principle 1:** Most specific physiology trained closest to race; least specific furthest away. Example: for a low-intensity 100-miler, RunningIntervals (least specific) are placed earliest in the season; EnduranceRun/SteadyStateRun (most specific) are placed in the final 8 weeks.

**Principle 2:** All three critical intensities (SteadyStateRun, TempoRun, RunningIntervals) must appear at least once in the season. The "string theory" requires that each energy-system segment be stressed for interconnected adaptations.

**Principle 3:** Weaknesses trained furthest from the race; strengths trained closest. Prevents arriving at race day with undertrained strengths.

**Phase length is determined by three factors:**
- **Intensity of the phase** — Higher-intensity phases (RI) are shorter (2–3 weeks); lower-intensity phases (SSR/ER) can run 8–12 weeks.
- **Significance** — More race-relevant phases are longer and closer to the race.
- **Diminishing returns** — Switch phases when performance stops improving after rest weeks.

**Block periodization vs. mixed-intensity periodization:** Research slightly favors block structures for trained athletes (García-Pallarés 2010; Mølmen et al. 2019). Mixed-intensity is described as appropriate for novice/collegiate athletes but suboptimal for experienced ultrarunners.

### The Short-Range Plan (SRP)

Three governing concepts:

**1. Do the biggest training load when freshest.** The hardest workout of a phase is the first workout after a recovery week. Volume of intensity then decreases across the phase. This inverts the common "progressive weekly overload" model, which Koop characterizes as placing the hardest work at maximum cumulative fatigue.

**2. Higher intensity = shorter adaptation, longer recovery, lower workout density.** RunningInterval phases: 2–3 weeks total. Recovery needed between RI sessions: more days than for TempoRuns. TempoRun phases: 3–4 weeks. SteadyStateRun phases: 3–6 weeks.

**3. Lower intensity = longer adaptation, shorter recovery, higher workout density.** SSR can be performed up to 4 days/week in peak weeks. TempoRun can be done on back-to-back days.

**Back-to-back hard days:** Advocated not to "teach running on tired legs" but because concentrated training load produces greater aerobic engine stimulus and allows one or two additional quality workouts per phase vs. spreading sessions out.

**When to apply a recovery phase (3-point checklist):**
1. Performance drops 3–8% on 2 of the last 3 workouts.
2. Feel progressively worse during runs on 3 consecutive days.
3. Phase has reached its maximum duration (4 weeks for RI, 4 weeks for TR, 6 weeks for SSR).

**Recovery phase architecture:** 4–6 days of 45–60 min RecoveryRuns. Beyond 7 days, fitness decay begins to exceed recovery benefit.

**Taper:** Defined as "progressive nonlinear reduction of the training load." Average performance gain from a well-executed taper: ~3% vs. ~2% from simple rest (Mujika and Padilla 2003) — a 1% difference that should not be over-indexed. Taper variables:
- **Volume:** Fast-decay reduction to 40–50% in week 1, 20–30% in week 2, <20% in week 3.
- **Intensity:** Reduce volume of intensity, not the intensity itself. Maintain workout type throughout.
- **Frequency:** Maintain or reduce by at most 1 day/week.
- **Environment:** Maintain specificity of terrain.

**Minimum Maximum training volumes:**

| Distance | Minimum Maximum Volume | Duration of Key Period |
|----------|------------------------|------------------------|
| 50K / 50 miles | 6 hrs/week | 3 weeks, starting 6 weeks before race |
| 100K / 100 miles | 9 hrs/week | 6 weeks, starting 9 weeks before race |

### Seiler's Hierarchy of Training Priorities (referenced)

Koop references and adapts Stephen Seiler's endurance training hierarchy: accumulate high training volume and time near lactate threshold before adding specialized techniques. He overlays his own ultramarathon hierarchy on top of this framework.

### Block Structure Example (Western States 100 athlete)

Order of intensity phases (earliest to latest): RunningIntervals → TempoRun → SteadyStateRun/EnduranceRun. Heat acclimation and terrain specificity concentrated in final 8 weeks. VO2 max work in early season (least race-specific). This reflects all three LRP principles simultaneously.

## Workout Catalog

### RecoveryRun (RR)

| Duration | Intensity (RPE) | Rest | Repetitions/Frequency |
|----------|-----------------|------|-----------------------|
| ≤60 min (typ. 40 min) | 4–5 | N/A | 2–3×/week |

Purpose: Loosen legs, increase circulation. Must be easy enough that it does not stress the system. Used between hard sessions and as the entire content of recovery phases.

### EnduranceRun (ER)

| Duration | Intensity (RPE) | Rest | Repetitions/Frequency |
|----------|-----------------|------|-----------------------|
| 30 min – 6+ hours | 5–6 | N/A | 2–6×/week |

Purpose: The base of all training. Surrounds focused interval sets. Must actively slow on uphills to avoid drifting into SSR territory.

---

### SteadyStateRun (SSR)

| Duration | Intensity (RPE) | Work:Rest | Frequency |
|----------|-----------------|-----------|-----------|
| Total 30 min–2 hrs at intensity | 7–8 | 5:1 to 8:1 | 2–4×/week in SSR phases |

Individual interval length: 20–60 min continuous. Typical workout: 2 × 30 min SSR with 4 min easy recovery.

**Examples from the book (Table 9.2):**

| Total ER Duration | Intervals | Recovery |
|-------------------|-----------|----------|
| 2-hr ER | 2 × 30-min SSR | 4 min between |
| 2-hr ER | 3 × 20-min SSR | 4 min between |
| 3-hr ER | 2 × 45-min SSR | 5 min between |

Terrain: flat, uphill, or slightly rolling. Most runners naturally drift into SSR on sustained climbs >20 min.

---

### TempoRun (TR)

| Duration | Intensity (RPE) | Work:Rest | Frequency |
|----------|-----------------|-----------|-----------|
| Total 40–75 min at intensity | 8–9 | 2:1 | 2–3×/week |

Individual interval length: 8–20 min. Typical workout: 3 × 12 min TR with 6 min easy recovery.

Described as "crucial for making you a faster and stronger runner." Drives mitochondrial density, plasma volume, lactate processing. "Most trained runners could sustain TempoRun intensity for 40–90 minutes in an all-out time trial." RPE creeps up gradually through the interval and set.

**Examples (Table 9.3):**

| Total ER Duration | Intervals | Recovery |
|-------------------|-----------|----------|
| 2-hr ER | 3 × 10-min TR | 5 min between |
| 2-hr ER | 4 × 8-min TR | 4 min between |
| 2.5-hr ER | 5 × 12-min TR | 6 min between |

---

### RunningIntervals (RI)

| Duration | Intensity (RPE) | Work:Rest | Frequency |
|----------|-----------------|-----------|-----------|
| Total 12–24 min at intensity | 9–10 | 1:1 | 2–3×/week |

Individual interval length: 2–4 min. Typical workout: 6 × 3 min RI with 3 min easy.

Key requirement: >10 total minutes at ≥90% VO2 max oxygen consumption (per Véronique Billat's research). Intervals shorter than 2 min don't achieve this threshold; intervals longer than ~4 min tend to elicit lower absolute intensities because athletes self-regulate.

**Optimal interval length for VO2 max** (sidebar, adapted from Seiler and Sjursen 2004): 2–4 min intervals at 1:1 work:rest maximize mean oxygen consumption. 1 min intervals are too short; 6 min intervals result in athletes pacing below VO2 max.

**Examples (Table 9.4):**

| Total ER Duration | Intervals | Recovery |
|-------------------|-----------|----------|
| 90-min ER | 6 × 3-min RI | 3 min between |
| 90-min ER | 4 × 4-min RI | 4 min between |
| 90-min ER | 8 × 2-min RI | 2 min between |

**Terrain preference:** Uphill strongly preferred. Uphill RI: higher cardiovascular response, reduced ground reaction forces (injury hedge), more race-specific for mountainous events. "RunningIntervals should never be done downhill, as it will be nearly impossible to achieve >90% of your VO2 max in this condition."

---

### Peak and Fade RunningIntervals (Advanced)

Same workout template as standard RI (e.g., 6 × 3 min), but each interval is begun at the highest sustainable intensity for 60–90 seconds, then allowed to fade. Result: higher mean and absolute VO2 levels than evenly paced intervals (Rønnestad et al. 2019). Requires 2–3 years of ultra running experience and prior interval work. "You are intentionally starting each interval at an unsustainable intensity."

### The Four Disciplines of Ultrarunning (Chapter 5)

Koop categorizes ultrarunning locomotion into four disciplines, each with distinct biomechanics requiring specific training:

1. **Flat running** — 2.5–3× bodyweight GRFs; standard running economy applies.
2. **Uphill running** — Preferred for intervals (~80% of all intervals prescribed). Lower GRFs than flat at equivalent effort; higher cardiovascular response; more race-specific for mountain ultras.
3. **Downhill running** — Much larger GRFs at race speed (athletes run downhills faster than uphills). Primary source of eccentric muscle damage and prolonged recovery. "A little downhill work goes a long way, and the effects last a long time." Avoid high-volume downhill intervals.
4. **Power-hiking / Walking** — GRFs only ~1.2× bodyweight. Completely different EMG activation patterns from running. Must be trained specifically if the race requires it.

**Terrain specificity formula:**

```
Average elevation change per mile = (total gain ft + total loss ft) / total miles
```

Example — Western States 100: (18,090 + 22,970) / 100.2 = **410 ft of change per mile**. Athletes should approximate this average across a week of training, particularly in final weeks.

**When to hike vs. run:** No universal grade threshold exists. Current research (University of Colorado Boulder steep treadmill lab) suggests both speed and grade determine the metabolically optimal transition. On flat ground, the preferred walk-to-run transition speed is ~13:30–14 min/mi (below this, running is less economical than walking). Practical guidance:
1. Practice power-hiking in training if the race requires it.
2. Use RPE to govern — hike when RPE exceeds race plan, run when it doesn't.
3. For longer/steeper climbs, prioritize hiking; for shorter climbs, consider running.

**Poles:** Recommended when the race features substantial power-hiking and vertical. Begin training with them ≥4 weeks pre-race. Sizing: ~68% of height in centimeters (Hansen and Smith 2009).

### Strength Training

Two categories are defined:

**"Strength training"** — Progressive resistance training targeting strength, hypertrophy, or power. Fatiguing. Trade-off with running quality must be managed.

**"Corrective exercise training"** — Addresses imbalances via bodyweight, resistance bands, unstable surfaces. Low fatigue cost. Suitable for most runners year-round.

**Five fundamental movement patterns for strength training:**
1. Push (push-ups, press)
2. Pull (rows, pull-ups)
3. Hinge (Romanian deadlift)
4. Squat (squat, lunge, reverse lunge)
5. Carry / dynamic core (farmer's carry, medicine ball slam)

**Seasonal strength programming:**

| Season Phase | Sets × Reps | Intensity | Sessions/Week |
|---|---|---|---|
| Early season (months 1–3) | 1–5 × 1–5 | Heavy | 2 |
| Mid season (months 4–7) | 3 × 8–12 | Moderate (1–2 reps in reserve) | 2 |
| Late season (months 8–9) | Corrective only; 2–3 × 12 or 15–30 sec | Light | 1–2 |

A 66% reduction in injury risk from strength training is cited (Lauersen 2018 meta-analysis), comparable to sleeping >8 hours/night. Body weight gain from concurrent strength + endurance training is minimal due to mTOR suppression by AMP-activated kinase.

## Athlete Assessment & Testing Protocols

### Physiology Lab Testing

Koop uses lab-based lactate threshold and VO2 max testing primarily as **longitudinal benchmarks**, not to set training zones (RPE is used for that). The most diagnostically useful finding from testing ultrarunners:

> Elite ultrarunners frequently record a lactate threshold at 95–97% of VO2 max. "What it actually shows is where most ultrarunners are underdeveloped. VO2 max is your ceiling. If you want to fit more stuff in the building, raise the roof."

This finding drives the prescription of RunningInterval phases even for athletes whose goal events are low-intensity.

### GPS and Training Platforms

**GPS watches:** Record at 1-second intervals in highest-quality mode. Key metrics:
- **Normalized Graded Pace (NGP)** (TrainingPeaks) — Converts uphill/downhill pace to flat-equivalent pace. Limitation: does not account for surface differences or underestimates downhill stress.
- **Grade Adjusted Pace (GAP)** (Strava) — Same concept, similar limitations.
- **Training Stress Score (TSS/rTSS/hrTSS)** (TrainingPeaks) — Calibrated to 100 pts = 60 min at lactate threshold intensity. Cumulative ATL (7-day rolling) and CTL (42-day rolling) track fitness and fatigue. Limitation: equates endurance and intensity stress on a single scale; underestimates descending stress.
- **Relative Effort** (Strava) — Heart-rate-based analogue of TSS (derived from Bannister's TRIMP system).

**Example TSS benchmarks:**

| Workout | Approx. TSS/rTSS |
|---|---|
| 60-min RecoveryRun | 50–80 |
| 90-min ER with 3×10-min TR | 100–150 |
| 90-min ER with 6×3-min RI | 100–150 |
| 2.5-hr EnduranceRun | 150–200 |
| 50-mile race | 400–600 |

Koop's workflow: Schedule workouts in TrainingPeaks → evaluate NGP/rTSS/Strava segments → layer with athlete subjective feedback → synthesize to drive decisions. "Very little, if any, of this information is actionable by itself."

### Day-to-Day Hydration Assessment (WUT Diagram)

Three morning indicators (Cheuvront and Sawka 2005):
- **W** = body weight (1–2% loss vs. previous day = dehydration signal)
- **U** = urine color (apple juice or darker = dehydration signal; clear may mean overhydration)
- **T** = thirst (present = dehydration signal)

If 2 of 3 indicators flag dehydration: likely dehydrated. If all 3: very likely. Use as a rolling 3-day pattern, not single-day alarm.

### Sweat Rate Test (Figure 13.5)

1. Weigh without clothing before run.
2. Run at known intensity/temperature (no drinking, or record exactly how much consumed).
3. Weigh without clothing after run.
4. Weight loss (adjusted for fluid consumed) = sweat loss per unit time.

Used to set fluid targets for specific race conditions; must be repeated across temperature ranges.

## Nutrition & Recovery

### Essential Macronutrient Principles

**Carbohydrate** is the mission-critical race fuel. Fat is a large reservoir but energetically more expensive (requires ~8% more oxygen to liberate equivalent energy). Protein contributes 10–15% of energy even at high intensities; primary roles are muscle repair, immune function, and enzyme production.

**Crossover point:** At ~65% VO2 max, fuel is approximately 50% fat / 50% carbohydrate. At ultramarathon intensities (<65% VO2 max), carbohydrate demand is lower than in shorter events. A 70 kg runner at 10 km/hr uses ~700 cal/hr; ~350 cal/hr from carbohydrate.

### During-Exercise Nutrition Targets

| Nutrient | Target | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Carbohydrate | 60–90 g/hr (typically; lower adequate for ultras) | Start at 60 g/hr; adjust by intensity. Multiple sugar types (glucose + fructose) increase absorption ceiling to ~84 g/hr |
| Fluid | 16–32 oz/hr | Adjusted for temperature, sweat rate, intensity |
| Sodium | 600–800 mg per 32 oz (1 L) fluid consumed | From drinks + foods + gels combined; not solely from salt tablets |
| Calories | 200–300 cal/hr (after first hour) | ~90% carbohydrate; elites may reach 400 cal/hr |

**"Calories in your pocket, hydration in your bottles":** Koop recommends separating calorie delivery (solid foods, gels) from hydration delivery (water or light electrolyte drink) to allow independent adjustment of each as conditions change.

### Preworkout Nutrition

| Time Before Workout | Carbohydrate |
|---|---|
| 3 hours | 1.5 g/kg bodyweight |
| 2 hours | 1 g/kg bodyweight |
| <1 hour | Small, easily digestible snack |

Avoid high-fiber foods in final meal before intervals.

### Postworkout Nutrition

Recovery beverages only needed if: workout >3 hours, OR workout >2 hours with structured intensity, AND no access to a quality meal within 30–60 min. Otherwise, a normal balanced meal achieves the same glycogen restoration within 24 hours.

Post-run hydration: consume fluid = 1.5× weight lost during session + ~500 mg sodium per liter within 2 hours.

### Fat Adaptation

Koop's position: full-time low-carbohydrate or ketogenic diets are not recommended for ultrarunners. Key reasons:
1. Ultrarunners are already efficient fat-burners by virtue of high training volume at low intensity.
2. LCHF impairs carbohydrate transport and processing even in small amounts, increasing GI distress risk.
3. Economy penalty: fat requires ~8% more oxygen per equivalent energy unit.
4. Training quality suffers: reduced capacity for high-intensity work (TempoRun, RI).
5. Potential bone stress injury risk (increased CTX, decreased P1NP after 3.5 weeks; Heikura et al. 2020).
6. Adherence is difficult and effect is transient.

**Low-risk fat metabolism strategies:**
- Periodize carbohydrate: ~50% carbs during EnduranceRun/SSR phases, ~60% during TR/RI phases.
- Early morning EnduranceRuns up to 2 hours in fasted state (overnight fast only).
- Two-a-day training: interval session (fueled) followed by EnduranceRun (lower carb availability).

**Dietary strategies table:**

| Strategy | Carbohydrate Content |
|---|---|
| Ketogenic diet | <50 g/day |
| Low-carbohydrate high-fat (LCHF) | 15–30% of total calories |
| High-carbohydrate diet | 60–65% of total calories |
| ACSM consensus | 60% of total calories |

### Hydration States and Sodium Balance

Nine-combination grid (euhydrated/hypohydrated/overhydrated × normonatremic/hyponatremic/hypernatremic). Key findings:

| Combination | Occurrence | Most Dangerous? |
|---|---|---|
| Euhydrated + Normonatremic | Common (>30%) | No — target state |
| Overhydrated + Normonatremic | Common (>30%) | No — nausea/sloshing |
| Dehydrated + Normonatremic | Somewhat common (10–15%) | Heat stress risk |
| Euhydrated + Hyponatremic | Rare (3–6%) | Moderate |
| Dehydrated + Hyponatremic | Rare (3–6%) | Yes |
| Overhydrated + Hyponatremic | Rare (3–6%) | Yes — most dangerous; can be fatal |
| Dehydrated + Hypernatremic | Extremely rare (<1%) | Yes |
| Euhydrated + Hypernatremic | Extremely rare (<1%) | Moderate |
| Overhydrated + Hypernatremic | Extremely rare (<1%) | Moderate |

Hyponatremia in ultrarunning is "more often caused by overdrinking fluids that do not contain sodium than not consuming enough sodium." The "drink early and often" vs "drink to thirst" debate is characterized as a false dichotomy; sweat testing and awareness of state are preferred.

**NSAIDs:** Explicitly contraindicated for endurance athletes. Increase risk of hyponatremia and acute kidney injury. "The use of NSAIDs in endurance and ultra-endurance sports is dangerous. The research is unequivocal."

### Environmental Adaptations

**Altitude:**
- Fitness is the primary weapon; no altitude protocol substitutes for fitness.
- Live High, Train Low/High is the most efficacious protocol (Constantini, Wilhite, and Chapman 2017).
- Minimum effective chronic altitude: 5,900–9,840 ft (1,800–3,000 m).
- Minimum effective duration: 2–4 weeks for maximum adaptation; 1 week minimum to get past the worst acute response (peak discomfort at 24–72 hours).
- Ferritin <35 ng/mL blocks hematological adaptation even after 4 weeks (Constantini 2017) — check iron panel 3 months out.
- Altitude tent: evidence limited if living below 5,500 ft; possible benefit if used at simulated 8,200–9,840 ft for 9 hrs/night.
- Arrive early: 3–5 days early for races at 1,640–6,561 ft; 1–2 weeks for 6,561–9,842 ft; ≥3 weeks for >9,842 ft.

**Heat:**
- All acclimation methods work; "more is not better."
- Preferred method: **passive heating** (postexercise sauna or hot-water immersion) because it preserves workout quality and time-efficiency. Target core temp ~101°F (38.5°C) sustained ~90 minutes per session.
- Need 7–10 consecutive days for adaptation; maintain with sessions every 3 days.
- Decay rate after stopping: ~2.5%/day; full taper without heat exposure = ~35% HR control decay, ~6% core temp control decay, ~30% sweat rate decay.
- Two-phase protocol: first acclimation block ~6 weeks out; maintenance sessions every 3–4 days; second re-acclimation block in final week.
- Race-day cooling: ice on neck/torso, ice bandanas, cool fluids, loose light clothing. Maintaining hydration is essential to preserve sweat response.

## Special Populations & Conditions

### Time-Crunched Athletes

The "minimum maximum" concept (above) is the primary guidance tool. The training-to-event relationship is not linear: a 50-miler does not require twice the training of a marathon.

### 200-Mile Race Preparation

Sleep deprivation training has no physiological adaptive value — familiarization is the only benefit, at significant training quality cost. Recommended: no more than 1–2 overnight sessions for equipment/headlamp testing.

**Sleep extension protocol:** 9–10 hours/night for 2 weeks pre-race. Appears to improve cognitive performance during subsequent sleep-deprivation periods.

**In-race sleep:**
- Races <48 hrs: no sleep needed (with prior sleep extension).
- Races 48–72 hrs: 20–40-min trail-side naps near sunrise each day.
- Races >72 hrs: 90–120-minute sleep chunks starting during the second night.

**Witching hour (2–4 a.m.):** Circadian-driven drop in thermoregulatory control. Slowest paces and highest dropout rates consistently cluster at ~3:30 a.m. in field research (Brager et al. 2020).

**Nappuccino:** Consume caffeine immediately before a 20–40-min nap. Caffeine peaks as you wake, combining sleep refreshment with stimulant effect.

### Aging Athletes

Muscle mass declines accelerate after age 60. "Strength training isn't just corrective or ergogenic [for aging athletes], it's essential for general health and well-being." Shift some priority from running volume toward 2–3 heavy sessions/week year-round.

## Mental Skills

### Psychobiological Model of Fatigue (PBM)

"Physiological capacity determines the upper limit of performance capacity, but psychological skills are the deciding factor in how much of that capacity we ultimately utilize." (Marcora 2008, 2009.)

PBM posits that motivation and perception of effort regulate performance, not a protective brain "governor" (contra Noakes's central governor theory, which centered purely on brain-as-protector from catastrophe). PBM allows the athlete to voluntarily shift pacing, attention, and effort through mental skills — including using ergogenic aids like caffeine to alter effort perception.

### Perceived Exertion End Point Interaction

During any task, athletes continuously draw an internal line from current effort to projected end-state and adjust pacing accordingly. In ultramarathons, this forecasting fails because the duration is too long and cognitive capacity too diminished for accurate prediction. "Staying in the moment" (compartmentalizing effort) is effective precisely because it bypasses this mechanism and avoids catastrophically conservative early pacing or premature dropout.

### Mental Skills in Order of Development

1. **Associative focus** — Directing attention inward toward physiological cues (breathing, body sensations). Tools: body scan, follow-your-breath technique.
2. **Dissociative focus** — Redirecting attention away from discomfort. Tools: "count the purple things," elaborate meal planning during a run.
3. **Mindfulness (MAC approach)** — Accepting thoughts as impermanent, neither good nor bad; redirecting toward performance-relevant cues. Five-step framework (Gardner and Moore 2004): identify what matters → acquaint with mindfulness in benign settings → accept thoughts → field application → integrate and act.
4. **Imagery** — Mentally rehearsing race scenarios and crisis scenarios. 99% of Canadian Olympic athletes used imagery pre-competition (Orlick and Partington 1988). Two types: (a) rehearsing critical race sections to create "been there, done that" familiarity; (b) worst-case scenario rehearsal to automate crisis response.
5. **Self-talk** — Instructional ("plant poles in line with foot strike") or motivational ("you've trained hard for this"). IMPACT framework: Identify → Match → Practice → Ascertain → Create a plan → Train.

### Purpose / WHY Framework

Four-step process (adapted from Simon Sinek) to identify the deeper motivation for training beyond race outcomes. A strong WHY anchors motivation when races are cancelled or conditions deteriorate. Steps: (1) list consistent voluntary activities, (2) identify threads connecting them, (3) external validation, (4) craft a one-sentence WHY statement.

## Detected Internal Inconsistencies

1. **Aerobic Deficiency Syndrome claim vs. high-intensity caution:** Koop forcefully dismisses ADS as fictitious while simultaneously acknowledging that most ultrarunners chronically under-develop VO2 max. The practical recommendations (include RunningInterval phases) implicitly acknowledge that high-intensity work is systematically avoided by ultrarunners — the very behavior ADS proponents claim to observe — yet the physiology invoked to explain that avoidance (sufficient aerobic stimulus from low-intensity work) is the same mechanism Koop dismisses.

2. **VO2 max testing used but not to set zones:** Koop states lab testing is used as a benchmark, not to create training intensity ranges for ultrarunners, while simultaneously using the lab finding (lactate threshold at 95–97% of VO2 max) as a direct training prescription trigger (add RI phases). This creates an implicit zone from testing even while explicitly denying its use for zone definition.

3. **Block periodization preference vs. "all three intensities must be visited":** The block structure (one intensity per phase) is endorsed, yet Principle 2 of the LRP mandates that all three critical intensities appear every season. For very short seasons or athletes with limited time, fitting three complete blocks with adequate adaptation time may not be feasible — the text does not provide explicit resolution.

4. **LCHF for females specifically:** Chapter 2 (Malcolm) states "one of the reasons 'fat-adapted' strategies are generally ineffective for females is because they are already efficient fat burners," while Chapter 14 (Koop) discusses LCHF as a universal non-recommendation without differentiating by sex. These are directionally consistent but the strength of the female-specific argument differs between chapters.

5. **Recovery phase duration:** Chapter 12 states recovery phases should be 4–6 days. Chapter 8 states "training cessation for several weeks" causes >20% fitness loss but "adding as few as three sessions per week can keep losses in the 5–10% range even over several weeks." The 4–6-day recovery prescription appears more conservative than the detraining literature would strictly require, though this may reflect practical experience over pure physiology.

6. **Pace as race strategy:** Chapter 6 extensively argues against pace as a reliable intensity metric for trail running, yet Chapter 18's course guides and Chapter 16 describe pace-based racing strategies as appropriate for flat courses and for athletes who can train on the racecourse. This is a conditional resolution, not a contradiction, but the threshold between "pace is invalid" and "pace is acceptable" is not precisely defined.

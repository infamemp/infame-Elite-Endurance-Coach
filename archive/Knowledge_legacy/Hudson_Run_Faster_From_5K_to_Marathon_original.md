## 2. Philosophy & Core Principles

### PH-1 — Adaptive Running (the training philosophy)
A responsive, evolving, creative approach to training, opposed to rigid/formulaic one-size-fits-all programs. Named "adaptive running" by Fitzgerald; adaptation is its essence. No single formula works perfectly for every runner; responses can't be predicted; even a formula that works changes the runner, so the formula must change too.
tags: type:philosophy subtopic:adaptive-running level:general-population
[SRC: Ch.1] [EVIDENCE: author-opinion]

### PH-2 — Two classic self-knowledge mistakes runners make
1. Following someone else's recipe instead of developing one's own based on individual strengths/weaknesses/needs/goals.
2. Guiding training too much by plans and not enough by how the body responds.
tags: type:philosophy type:heuristic subtopic:self-coaching level:general-population
[SRC: Introduction] [EVIDENCE: author-opinion]

### PH-3 — Coach's true value = self-knowledge, not knowledge of training
The coach's biggest job is helping the runner gain knowledge of himself/herself as a runner; a second (coaching) perspective. Customization + responsiveness matter more than any "system."
tags: type:philosophy subtopic:self-coaching level:general-population
[SRC: Introduction] [EVIDENCE: author-opinion]

### PH-4 — 90/10 rule for the next workout
The details of any runner's next workout should be informed **90% by how he/she feels after today's workout** and only **10% by the pre-made training plan.** Train workout-to-workout, not plan-to-plan.
tags: type:philosophy type:heuristic subtopic:responsive-training level:general-population
[SRC: Introduction] [EVIDENCE: author-opinion]

### PH-5 — Plan in pencil, not ink
All planned workouts are tentatively scheduled. Final decision made at the last minute (possibly hours before, or even after starting). Different from not planning at all — plans establish the realistic steps; you just hold the final decision until you have all the data.
tags: type:philosophy type:heuristic subtopic:responsive-training level:general-population
[SRC: Ch.1] [EVIDENCE: author-opinion]

### PH-6 — Fatigue masks fitness
Starting a workout carrying fatigue → worse performance → limited fitness effect even if you rest afterward. More recovery between hard workouts often lets you train *harder*, not less.
tags: type:philosophy type:physiology subtopic:recovery level:general-population
[SRC: Ch.2] [EVIDENCE: author-opinion]

### PH-7 — Asymptote model of "perfect training"
Training approaches "perfect training" like an asymptote — never reaching it. You can always improve; you're always changing (aging + fitness development), so training must keep adapting. Starting from best practices puts you far along the line quickly.
tags: type:philosophy subtopic:continuous-improvement level:general-population
[SRC: Ch.1] [EVIDENCE: author-opinion]

## 3. Underlying Mechanisms

### MECH-1 — Aerobic vs. anaerobic energy
- Aerobic breakdown of a single glucose molecule yields **20×** as much energy as anaerobic breakdown of the same molecule.
- Aerobic system can metabolize fat (most abundant fuel); anaerobic cannot.
- Anaerobic releases energy *faster*, suited to short high-intensity efforts.
- Muscles always use both; lower intensity → more aerobic reliance; higher intensity → more anaerobic.
tags: type:physiology subtopic:energy-systems system:aerobic system:anaerobic level:general-population
[SRC: Ch.3] [EVIDENCE: derivation]

### MECH-2 — VO2max & performance
- VO2max = maximal oxygen uptake. Accounts for roughly **70%** of variation in race performances among individual runners.
- Aerobic capacity depends on: large powerful heart, high blood volume, high muscle capillary density, high muscle mitochondria concentration, high aerobic enzyme concentration. Training increases all of these.
tags: type:physiology subtopic:vo2max system:vo2max metric:vo2max level:general-population
[SRC: Ch.3] [EVIDENCE: study] — "Research has shown... roughly 70 percent" (no specific citation given)

### MECH-3 — Neuromuscular fitness as co-determinant
Neuromuscular fitness (stride power, stride efficiency, fatigue resistance) affects performance as much as aerobic fitness. Maximal sprint speed and broad-jump distance predict long-distance racing performance as well as VO2max does (among trained distance runners).
tags: type:physiology subtopic:neuromuscular system:neuromuscular level:general-population
[SRC: Ch.3, Ch.4] [EVIDENCE: study] (no specific citation)

### MECH-4 — Aerobic/anaerobic contribution by race distance
| Event | Aerobic contribution | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| 800m (~2 min) | ~50% aerobic / ~50% anaerobic | roughly 50/50 event |
| Every race > 2 min | Predominantly aerobic | for all ability levels |
| 5K | Anaerobic still 20–30% | "lactate tolerance" matters |
| 10K | More aerobic | "lactate avoidance"; VO2 slow component appears |
| Half-marathon | Similar to 10K, slightly more endurance | run at/below lactate threshold |
| Marathon | ~95% aerobic | Top runners ~85% VO2max; mid-pack ~75% VO2max |
tags: type:physiology subtopic:energy-systems system:lactate metric:vo2max level:general-population
[SRC: Ch.3] [EVIDENCE: derivation]

### MECH-5 — VO2 slow component
In efforts ≤15 min, oxygen consumption rises to (near-)max and holds. In longer events, it rises to a submaximal level then continues slowly increasing for the remainder as the runner fatigues. Effect present at 10K and marathon.
tags: type:physiology subtopic:vo2-slow-component system:aerobic level:general-population
[SRC: Ch.3] [EVIDENCE: study]

### MECH-6 — Stride power mechanisms
- Power = rapid application of force to the ground.
- Average person can activate only ~half of a muscle's fibers at maximal contraction; training increases the number activatable simultaneously.
- Motor-unit firing rate: training shortens time between brain command and contraction.
- Power is movement-specific; best improved by running + running-similar exercises (e.g., single-leg jumping drills).
- Aerobic capacity and maximal power are somewhat mutually exclusive (max-power efforts are almost all anaerobic).
tags: type:physiology subtopic:stride-power system:neuromuscular level:general-population
[SRC: Ch.4] [EVIDENCE: derivation]

### MECH-7 — Running economy mechanisms
Three interrelated neuromuscular changes improve economy:
1. Improved stride technique (e.g., reduced overstriding).
2. Stiffening of the stride (tensing muscles just before footstrike → faster energy return, less dissipation; stabilizes knee/hip/pelvis).
3. Reduced wasteful muscular co-contraction.
- ~Half of energy propelling a runner is "free energy" from impact forces (elastic return). Stiffer stride → slightly more than 50%; looser stride → less.
- Types increasing stride stiffness most: short hill sprints, power drills (skipping), short fast intervals.
tags: type:physiology subtopic:running-economy system:neuromuscular level:general-population
[SRC: Ch.4] [EVIDENCE: derivation]

### MECH-8 — Motor-unit cycling & fatigue resistance
- Only 20–30% of motor units active simultaneously during running; they rotate ("take turns").
- Least-preferred motor units contain the most fast-twitch fibers; recruited in two cases: maximal-intensity effort, or when slow-twitch fibers are already tired.
- Training refines cycling → most improvement at emphasized intensities.
- Two training types best expand the recruitable pool: **maximum-intensity efforts (hill sprints)** and **very long runs.**
tags: type:physiology subtopic:motor-unit-cycling system:neuromuscular level:general-population
[SRC: Ch.4] [EVIDENCE: derivation]

### MECH-9 — Fatigue is brain-mediated
Running fatigue caused mainly by self-protective inhibition of motor impulses from the brain, in response to feedback (overheating, energy depletion, loss of acid-base balance, etc.). Running is two-way (feed-forward/feedback) brain-muscle communication.
tags: type:physiology subtopic:central-fatigue system:neuromuscular level:general-population
[SRC: Ch.4] [EVIDENCE: author-opinion]

### MECH-10 — Repeated bout effect
First steep hill sprint session stimulates rapid protective adaptations. Do first session Monday → ready by Thursday, with less soreness the second time. Enables rapid progression of hill sprint training.
tags: type:physiology subtopic:repeated-bout-effect system:musculoskeletal level:general-population
[SRC: Ch.4] [EVIDENCE: study] ("Known to exercise scientists as the 'repeated bout effect'")

### MECH-11 — Detraining rates
- Muscle aerobic enzyme concentration may begin declining within **2 days** of stopping training.
- One study (early 1990s): high-intensity treadmill running test performance decreased **>9%** after **2 weeks** of stopping cold-turkey.
- Fitness is lost significantly faster than it is gained.
tags: type:physiology subtopic:detraining level:general-population
[SRC: Ch.9] [EVIDENCE: study] (early-1990s study, no specific citation)

## 4. Glossary — Author's Definitions

- **Adaptive running** — Hudson's training philosophy: responsive, individualized, evolving. [SRC: Ch.1]
- **Performance threshold** — a runner's maximal average pace sustainable start-to-finish at a given race distance. [SRC: Ch.1]
- **Specific endurance** — the ability to resist fatigue at your goal race pace long enough to reach the finish without slowing; a blend of aerobic + neuromuscular fitness. [SRC: Ch.3, Ch.5]
- **Aerobic fitness** — ability of the body to use oxygen at a high rate during running. [SRC: Ch.3]
- **Neuromuscular fitness** — ability of brain's motor centers to efficiently activate large numbers of muscle fibers; manifests as stride power, efficiency, fatigue resistance. [SRC: Ch.3]
- **Aerobic support** — the specific foundation of aerobic development best for a given race distance; conceptualized as a running speed/intensity your body must be well adapted to so oxygen delivery doesn't limit racing. [SRC: Ch.3]
- **Power efficiency** — amount of stride power generatable at the highest sustainable level of energy expenditure for the full race. [SRC: Ch.4]
- **Muscle training** — training whose primary purpose is neuromuscular adaptations that enhance performance (hill sprints/repetitions, speed intervals, strides/drills). [SRC: Ch.4]
- **Threshold training (Hudson's usage)** — workouts focused on 2.5-hour max pace, 90-min max pace, and 1-hour max pace (multiple thresholds), NOT solely lactate threshold. [SRC: Ch.2, Ch.3]. **[GLOSSARY — differs from common usage]** — conventionally "threshold training" = lactate/anaerobic threshold pace only.
- **Training stimulus** — a workout/sequence that provokes an adaptive response; remains a stimulus only while the body is still adapting. [SRC: Ch.1]
- **Planning in pencil** — treating all planned workouts as tentative; final decision made last-minute. [SRC: Ch.1]
- **Responsive adjustment** — altering planned training based on info from your own body. [SRC: Ch.1]
- **Training cycle** — a period of training culminating in a peak race. [SRC: Introduction]
- **Sharpening period (Hudson's usage)** — final ~4–6 weeks emphasizing challenging race-pace workouts. **[GLOSSARY — differs from common usage]** — traditionally = brief period of short high-speed intervals to "sharpen." [SRC: Ch.2]
- **Key workouts** — the weekly long run + the twice-weekly hard workouts, collectively. [SRC: Ch.2]
- **Hard workout** — a workout involving a moderate-to-large dose of high-intensity running (half-marathon race pace or faster). [SRC: Ch.2]
- **Intensity modulation** — changing target pace level(s) from workout to workout. [SRC: Ch.2]
- **Workload modulation** — changing the overall challenge level of a workout from run to run. [SRC: Ch.2]
- **Multi-pace workout** — workout featuring multiple intensity targets. [SRC: Ch.2]
- **Nonlinear periodization** — all training types mixed throughout the cycle; changes in emphasis less extreme. [SRC: Ch.2]
- **Linear periodization** — each phase very different, strong emphasis on one training type per phase. [SRC: Ch.2]
- **Specific-endurance test ("spec test")** — a race-specific workout designed to assess proximity to goal fitness. [SRC: Ch.5]
- **Aerobic test** — a threshold workout run at a fixed heart rate to measure aerobic progress. [SRC: Ch.3, Ch.5]
- **Performance limiter** — a relative weakness that is the primary factor preventing better performance. [SRC: Ch.9]
- **Running age** — one's current place in the long-term development process as a runner. [SRC: Introduction]
- **Doubling** — running twice a day. [SRC: Ch.3]
- **Junk miles** — wasteful running that adds wear without new adaptation (relevant after age 40). [SRC: Ch.6]

[SRC: Run Faster | 2008 | multiple chapters]

### Q-2 — Three Threshold Pace Levels (Hudson's threshold system)
- **Threshold 1:** fastest pace sustainable for **2.5 hours** (≈ a little slower than marathon pace for elite males).
- **Threshold 2:** fastest pace sustainable for **90 minutes** (≈ a little faster than marathon pace; near half-marathon pace).
- **Threshold 3:** fastest pace sustainable for **1 hour** (≈ half-marathon race pace or a bit faster).
- For **sub-elite runners:** the three thresholds ≈ marathon pace (or slightly faster), half-marathon pace, and a little slower than 10K pace.
- Practical proxy: run threshold workouts at real/estimated marathon pace (minus a few sec/mi), half-marathon pace, and/or 10K pace (plus a few sec/mi). No scientific precision needed.

### Q-4 — Specific-endurance pace window
- Qualifying specific-endurance workout emphasizes efforts within **10% slower to 10% faster** than goal race pace.
- Late fundamental & sharpening: narrow to **3–4% above and below** goal race pace.

### Q-8 — Volume-related numeric rules
- Increase first training week's mileage by only **10–15%** over recent weeks (unless coming off a break).
- Increase running volume ~**10%** per subsequent week until within 10–15% of target.
- Introductory period **should not exceed 6 weeks**; if you can't reach target volume in 6 weeks, target is too high.
- Do **not** increase peak weekly mileage by more than **50%** vs. most recent completed cycle.
- **>70 mi/week** requires doubling (2×/day) ≥1×/week.
- Trying to average >10 mi/run places too much stress (no room for truly easy runs).
- Replace running shoes every **500 miles.**

### Q-11 — Period duration guidelines
- Introductory: as short as **2 weeks** (fit runner, 12-wk 5K plan) up to **6 weeks** (low starting fitness, half/marathon).
- Sharpening: **4 weeks** in almost every plan (2–3 wks peak race-specific work + 1–2 wks taper).
- Fundamental: ≥6 weeks (5K/10K), ≥8 weeks (half/marathon).
tags: type:formula subtopic:periodization phase:base phase:peak phase:taper level:general-population
[SRC: Ch.7]

### Q-12 — Hill sprint gradient/duration progression
- Start: 6–8% gradient, 8-second sprints, 1–2 reps (first session).
- Progress to 8–10 reps of 8-sec → 10-sec sprints on slightly steeper hill → 12-sec sprints on 10% gradient.
- Maintenance ceiling: most get all strength/power gains from **10–12 sprints of 10–12 sec, twice/week**; then cut to 1 set of 6–10 sprints/week.
- Full recovery between sprints (walk back down; take more time if needed).
tags: type:formula subtopic:hill-sprints system:neuromuscular metric:load level:general-population
[SRC: Ch.4, Ch.2]

### Q-13 — Recovery week guidelines
- Reduce mileage **20–30%**; keep same number/sequence/types of workouts (shortened).
- Frequency: every **3rd week** if training near personal limit; every **4th week** if more moderate; low-key/low-volume runners may skip formal recovery weeks.

## 6. Testing & Assessment Protocols

### T-1 — Aerobic Test (threshold/HR-based)
- Format: after a long warm-up, run **4 miles at half-marathon pace at the track**, controlling pace by **heart rate** (keep HR at the level associated with half-marathon racing), not by split times. Between long warm-up and cool-down.
- Interpretation: each repeat, four-mile time should improve at the same heart rate.
- Alternative for half-marathon: run **20–30 min at half-marathon heart rate**, note distance, calculate pace; repeat every 5–6 weeks, moving toward goal pace. Peak fitness = when half-marathon HR and goal half-marathon pace match.
- Requires a heart-rate monitor.
tags: type:test subtopic:aerobic-test metric:hr metric:pace level:intermediate
[SRC: Ch.3, Ch.5]

### T-2 — Spec Test 5K
- Format: **5 × 1K @ 5K goal pace with 2-min recoveries** (vs. 1-min in peak workout).
- Try to sustain goal pace across all five; ease/closeness reveals current 5K specific endurance.
- Frequency: every 5–6 weeks (or run a 5K tune-up race instead).
- No formula for expected splits — "your gut will tell you."
tags: type:test subtopic:spec-test level:intermediate
[SRC: Ch.5]

### T-3 — Spec Test 10K
- Format: **4 × 2K @ 10K goal pace + 1K @ maximal effort, with 3-min jog recoveries** (vs. 1-min in peak workout).
- Max-effort 1K finish shows fuel left after 8K at goal pace.
- Frequency: every 5–6 weeks (or 10K tune-up race).
tags: type:test subtopic:spec-test level:intermediate
[SRC: Ch.5]

### T-4 — Half-Marathon Spec Test (perceived-effort alternative, no HR monitor)
- Go to track, warm up thoroughly. Run exactly **4 miles (16 laps + 40 yards)** at the fastest pace you feel you could sustain for a full half-marathon (perceived effort).
- Don't take splits (may affect pacing). Calculate pace from total time.
- Repeat every 5–6 weeks; should move closer to goal half-marathon pace.
tags: type:test subtopic:spec-test metric:rpe level:intermediate
[SRC: Ch.5]

### T-5 — Marathon: no effective spec test
Performance determined by final 10K after 20 miles of race-pace running; no workout can predict it. Best proxy: look for consistent improvement in specific-endurance workout progression. Do ≥1 long run whose total duration equals marathon goal time (or ≥1 20-mile run if goal is just to finish).
tags: type:test type:caution subtopic:marathon-assessment level:intermediate
[SRC: Ch.5]

### T-6 — Hill sprints as readiness predictor
Poor performance in a set of steep hill sprints (feeling flat/drained/lacking power) is a reasonably reliable predictor of poor performance in a hard workout ~24 hrs later — hill sprints reveal underlying nervous-system fatigue. Standard schedule places them Mon (and sometimes Thu), one day before hard Tue/Fri workouts. **Not equally reliable for all runners** — verify your own pattern first.
tags: type:test type:heuristic subtopic:readiness-monitoring system:neuromuscular level:intermediate
[SRC: Ch.8] [EVIDENCE: benchmark]

### T-7 — Five-jump broad-jump test (referenced)
An Australian study found a five-jump broad-jump test was a very reliable indicator of inability to perform normally in a 3K running time trial for triathletes during especially hard training. Challenges the neuromuscular system similarly to hill sprints.
tags: type:test type:evidence subtopic:readiness-monitoring system:neuromuscular level:intermediate
[SRC: Ch.8] [EVIDENCE: study] (Australian study, no specific citation)

## 7. Methodology & Progression Logic

### METH-1 — Two Rules of Running (universal)
- **Rule 1:** Understand how the human body adapts to different types of training, and train accordingly (do what works, avoid what doesn't).
- **Rule 2:** Learn how *your individual* body adapts, and train accordingly (do what works *for you*, even if different from others).
tags: type:methodology subtopic:training-rules level:general-population
[SRC: Ch.1] [EVIDENCE: author-opinion]

### METH-2 — Four Principles of Adaptive Running
1. Goal of training = stimulate the precise set of physiological adaptations needed for maximum performance in a peak race.
2. Programs must be adapted to individual strengths, weaknesses, needs, goals.
3. Individualized schedules must be adapted daily, based on response to recent training and readiness factors.
4. Runner must adapt training season to season / year to year in response to the last completed cycle.
tags: type:methodology subtopic:adaptive-principles level:general-population
[SRC: Ch.1]

### METH-3 — Three fitness components / three training types
- Aerobic fitness ← aerobic-support training
- Neuromuscular fitness ← muscle training
- Specific endurance ← specific-endurance training

Specific endurance = a race-specific blend of aerobic + neuromuscular fitness. Higher aerobic or neuromuscular fitness raises the ceiling on specific endurance.
tags: type:methodology subtopic:fitness-model system:aerobic system:neuromuscular level:general-population
[SRC: Ch.1, Ch.3]

### METH-4 — General→specific progression (whole cycle)
Build a foundation on the "extremes" (very slow long runs; all-out short sprints), then converge them by adding an aerobic element to neuromuscular training and a neuromuscular element to aerobic training, culminating in true specific-endurance workouts at/near goal pace in the last few weeks. Race-specific fitness only progressively adapts for a few weeks before hitting a peak — so build high non-race-specific fitness first. Adaptive limit reached ~4–6 weeks after starting race-specific work.
tags: type:methodology subtopic:specificity phase:base phase:build phase:peak level:intermediate
[SRC: Ch.2, Ch.3]

### METH-5 — 12 Adaptive Running Methods (general characteristics of every plan)
1. Consistent, moderately high running volume
2. Nonlinear periodization
3. Progression from general to specific training
4. Three-period training cycles (introductory / fundamental / sharpening)
5. Lots of hill running
6. Extreme intensity and workload modulation
7. Multi-pace workouts
8. Nonweekly workout cycles
9. Multiple threshold paces
10. Constant variation
11. One rest day per week
12. Selective cross-training
tags: type:methodology subtopic:core-methods level:general-population
[SRC: Ch.2]

### METH-6 — Volume philosophy: split the difference
Balance quantity (volume) and quality (intensity): do as much faster running as possible without seriously limiting total volume, and as much total volume as possible without seriously limiting high-intensity running. High volume indispensable for max aerobic development but overemphasizing mileage (foregoing intensity) is a mistake. Keep volume fairly consistent year-round (except brief off-season breaks) — reduces injury risk (injuries occur during volume ramp-ups), allows starting cycles higher, avoids bogging down cycles rebuilding.
tags: type:methodology subtopic:running-volume metric:load level:general-population
[SRC: Ch.2]

### METH-7 — Nonlinear periodization rationale
Never let any single fitness aspect fall too far behind (they're deeply interdependent; a weak link limits the others). Keeping all aspects high increases adaptability — you can redirect training quickly (e.g., aggressively boost muscle training to fix a neuromuscular deficit without overtraining risk, because it never went away). Only clear emphasis shift = the sharpening period (zeroing in on race pace).
tags: type:methodology subtopic:nonlinear-periodization level:intermediate
[SRC: Ch.2]

### METH-8 — Peaking: factors that trigger vs. delay a peak
- **Trigger a peak:** pushing fitness strongly in a single direction (heavily emphasizing one training type) → the sharpening period.
- **Delay a peak:** maintaining balance in training. Keep balanced through the fundamental period, then push toward race-specificity only in the final ~4 weeks. Begin sharpening only **4–6 weeks before** the peak race. Prolonging race-specific training beyond a few weeks → premature peak or failure to peak.
- Successful peak requires: (1) be near race fitness from prior training; (2) be sufficiently recovered; (3) workout format very similar to the race.
tags: type:methodology subtopic:peaking phase:peak phase:taper level:advanced
[SRC: Ch.2]

### METH-9 — Extreme workload modulation
- Do **two** hard workouts/week (not counting the weekend long run), not three. Two *really* hard workouts > three *merely* hard workouts — more recovery lets you perform higher.
- Bring back the moderate run (stronger stimulus than easy run) on 1–2 days that aren't hard/easy.
- Run easy days genuinely slow (longer + slower beats shorter + faster; volume is #1 determinant; very slow recovery runs leave you less flat for next hard day). Most common error: running too hard on easy days → vicious circle. Use a HR monitor to stay honest.
tags: type:methodology type:heuristic subtopic:workload-modulation metric:hr level:intermediate
[SRC: Ch.2]

### METH-10 — Multiple threshold paces rationale
Use ≥3 distinct threshold paces, sequenced toward race-specificity. Lactate-threshold pace (between 10K and half-marathon pace for most) is useful but "nothing magical." Purpose: increase how long you sustain near-race pace and how fast you sustain over race distance. Advantage of multi-pace approach = variability, not precision.
tags: type:methodology subtopic:threshold-paces system:lactate metric:pace level:advanced
[SRC: Ch.2]

### METH-11 — Constant variation rationale
- Variation drives brain-muscle learning (activating new fibers, energy-saving timing, relaxing unneeded muscles) → efficiency/power gains.
- Reduces injury risk (spreads stress; assembly-line/carpal-tunnel-rotation analogy).
- Increases mindfulness (avoids autopilot).
- Variables to manipulate: pace, surfaces, gradients, duration, fatigue states, even shoes.
tags: type:methodology subtopic:variation level:general-population
[SRC: Ch.2]

### METH-12 — Muscle-training general progression (all distances)
Increase maximal running-specific strength & stride power → transform power/strength into speed → gradually extend speed into specific endurance. Small maintenance work preserves earlier gains. Tools: short hill sprints (strength/power), hill repetitions (bridge strength→speed), speed intervals (speed), strides & drills (warm-up + power efficiency).
tags: type:methodology subtopic:muscle-training system:neuromuscular level:intermediate
[SRC: Ch.4]

### METH-13 — Speed-interval progression patterns
1. General movement from shorter/faster → longer/slower (but still fast) intervals.
2. Speed training becomes more integrated with specific-endurance training.
3. Overall speed emphasis decreases to minimal in sharpening (esp. longer distances).
Establish a speed baseline (ladder workout) at start of fundamental period; sustain it throughout even as training becomes race-specific; repeat ladder every few weeks to check/boost.
tags: type:methodology subtopic:speed-intervals system:neuromuscular level:advanced
[SRC: Ch.4]

### METH-14 — Specific-endurance progression (4 ways + integration)
Progress toward peak level via: (1) efforts move closer to exact goal pace; (2) more total specific-endurance running per workout; (3) longer race-pace efforts; (4) shorter recoveries (or, in marathon, faster "off" segments). Also: move from multi-pace/integrated workouts → workouts focused squarely on race pace. Need not be totally systematic. Major spec sessions may be spread up to 10 days apart in the fundamental period; do some race-pace running weekly. Peak-level specific-endurance workout performed **15–10 days before peak race.**
tags: type:methodology subtopic:specific-endurance metric:pace level:advanced
[SRC: Ch.5]

### METH-15 — Six keys to year-to-year improvement
1. Consistency (avoid detraining; limit off-season slacking — run ≥2×/week through winter)
2. Avoiding injuries
3. Building on past training (increase volume and/or specificity; arc from generality→specificity over a career)
4. Learning from experience (year-end review)
5. Experimentation (every workout is an experiment; controlled changes between cycles)
6. Setting higher goals (a goal is required to produce a truly maximal effort)
tags: type:methodology subtopic:long-term-development level:general-population
[SRC: Ch.9]

### METH-16 — Event-specific strength/weakness training logic
Two runner types at any distance: more speed than endurance (better at shorter) vs. more endurance than speed (better at longer). Train **from your strength toward your weakness**:
- Speed type: start with short intervals at goal pace, then extend distance at same pace (extend speed over distance).
- Fatigue-resistant type: start with slower longer intervals, then go faster (increase speed over distance).
- Both converge on identical most-race-specific workouts in final weeks (same goal → same endpoint).
- Marathon exception: everyone brings more speed than endurance to it.

## 9. Discrete Protocol Library

### P-1 — Steep Hill Sprints
- Goal: build maximal running-specific strength & stride power; injury prevention; stride stiffness/efficiency.
- Domain: endurance-sport
- Structure: max-effort sprint up a steep hill; walk back down to recover; repeat.
- Intensity/dose: Maximal effort. Duration 8→10→12 sec. Gradient 6–8% (start) → up to 10%.
- When used: throughout the cycle; introduce cautiously (1–2 reps first session, esp. if over 30). Placed after an easy run, typically Monday (± Thursday).
- Progression: add 1–2 sprints/session/week to 8–10; then 10-sec on steeper hill; then 12-sec on 10% gradient. Ceiling 10–12×10–12 sec twice/week → maintenance 6–10×/week once.
- Regressions: fewer reps; shorter duration; gentler gradient.
- Cues: full recovery between reps (cover same distance each rep); truly maximal.
- tags: type:protocol subtopic:hill-sprints system:neuromuscular metric:rpe level:general-population
- [SRC: Ch.4, Ch.2]

### P-2 — Hill Repetitions
- Goal: bridge strength→speed; strength for injury-prone/fast-twitch runners; less impact than track speed work.
- Domain: endurance-sport
- Structure: uphill intervals; jog slowly back down (full recovery). Basic 4-week progression: Wk1 6×200m, Wk2 6×300m, Wk3 6×400m, Wk4 5×600m.
- Intensity/dose: 200–300m @ ~1,500m effort; 400–600m @ ~3K effort. Gradient 4–6% (shallower than hill sprints).
- When used: later introductory / early fundamental period; 4–6 sessions suffice. Do ≥1 before first hard track speed session. Can return later if track intervals break you down or you lack specific strength.
- Cues: time each rep to ensure no slowing in later intervals.
- tags: type:protocol subtopic:hill-repetitions system:neuromuscular metric:rpe level:intermediate
- [SRC: Ch.4]

### P-3 — Uphill Progression
- Goal: increase aerobic + strength-building stimulus without taking too much out of the runner.
- Domain: endurance-sport
- Structure: prolonged uphill running (10+ min) at the end of an otherwise easy run.
- Intensity/dose: moderate to hard effort (by feel).
- tags: type:protocol subtopic:progression-run system:aerobic metric:rpe level:general-population
- [SRC: Ch.2, Ch.3]

### P-4 — Progression Run (6 formats, Table 3.2)
- Goal: moderate aerobic stimulus — stronger than easy run, weaker than most threshold runs; squeeze extra hard work in without overtaxing.
- Domain: endurance-sport
- Intensity by feel ("moderate" or "hard"), not race-pace targets.
- Formats:
  - Moderate Progression: 6 mi easy + 20 min "moderate" — introductory prep / squeeze extra work.
  - Moderate Uphill Progression: 6 mi easy + 20 min moderate (uphill) — build aerobic + strength.
  - Hard Progression: 6 mi easy + 20 min "hard" — late fundamental, coax aerobic support toward peak.
  - Hard Uphill Progression: 6 mi easy + 20 min hard (uphill) — fundamental, after moderate uphill base.
  - Long Run + Progression: 12 mi easy + 30 min moderate — throughout cycle; transform endurance→specific.
  - Fartlek Run + Progression: 2 mi easy / 4 mi (1 min @10K / 1 min easy) / 2 mi easy / 2 mi hard — late fundamental.
- Best use: most useful in introductory period (fitness low, not ready for threshold). Longer-distance runners use more.
- tags: type:protocol subtopic:progression-run system:aerobic metric:rpe level:general-population
- [SRC: Ch.3 Table 3.2]

### P-5 — Threshold Run (4 formats, Table 3.3)
- Goal: aerobic-system adaptations enabling faster paces for longer; support (not be) the toughest race-specific workouts. Finish "hungry for a little more."
- Domain: endurance-sport
- Formats (all book-standard between long warm-up & cool-down):
  - One interval: 2 mi easy / 4 mi @ HM pace / 2 mi easy — early fundamental (shorter) → late fundamental & sharpening (longer).
  - Two intervals: 2 mi easy / 15 min @ HM pace / 2 min easy / 15 min @ 10K pace / 2 mi easy.
  - Three intervals: 2 mi easy / 10 min @ marathon / 1 min easy / 10 min @ HM / 1 min easy / 10 min @ 10K / 2 mi easy — hits all 3 threshold paces.
  - Aerobic Test: 2 mi easy / 4 mi @ HM heart rate / 2 mi easy — measure progress (see T-1).
- Hardest elite threshold workout: 3×15 min (1st @2.5-hr pace, 2nd @90-min pace, 3rd @1-hr pace), 4-min active recoveries + long WU/CD. Average competitive runner: substitute 10–12-min efforts.
- Progression: increase duration OR pace OR proportion at faster paces per session — **not both volume and intensity simultaneously** (raises stress faster than aerobic system can adapt).
- Begin formal threshold runs at start of fundamental period.

### P-6 — Long Run (5 formats, Table 3.4)
- Goal: (job 1) build raw endurance to cover peak-race distance; (job 2) increase speed sustainable over long distances.
- Domain: endurance-sport
- Formats:
  - Easy Long Run: 14 mi easy — introductory long runs for less-fit runners.
  - Moderate Long Run: 14 mi moderate — introductory for fitter runners; "just finish" half/marathon runners.
  - Long Fartlek Run: 4 mi easy / 5×1K @ marathon pace / 1K easy / 4 mi easy — increased intensity, late fundamental & sharpening.
  - Long Progression Run: 10 mi easy + 4 mi hard — increased intensity, late fundamental & sharpening.
  - Marathon-Pace Run: 10 mi easy / 6 mi @ marathon pace — marathon peak sharpening.
- Frequency: 1 designated long run/week, almost every week. First long run 10–15% longer than longest recent run.

### P-8 — Fartlek Intervals

- Intensity/dose: e.g., 8×30 sec @ 10K–3K pace → progress duration (30→40→50 sec) and number.
- When used: introductory period (aerobic base building simultaneously). Best on quiet roads / smooth trails.

### P-9 — Repetition (Speed) Intervals

### P-10 — Ladder Intervals
- Goal: integrate speed with specific endurance.
- Domain: endurance-sport
- Structure: intervals in ascending distance/descending pace (or vice versa). Preferred: 6-5-4-3-2-1 min, 1-min active recovery jog between; first @~10K pace, each faster, final 1-min @~1,500m pace.

- Goal: neuromuscular coordination & efficiency at high speed; warm-up priming.
- Domain: endurance-sport
- Structure: ~100m at ~1,500m race pace; jog back to recover. Done after easy jog portion of warm-up, before any workout at 10K pace or faster.
- Cues: keep stride compact, relaxed, powerful.

### P-12 — Drills (warm-up)
- Goal: dynamic flexibility, stride power/stiffness/efficiency; injury prevention (hip stabilizers).
- Domain: endurance-sport
- Set: do 1 set (each drill + 1 stride once) → progress to 2–3 sets, after the jog portion of warm-up before ≥10K-pace efforts.
  - **Slow Skipping:** drive knee up hard, hop-step, alternate; 30m, leap as high as possible. → stride power & stiffness.
  - **High Knees:** fast tempo, slow forward progress, thighs to 90°, up on toes, spine straight; 30m. → hip dynamic flexibility, ankle stiffness.
  - **Butt Kicks:** slow forward progress, thighs in line with torso, heel to butt each stride; 30m. → hamstrings, knee dynamic flexibility.
  - **Sideways Jumping Jacks:** jumping jack pushing harder with one foot to travel ~1 ft laterally; 30m each direction. → hip abductors/adductors; hip/pelvis/spine stability; efficiency + injury risk reduction.
- tags: type:protocol subtopic:drills system:neuromuscular level:general-population
- [SRC: Ch.4, Ch.11]

- Domain: endurance-sport (cross-training)
- Structure: 5–6 exercises, 2–3×/week. Target: upper/lower back, buttocks, hips, lower abdomen, thighs. Examples: stomach crunches, side step-ups. "A little goes a long way."

### P-14 — Cross-training (alternative cardio)

## 10. Professional Heuristics & Decision Rules

### H-1 — Single-workout adjustment ladder
- Plan A: do the scheduled workout regardless of how you feel; cut it short if you feel lousy and can't perform.
- Plan B (feel bad): do a shortened version of the same session (e.g., 15 min threshold instead of 30) — better than postponing (avoids domino effect).
- Plan C (can't handle even shortened): cancel it (don't postpone).
- Always try to get at least a small stimulus of the day's special kind.
tags: type:heuristic subtopic:single-workout-adjustment level:general-population
[SRC: Ch.8]

### H-2 — When you feel unexpectedly strong
- Generally do NOT convert an easy run into a hard workout; just enjoy it, maybe add a short (~10-min "hard") progression.
- In a hard workout, take advantage by running a little faster (staying true to intended intensity), not by lengthening it. Don't turn a threshold run into a time trial.
- Occasional exception ok when confident (e.g., extend a long run slightly ahead of schedule) — can revert to Plan A mid-workout.
- "Don't get greedy." If the whole plan is unexpectedly easy, make the *next* plan harder rather than spoil the current one.
tags: type:heuristic subtopic:responsive-training level:intermediate
[SRC: Ch.8]

### H-3 — Multi-workout adjustment (fitness off-track)
Diagnose which component lags (see Table 8.1 in Section 14), then over the next 2–3 weeks add more of that training type while possibly slightly reducing the others:
- Neuromuscular lagging → more muscle training (add to Tue threshold workouts; reduce threshold volume; change Sun long-run progressions moderate→hard to preserve aerobic momentum).
- Aerobic lagging → more aerobic-support (add 1–2 mi to Sun long runs; add progressions to Wed easy runs; no need to cut other types).
- Specific endurance lagging → more specific-endurance (replace a threshold with spec intervals; shorten long run + add threshold/race-specific effort; scale back overall aerobic volume to avoid overtraining).
- Reassess after; then continue or revert.
- Not every off-track sign requires action — keep perspective; act on hunches informed by knowledge of yourself. Adjustments are not drastic.
tags: type:heuristic subtopic:multi-workout-adjustment level:advanced
[SRC: Ch.8]

### H-4 — All components lag simultaneously → likely overtraining
Take a few easy/rest days, then resume normal training at a slightly reduced workload vs. original plan.
tags: type:heuristic type:caution subtopic:overtraining level:intermediate
[SRC: Ch.8]

### H-5 — Missing >5 days of training
1. First 2–3 days back: run by feel, cautiously (easy pace, only as long as comfortable).
2. Day 3–4: do a challenging specific-endurance workout to assess fitness lost.
3. Little lost → gradually resume normal training over ~10 days.
4. Substantial loss → scrap original plan; create a new plan and a new, commensurate peak-race goal (or cancel/reschedule peak race).
tags: type:heuristic subtopic:comeback-training level:intermediate
[SRC: Ch.8]

### H-6 — Training through injury
At pain in bone/muscle/joint that affects running: immediately stop running, transfer workouts to cross-training (bike, inline skate, elliptical, anti-gravity treadmill) matching frequency/duration/intensity. Resist resuming until fully healed; do 1–2 extra cross-training workouts for good measure, then a test run. The more readily you switch to cross-training when pain begins, the more closely you follow your plan long-term (and the less total time injured).
tags: type:heuristic type:caution subtopic:injury-management level:general-population
[SRC: Ch.8]

### H-7 — Injury-prone runner adjustments
- Include more hill running than usual.
- Do steep hill sprints twice/week throughout the whole cycle (don't drop to 1×).
- Move some high-intensity workouts (intervals, thresholds) onto inclines (less impact/strain).
- Take a more cautious approach to increasing mileage & high-intensity volume; possibly a longer cycle with slower ramp-up.
- Once at appropriate peak workload, maintain it (high mileage protects; *increasing* mileage causes injuries).
- Keep at least a small amount of high-intensity running at all times (avoid fully phasing it out).
tags: type:heuristic type:caution subtopic:injury-prevention level:general-population
[SRC: Ch.6]

### H-8 — Motivational profile management
- Overeager runners who "beat" prescribed paces: give slightly slower targets, or turn workouts into a pacing game (hit targets exactly). Running too fast in race-pace workouts is undesirable (defeats race-pace-specific adaptation).
- Runners who lose focus during long gaps between races: schedule more frequent tune-up races.
tags: type:heuristic subtopic:motivation level:general-population
[SRC: Ch.6, Ch.8]

### H-9 — Recovery-profile-driven design (4 variables)
Manipulate: (1) number of hard workouts/week, (2) distribution across week, (3) difficulty of hard workouts, (4) amount of running between hard workouts. Standard schedule fixes (1)&(2); focus on (3)&(4). Be conservative with key-workout difficulty in first 4–6 weeks (certain to complete/recover), then raise more aggressively if going well. Younger/higher-talent/higher-fitness recover faster; older recover slower.
tags: type:heuristic subtopic:recovery-profile metric:load level:intermediate
[SRC: Ch.6]

### H-10 — Taper troubleshooting
- Peaked too early (ran better ~1 month before) → concentrate peak-level training closer to race; extend fundamental period.
- Failed to peak (rushed cycle / too little peak-level work) → longer cycle; ensure ≥4 weeks peak-level race-specific work.
- Poor taper → if taper was short/modest, try a longer/sharper one (start 10–14 days out, minimal final week); if it was extreme, try a normal-recovery-week-style taper.
tags: type:heuristic subtopic:taper phase:taper level:intermediate
[SRC: Ch.9]

## 11. Applied Guidance

### AG-1 — Nutrition & fueling (race practice)
- Practice race fueling in longer race-pace workouts. Case (Dathan): first marathon he consumed no nutrition after mile 3 → conclusion he needed more fluid + energy → practiced carbohydrate gels + water in long race-pace workouts and stuck to a strict gel/water schedule in the race → improved from 2:14:01 to 2:11:07.
tags: type:nutrition subtopic:race-fueling level:elite
[SRC: Ch.10] [EVIDENCE: case-study]

### AG-2 — Healthy eating for injury prevention & performance
Balance food types (fruits, vegetables, meats, dairy, seafood, whole grains); minimize processed/sugary/fried foods; get enough calories.
tags: type:nutrition subtopic:diet level:general-population
[SRC: Ch.9] [EVIDENCE: author-opinion]

### AG-3 — Running form guidance
Learn to run flat-footed rather than heel-striking (reduces knee-injury and shin-splint risk). Don't overstride (reaching leg in front, heel-first) — make ground contact flat-footed with foot under the body.
tags: type:heuristic subtopic:running-form level:general-population
[SRC: Ch.9, Ch.4] [EVIDENCE: author-opinion]

### AG-4 — Footwear
Wear the lightest, most comfortable running shoes you can find; replace every 500 miles.
tags: type:heuristic subtopic:footwear level:general-population
[SRC: Ch.9] [EVIDENCE: author-opinion]

### AG-5 — Goal-setting practice
- Best goal-setters achieve race time goals only ~half the time. Always achieving → set too low; never achieving → too ambitious.
- Set short-term goal ≥12 weeks ahead; seldom need >24 weeks focused prep.
- Next year's goal should generally be slightly faster than last year's best for the distance — "improvement requires that you aim for improvement." A goal is required to produce a truly maximal effort (thought experiment: 13.91 mi without goal vs. 14 mi with goal).
tags: type:heuristic subtopic:goal-setting level:general-population
[SRC: Ch.6, Ch.7, Ch.9] [EVIDENCE: author-opinion]

### AG-6 — First adaptive-running year is a learning year
Expect the first year/cycle mainly to gather self-knowledge; you'll make progress but learn even more for the next cycle. Take the first step (try core methods, observe response) before planning further.
tags: type:heuristic subtopic:self-coaching level:general-population
[SRC: Ch.2, Ch.10] [EVIDENCE: author-opinion]

## 12. Recovery, Monitoring & Lifestyle Factors

### R-1 — One rest day per week
Prescribe one rest day/week for every runner (more when needed). "Rest is relative" — for elites, a 45-min very easy run; for typical runners, a day without exercise or just core work. Point: much less training stress than usual so the body absorbs training. Doing a workout ≠ absorbing it. But there is such a thing as too much rest — competitive runners should run 6–7×/week; vigorous exercise ≥6 days/week for leanness/health.
tags: type:recovery subtopic:rest-day level:general-population
[SRC: Ch.2]

### R-2 — Heart-rate monitoring
- Use a HR monitor on easy runs to stay honest (keep HR in the moderately-easy range). Learn your easy HR range.
- Use HR to fix half-marathon pace in the aerobic test (see T-1); half-marathon HR changes little over a cycle while sustainable pace at that HR improves.
tags: type:recovery type:monitoring metric:hr subtopic:hr-monitoring level:general-population
[SRC: Ch.2, Ch.3, Ch.5]

### R-3 — Daily body-scanning
Listen to your body daily; scan for fatigue/soreness before hard workouts. Track workout performance, fatigue level, aches/pains; keep a training log to gauge the proper rate to increase key-workout challenge and to inform year-end review.
tags: type:monitoring subtopic:self-monitoring level:general-population
[SRC: Ch.8, Ch.6]

### R-4 — Off-season / winter maintenance
A week's break is fine; otherwise run ≥2×/week through winter to maintain repetitive-impact adaptations (which take long to regain). Cross-train (cycling, aerobics, snowshoeing, mountain biking) to keep aerobic system sharp + motivational variation; may enhance strength via calisthenics, weightlifting, Pilates. Don't go cold-turkey on running.
tags: type:recovery subtopic:off-season level:general-population
[SRC: Ch.9]

## 13. Adaptations by Population / Context

### POP-1 — Age stages (guidance)
- **15–25 (late teens/early 20s):** greater margin for error; can benefit from less specific/borderline-irrelevant training; strong recovery → don't need very slow easy days (can train ≥moderately hard most runs if active background); but cannot yet handle the toughest workouts (no runner under 26 has that foundation). Hold back in planning hard sessions; take the long view.
- **Mid/late-20s to mid/late-30s:** recovery capacity decreasing → take easy days very easy; but body now far better designed for hard training → can handle harder workouts than as a youngster.
- **40–45+ (third stage):** get max "bang for buck," eliminate waste. Most key workouts very race-specific; little to gain from general training. Few/no junk miles. Best: 3–4 focused sessions/week (muscle, aerobic-support w/ threshold emphasis, specific-endurance) + strength/cardio cross-training other days.
tags: type:heuristic subtopic:age-adaptation level:masters level:beginner
[SRC: Ch.6]

### POP-2 — Masters runners (over 40)
- Slower recovery/adaptation; declining tissue elasticity (possibly accelerated by years of high-volume running — most 50+ age-group WRs held by late starters); declining joint mobility & muscle strength.
- Approach: cross-training-based — run only 3–4×/week; most/all runs are harder workouts; supplement with nonimpact cardio cross-training, mobility training, strength training.
- Speed work caution: muscles/connective tissues less elastic → more strain risk at high speed. Do as much speed work as safely handled/relevant; adjust by moving it onto hills or slowing it (e.g., 3,000m-pace intervals instead of 1,500m-pace).
tags: type:heuristic subtopic:masters system:musculoskeletal level:masters
[SRC: Ch.6, Ch.11]

### POP-3 — Youth runners (under 20)
- New to running/exercise → primary focus = build aerobic + neuromuscular foundation from the most basic level.
- Almost all running at easy pace (facilitates increasing mileage). Little to gain from advanced aerobic work (longer thresholds/faster long runs) yet.
- Neuromuscular start: small number of very short maximal-intensity efforts = short hill sprints (safest/most effective).
- Develop balanced whole-body strength: regular resistance training (esp. core) + other sports (basketball, soccer) → future injury reduction.
- More resilient than masters (faster adaptation/recovery); can increase and eventually handle greater workload.
tags: type:heuristic subtopic:youth system:neuromuscular level:beginner
[SRC: Ch.11]

### POP-4 — Beginners
Train the "extremes": basic aerobic (moderate-intensity runs of various lengths) + basic neuromuscular (very short high-intensity hill/track efforts). Minimal specific-endurance training. Beginners/less-competitive runners preparing for half/marathon may build volume throughout the cycle (exception to the "reach target volume in introductory period" rule).
tags: type:heuristic subtopic:beginner level:beginner
[SRC: Ch.3, Ch.6]

### POP-5 — Distance-specific specific-endurance approach
- **5K:** speed/specific-endurance line is fuzzy; emphasize 5K–1,500m range early → focus goal 5K pace late; peak workout 5×1K @5K goal pace, 1-min recoveries.
- **10K:** peak workout 4×2K @10K goal + 1K max, 1-min recoveries (~10 days pre-race).
- **Half-marathon:** distribute specific-endurance across spec intervals + threshold runs + long runs; threshold runs the primary vehicle; low-mileage runners rely on long runs.
- **Marathon:** specific-endurance occurs primarily within long runs; on/off cycles after long warm-up; supplement with long threshold runs; everyone is a "speed type" for the marathon.
tags: type:heuristic subtopic:specific-endurance metric:pace level:advanced
[SRC: Ch.5]

## 14. Common Mistakes, Contraindications & Cautions

### C-1 — Table 8.1 — Signs your fitness is off track
**Neuromuscular fitness lagging:**
- Feel weak running uphill
- Feel sluggish in speed workouts
- In threshold runs, breathing under control but legs feel heavy
- Fatigue faster than expected during long runs

**Aerobic fitness lagging:**
- Feel fast in speed-interval workouts but fatigue after just a few intervals
- In threshold workouts, legs want to go faster than lungs can handle

**Specific endurance lagging:**
- Trouble sustaining goal race pace in specific-endurance workouts
- Fatigue faster than you should in specific-endurance workouts
- Don't perform as well as expected in a spec test
- Perform poorly in a tune-up race
tags: type:caution subtopic:fitness-diagnosis level:intermediate
[SRC: Ch.8 Table 8.1]

### C-2 — Two failure scenarios from rigid plan-following
1. Ignoring a sore spot → escalates to full overuse injury → unable to train.
2. Pushing hard workouts while unexpectedly fatigued → deeper fatigue → plateau, downward spiral, sickness/injury.
A training plan is a plan, not laws/commandments.
tags: type:caution subtopic:rigid-training level:general-population
[SRC: Ch.8]

### C-3 — Nonsensical conventional practices to avoid
- Marathoners using short intervals (e.g., 400m repeats) to "sharpen" — non-specific. A fast 20-mile run is a better marathon sharpener than 3,000m-pace short intervals.
- Overusing the lactate threshold while underutilizing other threshold paces.
- Substituting the goal of raising VO2max for the goal of specific endurance (too much VO2max work like 3-min intervals) → fail to develop other qualities. Don't train to maximize any single physiological variable — train to maximize performance.
tags: type:caution subtopic:specificity system:vo2max level:intermediate
[SRC: Ch.1, Ch.2, Ch.3, Ch.5]

### C-4 — Most common training error
Running too hard on supposed easy days — impairs recovery → poor next hard workout → vicious circle. "There's no shame in running slow."
tags: type:caution subtopic:easy-run-pacing level:general-population
[SRC: Ch.2]

### C-5 — Hill sprint injury caution
Steep hill sprints place tremendous stress on muscles/connective tissues; beginners risk muscle/tendon strain. Do NOT jump into 10 sprints your first session (especially over age 30) — start with 1–2×8-sec on 6–8% gradient. After adaptation, they protect against injury.
tags: type:caution subtopic:hill-sprints system:musculoskeletal level:general-population
[SRC: Ch.4]

### C-6 — Threshold progression caution
Do not increase both volume and intensity of threshold running from one workout to the next — raises training stress faster than the aerobic system can adapt.
tags: type:caution subtopic:threshold system:threshold level:intermediate
[SRC: Ch.3]

### C-7 — Volume fluctuation caution
Problems that seem to result from excessive volume are often caused by excessive *fluctuations* in mileage. Injuries/overtraining develop during periods of *increasing* volume. Letting mileage fall too low forces larger, riskier ramp-ups later.
tags: type:caution subtopic:running-volume metric:load level:general-population
[SRC: Ch.2, Ch.9]

### C-8 — Ready-made plans still require modification
No plan is perfect; even a plan that looks perfect must be modified workout-to-workout based on body response. The "lack of good workouts/systems" is NOT the primary obstacle — lack of self-knowledge/observation for customization is.
tags: type:caution subtopic:self-coaching level:general-population
[SRC: Ch.7, Introduction]

### C-9 — "Balance" misconception
Lack of balance can come from doing too MUCH of a training type ("done to death") — such stimuli become too familiar to trigger improvement and should be deemphasized, along with workout types you "don't get much out of."
tags: type:caution subtopic:training-balance level:intermediate
[SRC: Ch.6]

## 15. Formulas & Calculations

### F-1 — Power-output % of maximum by race distance
- 100m: ~100% of max sprint speed (elite, ~10 sec)
- 1,500m: ~60% of maximal stride power (average)
- 5,000m: ~60% of maximum running speed sustained for ~13 min (world-class male)
- Marathon: ~40% of maximal stride/sprint speed sustained ~2 hr 10 min (world-class male)
Variables: % = fraction of maximal stride power (or sprint speed) sustained over full race.
tags: type:formula subtopic:power-percentage system:neuromuscular level:advanced
[SRC: Ch.4, Ch.5] [EVIDENCE: derivation]

### F-2 — Maximal-power → race-time improvement (worked example)
If a well-balanced program increases maximal sprint speed 7.5 → 7.8 m/s (a 4% improvement) without cost to aerobic capacity/economy (so % of max power sustainable is unchanged), marathon time improves ~4%: 3:00:00 → **2:52:48**.
- Variables: %Δ max power ≈ %Δ race time (when sustainable % held constant).
tags: type:formula subtopic:power-transfer level:advanced
[SRC: Ch.4] [EVIDENCE: derivation]

### F-3 — Marathon threshold-pace goal example

### F-4 — VO2max share of a race-time gap (worked example)
If you run a 5K one minute faster than me, likely your VO2max is higher by an amount sufficient to account for **42 seconds** of that minute (i.e., ~70% of the 60-sec difference).

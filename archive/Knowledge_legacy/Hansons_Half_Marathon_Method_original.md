## 2. Philosophy & Core Principles

**PHIL-001 — Cumulative fatigue (central organizing principle)**
Cumulative fatigue = a slow, strategic buildup of fatigue (short of overtraining) across days/weeks/months of consistent training, produced by repetitive training that does NOT allow full recovery between training days. It is the underlying foundation of all Hansons plans. Goal: take the runner close to the overtraining line but not over it.
- The five interdependent components: **mileage, intensity, balance, consistency, active recovery.** Removing or drastically changing one disrupts the whole (domino effect) and limits physiological adaptation.
- tags: type:philosophy subtopic:cumulative-fatigue level:general-population sport:running phase:build
- [SRC: Hansons Half-Marathon Method | 2014 | Ch. 1]
- [EVIDENCE: author-opinion]

**PHIL-002 — "Simulate running tired"**
By entering long runs and workouts with residual fatigue in the legs, the runner learns how the late stages of a race feel and builds confidence to nail workouts while tired. Stress is calibrated so the following day can be easy, not requiring a full week to recover.
- tags: type:philosophy subtopic:cumulative-fatigue level:general-population sport:running
- [SRC: Hansons Half-Marathon Method | 2014 | Ch. 1]
- [EVIDENCE: author-opinion]

**PHIL-003 — Build fitness "from the bottom up"**
Beneficial adaptations are best elicited through a large volume of aerobic running at paces slower than anaerobic threshold — pushing aerobic threshold, anaerobic threshold, and aerobic capacity UP from the bottom, rather than pulling them up from the top with high-end anaerobic work. This is a stated key difference from traditional programs.
- tags: type:philosophy subtopic:aerobic-development level:general-population sport:running system:aerobic
- [SRC: Hansons Half-Marathon Method | 2014 | Ch. 1, Ch. 2]
- [EVIDENCE: author-opinion]

**PHIL-004 — Strategic weekly mileage over weekend-loaded plans**
Criticism of typical plans: they load most mileage on Sat/Sun and make weekday runs high-intensity, leaving no room for easy accumulation. Hansons instead spreads mileage across ~6 days/week, adding easy mileage rather than intensity. Reasonable half-marathon volume ≈ 3× race distance/week (35–45 mi); by comparison a 5K runner may run 4–6× race distance.
- tags: type:philosophy subtopic:weekly-volume level:general-population sport:running metric:load
- [SRC: Hansons Half-Marathon Method | 2014 | Ch. 1]
- [EVIDENCE: author-opinion]

**PHIL-005 — Pacing discipline / specificity**
Running faster than prescribed converts each workout into something it wasn't intended to be (easy→tempo, tempo→strength, strength→speed), producing overtraining/burnout/injury and negating targeted adaptations. Proper pacing lets the runner tolerate higher volume and stay consistent. Elite illustration: Kevin & Keith assign push-ups per second run too fast in workouts.
- tags: type:philosophy subtopic:pacing-discipline level:general-population sport:running
- [SRC: Hansons Half-Marathon Method | 2014 | Ch. 1, Ch. 3]
- [EVIDENCE: author-opinion]

**PHIL-006 — Balance across all systems**
All physiological systems must be trained. Two run categories: **easy** and **SOS (something of substance)** = speed, strength, tempo, long runs. Variety maintains motivation and drives steady adaptation. "Nothing is make-or-break." Long run alone won't make the race; being strong + fast + having endurance will.
- tags: type:philosophy subtopic:balance level:general-population sport:running
- [SRC: Hansons Half-Marathon Method | 2014 | Ch. 1, Ch. 3]
- [EVIDENCE: author-opinion]

**PHIL-007 — Consistency > sporadic effort**
Fitness gains can be lost with a couple weeks of inconsistent running; regaining them costs additional weeks. Example given: 3 weeks of 5 days/week improves fitness; 2 weeks at 2–3 days/week erodes it, requiring 2 more weeks to recover — "6–8 weeks went by just to get back to week 3." Rule: modify training when life intervenes, but don't skip it. "Something is always better than nothing."
- tags: type:philosophy subtopic:consistency level:general-population sport:running
- [SRC: Hansons Half-Marathon Method | 2014 | Ch. 1, Ch. 5]
- [EVIDENCE: author-opinion]

**PHIL-008 — Peak on race day, not in training**
Every ~4 weeks mileage increases slightly via easy days, tempo, and long runs; stress is varied and progressed upward. Only at the end is full recovery allowed (taper) so the runner feels best on race day. "You never want to execute your best performance in practice."
- tags: type:philosophy subtopic:periodization level:general-population sport:running phase:peak phase:taper
- [SRC: Hansons Half-Marathon Method | 2014 | Ch. 1]
- [EVIDENCE: author-opinion]

**PHIL-009 — Run to enjoy / longtime competitor, not one-time finisher**
Enjoyment is the greatest predictor of exercise adherence. The method aims to make runners longtime competitors across multiple distances, transferable without a major change in overall philosophy. Straight-talk approach: no sugarcoating, no shortcuts.
- tags: type:philosophy subtopic:adherence level:general-population sport:running
- [SRC: Hansons Half-Marathon Method | 2014 | Introduction]
- [EVIDENCE: author-opinion]

**PHIL-010 — Half-marathon is its own distance (individualized by goal pace)**
Unlike the marathon (where all runners train the same energy systems), half-marathon training depends on runner type and goal. Faster runners (target <1:45) emphasize raising lactate/anaerobic threshold (they hover near it the whole race); slower runners (3-plus hours) emphasize aerobic pathways / fat utilization / glycogen saving; middle runners need a fairly equal blend.
- tags: type:philosophy subtopic:individualization level:general-population sport:running system:threshold
- [SRC: Hansons Half-Marathon Method | 2014 | Ch. 2]
- [EVIDENCE: author-opinion]

## 3. Underlying Mechanisms

**MECH-001 — Skeletal muscle & motor units**
>600 muscles; 3 types (cardiac, smooth, skeletal). Skeletal muscle drives locomotion and stores most energy. Fibers bundled by single type; each bundle controlled by one motor neuron (CNS). Fiber + motor neuron = motor unit. More motor neurons activated → more powerful contraction. Recruitment begins with slow-twitch fibers.
- tags: type:physiology subtopic:muscle-fibers level:general-population sport:running system:neuromuscular
- [SRC: Hansons Half-Marathon Method | 2014 | Ch. 2]
- [EVIDENCE: author-opinion]

**MECH-002 — Fiber-type distributions (TIER B values)**

| Population | Type I (slow-twitch) distribution |
|---|---|
| Most people (arms & legs), regardless of gender | 45–55% |
| Fitness-conscious but not fully devoted | ~60% |
| Trained distance runners | ~70% |
| Elite marathoners | greater than 70% |

- Type I are ~half the diameter of fast-twitch; aerobic; fatigue-resistant; slower shortening speed; high capillary + mitochondrial density.
- Type II (fast-twitch): bigger, faster, powerful, fatigue rapidly, few mitochondria, anaerobic, high ATP use. Subtypes: **Type IIa** (intermediate — more mitochondria/capillaries, considered aerobic, more forceful than slow-twitch) and **Type IIb** (powerful, anaerobic, fatigue quickly).
- tags: type:physiology subtopic:fiber-distribution level:general-population sport:running system:neuromuscular
- [SRC: Hansons Half-Marathon Method | 2014 | Ch. 2, Table 2.1/2.2]
- [EVIDENCE: study] (references inconclusive research on fiber conversion)

**MECH-003 — Fiber-type conversion (trainability)**
Type I ↔ Type II conversion unproven/inconclusive. However, transformation WITHIN Type II is shown: after a relatively short 10–12 week training block, a runner can shift from anaerobic fatigable Type IIb toward aerobic fatigue-resistant Type IIa. Hansons goal: maximize use of Type I and Type IIa, avoid resorting to Type IIb.
- tags: type:physiology subtopic:fiber-conversion level:general-population sport:running system:neuromuscular
- [SRC: Hansons Half-Marathon Method | 2014 | Ch. 2]
- [EVIDENCE: study]

**MECH-004 — Fiber recruitment order**
Slow-twitch relied on almost exclusively until one of: (1) increase pace, (2) encounter hill/resistance, (3) run long enough to exhaust slow-twitch. Some runners recruit fast-twitch after ~1 hour; others ~2 hours. Recruitment sequence: Type I → Type IIa → (undertrained runners forced to) Type IIb ("things will not end well").
- tags: type:physiology subtopic:recruitment level:general-population sport:running system:neuromuscular
- [SRC: Hansons Half-Marathon Method | 2014 | Ch. 2]
- [EVIDENCE: author-opinion]

**MECH-005 — VO2max definition & significance**
VO2max = "volume of oxygen uptake" = max capacity to transport and utilize oxygen while running, expressed ml/kg/min ("milliliters of oxygen per kilogram of body weight per minute"). Higher = better, but NOT the single best predictor of running performance. Elite marathoners tend to have slightly lower VO2max than elite 5K/10K runners. Faster half-marathoners tend to have slightly higher VO2max than slower runners; improves with training.
- tags: type:physiology subtopic:vo2max level:general-population sport:running system:vo2max metric:vo2
- [SRC: Hansons Half-Marathon Method | 2014 | Ch. 2]
- [EVIDENCE: author-opinion]

**MECH-006 — Central (cardiac) adaptations to endurance training**
Four central components of VO2max improvement (Figure 2.1):
1. Coronary artery circulation improves (more blood reaches heart).
2. Ventricle walls thicken (esp. left ventricle) → greater contraction force → more blood pumped.
3. Ventricle chamber enlarges → more oxygenated blood stored/circulated.
4. Pulse decreases (stronger cardiac muscle works less hard).
Net: more blood pumped with greater force and less effort; heart rate slows across all paces.
- tags: type:physiology subtopic:cardiac-adaptation level:general-population sport:running system:aerobic metric:hr
- [SRC: Hansons Half-Marathon Method | 2014 | Ch. 2, Figure 2.1]
- [EVIDENCE: author-opinion]

**MECH-007 — Blood/hematocrit adaptations**
Endurance training increases blood volume and plasma volume (~35–40% plasma increase cited in Ch. 3). Hematocrit (% RBC of total blood volume) decreases because plasma rises — blood is less viscous, travels more easily, less wear on system. Total RBC count can still be higher despite lower percentage (example: 20% of 500 > 20% of 100).
- tags: type:physiology subtopic:blood-volume level:general-population sport:running system:aerobic
- [SRC: Hansons Half-Marathon Method | 2014 | Ch. 2, Ch. 3]
- [EVIDENCE: author-opinion]

**MECH-008 — Peripheral (muscle) adaptations to endurance training**
- Increased capillary density (faster O2 exchange).
- Improved mitochondrial enzyme levels & activity.
- Improved mitochondrial density (more fat used as fuel).
- Increased size of existing mitochondria (more fat processed per site → less carb reliance, higher intensity before anaerobic onset).
- Capillary beds can increase ~40% after a few months; mitochondria can grow ~35% in size and ~5% in number over 6–7 months of training.
- tags: type:physiology subtopic:mitochondria level:general-population sport:running system:fatoxidation
- [SRC: Hansons Half-Marathon Method | 2014 | Ch. 2, Ch. 3]
- [EVIDENCE: study]

**MECH-009 — Anaerobic threshold (AT) mechanism**
AT / lactate threshold / onset of blood lactate = tipping point at which lactate is produced faster than it can be cleared. Occurs at ~60–90%+ of VO2max; best-of-the-best exceed 70% of VO2max. Best single predictor of endurance performance. Training raises VO2max only a few points but can substantially raise AT. "VO2max separates national class from recreational; anaerobic threshold separates champions from contenders."
- Rule of thumb: AT pace can be maintained ~1 hour.
- tags: type:physiology subtopic:lactate-threshold level:general-population sport:running system:threshold system:lactate metric:load
- [SRC: Hansons Half-Marathon Method | 2014 | Ch. 2]
- [EVIDENCE: author-opinion]

**MECH-010 — Lactate is fuel, not the villain**
Lactate/lactic acid serves as an energy source, allowing muscles a bit more work. Fatigue at high intensity is attributed to electrolyte dynamics: potassium ion accumulates outside the cell and cannot exchange with sodium inside → progressively weaker contractions = **neuromuscular fatigue.** The aerobic system processes and removes lactate at moderate pace.
- tags: type:physiology subtopic:lactate level:general-population sport:running system:lactate system:neuromuscular
- [SRC: Hansons Half-Marathon Method | 2014 | Ch. 2]
- [EVIDENCE: study] (stated as "research now tells us")

**MECH-011 — Aerobic threshold & fuel crossover**
Aerobic threshold = pace where fuel mix is ~50/50 fat/carbohydrate. Fat is main fuel up to ~50% VO2max for most people (painfully slow pace). Fat provides ~2× calories/gram vs carbs but oxidizes slower. Carbs (glycogen) dominate at faster paces because they metabolize faster. See Figure 2.2 (fat vs carb contribution by intensity).
- tags: type:physiology subtopic:fuel-crossover level:general-population sport:running system:fatoxidation system:aerobic
- [SRC: Hansons Half-Marathon Method | 2014 | Ch. 2, Figure 2.2]
- [EVIDENCE: author-opinion]

**MECH-012 — Glycogen limits & "the wall"/bonking**
Body stores only ~2 hours' worth of glycogen; ~2,000 total carb calories (liver + muscles + blood). When glycogen depletes, body draws blood glucose (runs out even faster) → "hitting the wall"/"bonking" (pace slows; described as "dragging a 300-lb anchor"). More common in marathon but possible in half for 2–3+ hour runners. Muscle glycogen is local-use only (quads can't borrow from traps); liver glycogen reserved for brain/CNS.
- tags: type:physiology subtopic:glycogen-depletion level:general-population sport:running system:fatoxidation
- [SRC: Hansons Half-Marathon Method | 2014 | Ch. 2, Ch. 8]
- [EVIDENCE: author-opinion]

**MECH-013 — Training the body to burn fat longer**
Fat processing speed doesn't change with training; to use more fat you must burn a higher volume of it → need more/bigger mitochondria. Aerobic (easy) running increases mitochondria, enzyme activity, and O2 → fat oxidized later, sparing muscle glycogen for faster paces → "the wall is pushed back." Figures 2.3 & 2.4 show VO2 and blood-lactate deflection points marking aerobic and anaerobic thresholds.
- tags: type:physiology subtopic:fat-adaptation level:general-population sport:running system:fatoxidation
- [SRC: Hansons Half-Marathon Method | 2014 | Ch. 2, Figures 2.3/2.4]
- [EVIDENCE: author-opinion]

**MECH-014 — Running economy**
Running economy = oxygen required to run a given pace. Example: two runners with VO2max 60; if Runner B uses 50 ml/kg/min and Runner A uses 55 ml/kg/min for a 6:30 mile, B is more economical (and probably faster). Depends on (1) sufficient training volume and (2) speed training at target pace ("train at a pace → become economical at that pace"). Some controversy over its comparative validity; best used to track personal improvement.
- tags: type:physiology subtopic:running-economy level:general-population sport:running metric:pace
- [SRC: Hansons Half-Marathon Method | 2014 | Ch. 2, Figure 2.5]
- [EVIDENCE: author-opinion]

**MECH-015 — Overload principle**
Activity that disrupts homeostasis triggers recovery mechanisms/adaptations that better prepare the body for that stress next time. Cumulative fatigue applies overload without reaching overtraining ("point of no return").
- tags: type:physiology subtopic:overload level:general-population sport:running
- [SRC: Hansons Half-Marathon Method | 2014 | Ch. 3]
- [EVIDENCE: author-opinion]

**MECH-016 — Detraining timeline (regression)**
- 1–2 days missed: no harm, resume normally.
- 3–6 days: minimal physiological regression.
- 7–10 days: body starts losing hard-earned gains ("lose it twice as fast as you gain it").
- 2 weeks off: physiological gains decrease ~3–5%.
- 3 weeks (21 days) off: ~10%+ fitness forfeited — VO2max and blood volume may drop up to 10%, AT decreases significantly, muscle glycogen decreases up to ~30%.
- tags: type:physiology subtopic:detraining level:general-population sport:running
- [SRC: Hansons Half-Marathon Method | 2014 | Ch. 5]
- [EVIDENCE: author-opinion]

**MECH-017 — SOS workout adaptation latency**
An SOS workout takes ~10 days to demonstrate physiological improvement; immediate effect is fatigue. This underpins the 10-day taper (last SOS 10 days before race).
- tags: type:physiology subtopic:adaptation-latency level:general-population sport:running phase:taper
- [SRC: Hansons Half-Marathon Method | 2014 | Ch. 3]
- [EVIDENCE: author-opinion]

## 4. Glossary — Author's Definitions

**GLOS-001 — Cumulative fatigue:** slow buildup (short of overtraining) of fatigue via consistent training that doesn't allow full recovery between days. [Ch. 1]
**GLOS-002 — SOS (Something of Substance):** any run requiring more effort than an easy day — speed, strength, tempo, long runs. [Ch. 1/3]
**GLOS-003 — Easy run:** run of ~20 min–2.5 hr at 55–75% VO2max; in this method paced 1:30–2:30/mi slower than goal race pace. [Ch. 3]
**GLOS-004 — Speed workout:** interval/repeat sessions run at 5K–10K goal pace (~95–98% VO2max target for intervals); 3 mi total at intensity. [Ch. 3]
**GLOS-005 — Strength workout:** longer intervals run 10 sec/mi FASTER than goal half-marathon pace, ~6 mi total; targets lactate tolerance/clearance. [Ch. 3]
**GLOS-006 — Tempo run (Hansons def.):** race-pace run = goal half-marathon pace (differs from other coaches who use shorter, faster tempos). [Ch. 3] *[Flag: differs from common usage]*
**GLOS-007 — Long run:** SOS run at 1:10–2:30/mi slower than race pace; 25–30% of weekly mileage, ≤3 hr; optimal metabolic window 2–3 hr. [Ch. 3]
**GLOS-008 — Aerobic threshold:** pace at which fuel use is ~50/50 fat/carbohydrate. [Ch. 2]
**GLOS-009 — Anaerobic threshold (AT) / lactate threshold / onset of blood lactate:** point where lactate production exceeds clearance; sustainable ~1 hr. [Ch. 2]
**GLOS-010 — VO2max:** maximum capacity to transport and utilize oxygen while running (ml/kg/min). [Ch. 2]
**GLOS-011 — Running economy:** amount of oxygen required to run a certain pace. [Ch. 2]
**GLOS-012 — Active recovery:** partial (incomplete) rest via easy running the day after a workout — no complete days off, to preserve aerobic adaptation. [Ch. 1]
**GLOS-013 — Fractional utilization of maximal capacity:** ability to run at a faster pace for longer time (raised by strength work); increases AT. [Ch. 3]
**GLOS-014 — Overpronation:** excessive inward roll of foot/ankle through footstrike (flat-footed runners). [Ch. 9]
**GLOS-015 — Underpronation / supination:** landing on outer foot and staying there through toe-off (high-arched runners). [Ch. 9]
**GLOS-016 — Toe-off:** point at which toes propel runner forward as foot pushes off ground. [Ch. 9]
**GLOS-017 — Negative split:** running the second half of a race faster than the first. [Ch. 3/10]
**GLOS-018 — Last (shoe):** the actual shape/form of the shoe (straight, curved, semicurved). [Ch. 9]
**GLOS-019 — Neuromuscular fatigue:** weakening contractions from potassium/sodium ion exchange failure at high intensity. [Ch. 2]
**GLOS-020 — Hyponatremia:** imbalance of sodium and water in blood (blood sodium diluted). [Ch. 8]

- [SRC: Hansons Half-Marathon Method | 2014 | Ch. 1–3, 8, 9, 10]
- [EVIDENCE: author-opinion]

1. Easy pace zone (below aerobic threshold; largest, slowest area)
2. Moderate pace zone (fastest of long run / fastest easy for beginners)

5. Strength pace zone = **10–15K pace** (just below anaerobic threshold; high end of "lactate" section)
6. Speed pace zone = **5–10K pace** (just below VO2max)

## 6. Testing & Assessment Protocols

**TEST-001 — Balke field test for VO2max** (TIER B)
Equipment: track, stopwatch, calculator. Equation (per Dr. Joe Vigil):

**VO2max = 0.178 × ([m ÷ 15] − 150) + 33.3**

Steps:
1. Thorough warm-up.
2. Run as far as possible in 15 minutes (pace so last minutes are fastest; don't sprint from the start).
3. Convert distance to meters (laps × 400 m; 1 lap = 400 m standard track).
4. Divide meters by 15 → meters/min.
5. Subtract 150 from meters/min.
6. Multiply remainder by 0.178, add base 33.3.
- Special case: if speed < 150 m/min, take (150 − speed), × 0.178, and SUBTRACT from base 33.3.
- Worked example: 10 laps → 4000 m; 4000÷15 = 266.67; 266.67−150 = 116.67; ×0.178 = 20.77; +33.3 = **54 ml/kg/min.**
- Use: repeat mid-training to track progress; the pace at VO2max is what matters most (more advanced runners see fewer VO2max changes).
- tags: type:test subtopic:vo2max-field-test level:intermediate sport:running metric:vo2 system:vo2max
- [SRC: Hansons Half-Marathon Method | 2014 | Ch. 2]
- [EVIDENCE: derivation] (equation attributed to Joe Vigil)

**TEST-002 — Lab VO2max testing (guidance)**
Treadmill test with breathing tube, incremental speed (~20 min, ~$100) or university exercise-phys lab (often free). ## 7. Methodology & Progression Logic

**METH-001 — Two run categories & weekly structure**
Runs = **Easy** and **SOS** (speed, strength, tempo, long). SOS days scheduled Tue/Thu/Sun. Wednesday = traditional day off (originally clinic day). Easy days sit between SOS days as active recovery. Progression once workouts peak comes from adding easy mileage (e.g., a Wednesday easy run) and/or lengthening easy days — NOT from running harder.
- tags: type:methodology subtopic:weekly-structure level:general-population sport:running
- [SRC: Hansons Half-Marathon Method | 2014 | Ch. 3, Ch. 4]
- [EVIDENCE: author-opinion]

**METH-002 — Phase sequence (macro logic)**
General flow: (1) base/easy mileage → (2) speed block (5K–10K pace intervals; build VO2max & AT) → (3) transition to strength block (HM pace −10 s/mi; longer intervals; maintain gains, half-marathon-specific) → (4) tempo runs progressively lengthen at goal pace → (5) taper. "Speed produces the gains; strength maintains the gains." Speed placed early because it (a) delivers key adaptations, (b) establishes a pacing baseline, (c) serves as dress rehearsal for strength work, and (d) builds mental toughness.
- tags: type:methodology subtopic:phase-sequence level:general-population sport:running phase:base phase:build phase:peak phase:taper
- [SRC: Hansons Half-Marathon Method | 2014 | Ch. 3, Ch. 4]
- [EVIDENCE: author-opinion]

**METH-003 — Speed progression ladder**
Start with shorter-duration repeats and climb, then descend: 12×400 → 8×600 → 6×800 → 5×1000 (1K) → 4×1200 → 3×1600 (ladder workout also used), then work back down (4×1200 → 5×1000 → 6×800 → final week 12×400). Once top of ladder reached, choose workouts fitting optimal development. Keep each repeat within **2–6 min** (optimal); avoid repeats <2 min (insufficient VO2max stress) or >6 min (excess fatigue). Each session totals **3 mi** at intensity.
- tags: type:methodology subtopic:speed-progression level:intermediate sport:running system:vo2max metric:pace
- [SRC: Hansons Half-Marathon Method | 2014 | Ch. 3]
- [EVIDENCE: author-opinion]

**METH-004 — Speed recovery logic**
Recovery = 50–100% of repeat duration; assigned by distance (e.g., 6×800 with 400 jog recovery). Beginners get longer recovery early (e.g., 12×400 first speed session). As fitness improves, shorten recovery (rather than increasing work, since work is tied to plan mileage). Keep jogging during recovery; if too tired to jog, running too hard.
- tags: type:methodology subtopic:interval-recovery level:intermediate sport:running metric:rest-interval
- [SRC: Hansons Half-Marathon Method | 2014 | Ch. 3]
- [EVIDENCE: author-opinion]

**METH-005 — Strength progression ladder**
Begin with 1-mile repeats, progress to longer, then descend: 3×1mi → 4×1.5mi → 3×2mi → 2×3mi (up), then back down. Total ~6 mi at intensity. Recovery kept to a fraction of repeat duration to maintain lactate (e.g., 6×1mi with 1/4-mile jog ≈ 2:30–3:00 = <50% of interval). Note: pace charts specify **800 jog recovery** for 1-mile, 1.5-mile, 2-mile, and 3-mile strength repeats. Run on marked bike path/loop (not track — turning torque/monotony/injury). Add 1.5–3 mi warm-up + cooldown.
- tags: type:methodology subtopic:strength-progression level:intermediate sport:running system:threshold metric:pace
- [SRC: Hansons Half-Marathon Method | 2014 | Ch. 3]
- [EVIDENCE: author-opinion]
- [CONFLICT] Recovery jog for strength repeats stated as "quarter mile" in prose (Ch. 3 example) but pace charts list "800 jog recovery" for all strength distances. Both recorded as printed; CURRENT/most complete = pace-chart value where a specific chart applies.

**METH-006 — Tempo progression**
Tempo = goal HM pace throughout, distance increases every few weeks. Beginner: 3 mi → 6 mi (jumps by intervals; Beginner tops ~6-7 mi at pace). Advanced: 5 mi → 7 mi. With warm-up/cooldown, advanced tempo days can total 12–14 mi / ~90 min. Tempo is the most telling workout for goal validation.
- tags: type:methodology subtopic:tempo-progression level:beginner level:advanced sport:running metric:pace
- [SRC: Hansons Half-Marathon Method | 2014 | Ch. 3, Ch. 4]
- [EVIDENCE: author-opinion]

**METH-007 — Long run every-other-week logic (Beginner)**
Start with 10-mi Sunday long run, increase ≤2 mi week to week, keeping long run ~25% of weekly mileage. Alternate a true long run one week with a standard higher-mileage run the next to accommodate lengthening tempo runs (avoiding effectively three long runs per 8 days). Beginner peaks at 12-mi long run; Advanced peaks at 14-mi.
- tags: type:methodology subtopic:long-run-frequency level:beginner sport:running phase:build
- [SRC: Hansons Half-Marathon Method | 2014 | Ch. 3, Ch. 4]
- [EVIDENCE: author-opinion]

**METH-008 — Long run sizing philosophy (vs arbitrary 20-miler)**
Rather than a fixed 20-mile run, size by % of weekly mileage AND total time. Cited authorities: Jack Daniels (never exceed 25–30% of weekly mileage; 2:30–3:00 hr limit); Dave Martin (90 min–2 hr); Tim Noakes (2+ hr at 70–85% VO2max → greatest glycogen depletion); David Costill (2-hr run reduces muscle glycogen ~50%; takes up to 72 hr to replenish — acceptable on race day, counterproductive mid-cycle).
- tags: type:methodology subtopic:long-run-sizing level:general-population sport:running phase:build
- [SRC: Hansons Half-Marathon Method | 2014 | Ch. 3]
- [EVIDENCE: study] (cited researchers Daniels, Martin, Noakes, Costill)

**METH-009 — Progression cadence**
Every ~4 weeks, mileage increases slightly via easy days, tempo, and long runs. Once workouts peak, added weekly mileage comes from easy days only.
- tags: type:methodology subtopic:progression-cadence level:general-population sport:running phase:build
- [SRC: Hansons Half-Marathon Method | 2014 | Ch. 1, Ch. 3]
- [EVIDENCE: author-opinion]

**METH-010 — Crosstraining as replacement (specificity rule)**
"To get better at running, you need to run" (rule of specificity). Crosstraining should be supplemental, not a running replacement, except during injury/rehab, planned downtime, or true-beginner easing-in. To add running mileage without injury: phase out crosstraining gradually (replace one session with a run each week); add mileage in small increments; run appropriate (slower) paces.
- tags: type:methodology subtopic:crosstraining level:general-population sport:running
- [SRC: Hansons Half-Marathon Method | 2014 | Ch. 3, Ch. 7]
- [EVIDENCE: author-opinion]

- Dosing: hold 20 s, 1–3 reps each; ~10–15 min post-run (or later in day — never pre-run); never to pain/shaking; slow/controlled
- Frequency: regular/daily (sometimes twice/day); ≥3 weeks before improvements seen
- Stretches: (1) low back; (2) shoulder; (3) chest; (4) calves; (5) gluteal; (6) groin; (7) hamstrings; (8) hip flexors/quads; (9) hips
- Caution: static stretching BEFORE running/racing reduces muscle force & risks tears — worst time
- tags: type:protocol subtopic:static-stretching sport:running caution level:general-population
- [SRC: Hansons Half-Marathon Method | 2014 | Ch. 7]

### P-020 — Core/strength routine (5 exercises)
- Goal: form, injury prevention, fight fatigue via fast-twitch recruitment late in race
- Dosing: choose 2–3 exercises after easy running days; start 1–2 days/wk, 1–2 sets; progress slowly
- Exercises:
    - Crunch — start 3×10, progress to 3×25, then add sets/reps
    - Back extension — hold 2–3 s, 12–15 reps
    - Superman (on exercise ball, opposite arm/leg) — hold 1–2 s, 12–15 reps/side
    - Bridge — hold 1–3 s, 12–15 reps (progress to one-legged)
    - Side plank — hold 10–20 s, increase with strength
- Principle: complements (never replaces) running; corrects weaknesses/imbalances; short & simple; no gym needed
- Reassurance: hard to add significant bulk with correct exercises/volumes
- tags: type:protocol subtopic:strength-training sport:running system:neuromuscular level:general-population
- [SRC: Hansons Half-Marathon Method | 2014 | Ch. 7]

## 10. Professional Heuristics & Decision Rules

**HEUR-001 — Day-switching rules**
If a scheduled workout day conflicts: shift the WHOLE schedule consistently (keep the pattern), don't swap different days each week. Common move: tempo → Friday (reverse Thu/Fri) for extra recovery before Tue speed/strength. Rule: always ≥1 rest or easy day between SOS days; never back-to-back SOS.
- tags: type:heuristic subtopic:scheduling level:general-population sport:running
- [SRC: Hansons Half-Marathon Method | 2014 | Ch. 4, Ch. 5]
- [EVIDENCE: author-opinion]

**HEUR-002 — Adding weekly mileage (priority order)**
1. Run easy on the Wednesday rest day (adds 4–8 mi ≈ +10%).
2. Lengthen weekday easy runs (e.g., 4–6 → 6–8 mi ≈ +8 mi; tops ~60 mi/wk).
3. Increase long run LAST (keep 25–30% of weekly mileage, <3 hr). Never just add to the long run first.
- Higher targets: easy days ≥10 mi each → 70–80 mi/wk; add second daily run only after >10 mi/day (then 14–15 mi/day). Two-a-days worthwhile mainly above ~10 mi/day. 95–100 mi/wk realistic only for sub-1:15 aspirants.
- tags: type:heuristic subtopic:add-mileage level:advanced sport:running metric:load
- [SRC: Hansons Half-Marathon Method | 2014 | Ch. 4, Ch. 5]
- [EVIDENCE: author-opinion]

**HEUR-003 — "Something is always better than nothing"**
If short on time, do a partial workout (e.g., 6 mi of a 10-mi tempo) or even a 25-min run rather than nothing. If a missed SOS truly can't be rescheduled within the ≥1-easy-day-between-SOS rule, cut losses and move to the next SOS.
- tags: type:heuristic subtopic:time-constraints level:general-population sport:running
- [SRC: Hansons Half-Marathon Method | 2014 | Ch. 4, Ch. 5]
- [EVIDENCE: author-opinion]

**HEUR-004 — Tired vs injured decision**
Feeling worn-out/achy is normal (cumulative fatigue) — don't take unscheduled days off; first check that easy days are truly easy and SOS paces aren't being cheated down. If injured, consult coach/physician, identify root cause (e.g., shin splints → new shoes/strength routine), reduce volume/intensity but keep running short/easy through healing if the body allows.
- tags: type:heuristic subtopic:tired-vs-injured level:general-population sport:running caution
- [SRC: Hansons Half-Marathon Method | 2014 | Ch. 4, Ch. 5]
- [EVIDENCE: author-opinion]

**HEUR-005 — Missed-training recovery matrix**

| Days missed | Action |
|---|---|
| 1–2 | Resume normally; do Tue SOS on Wed, move Thu tempo to Fri; or forge ahead and drop the missed SOS |
| 3–4 | Easy 2–3 days, then resume schedule |
| 5–6 | Easy 3–4 days, then repeat previous week, then jump ahead to catch up |
| 7–10 (1 wk) | Run easy same # of days missed; repeat last completed week, run the missed week, then resume (≈3 weeks to get back; subtract a week if you ran easy during time off). If after strength block begins, likely adjust race goals |
| >10 days / 2 wk | Adjust goal +3–5%; 3 wk off → +7–10%; >4 wk off → choose a new race |

- Goal-adjustment example: 2:00 goal, 2 wk missed → +3.6 min (120×0.03) to +6 min (120×0.05) → new goal 2:03–2:06.
- If crosstraining possible during layoff, return is faster. Always consult a runner-familiar physician.
- tags: type:heuristic subtopic:missed-training level:general-population sport:running caution
- [SRC: Hansons Half-Marathon Method | 2014 | Ch. 5]
- [EVIDENCE: author-opinion]

**HEUR-006 — Racing during a training block**
Race sparingly. Beginners: race 1 short event (5K/10K) to set baseline (best at end of Week 7). Suggested: 5K during speed cycle, 10K during strength cycle (builds race distance; roughly matches Thursday tempo distance so no SOS lost). Adjusting a race week (Table 5.1): replace Thu tempo with Sat easy; Sat = race (replaces tempo, both stress AT); Sun = longer easy run; resume Monday. If race Sat → easy Sun; if race Sun → easy Mon, assess Tue.
- tags: type:heuristic subtopic:racing-during-training level:general-population sport:running phase:race
- [SRC: Hansons Half-Marathon Method | 2014 | Ch. 4, Ch. 5]
- [EVIDENCE: author-opinion]

**HEUR-007 — Goal adjustment mid-training**
If struggling to hold goal pace on 6-mi tempo → the pace won't hold for 13.1 mi; revise to slightly slower goal for confidence. Conversely, don't ramp up goals aggressively when workouts go well — risk of overtraining/injury. "You'll run a better race slightly undertrained than overtrained." Choose goal BEFORE the strength segment.
- tags: type:heuristic subtopic:goal-adjustment level:general-population sport:running
- [SRC: Hansons Half-Marathon Method | 2014 | Ch. 6]
- [EVIDENCE: author-opinion]

**HEUR-008 — Weather pace adjustments (race)**
Slow ~5–8 s/mi when temps reach 60s+. Approx: 60°F → +5 s/mi; 70°F → +10 s/mi; 80°F → +15 s/mi. Heat-adapted, smaller, and highly trained runners affected less. [Also for goal-setting: adjust for terrain/heat/humidity/race size/corral position.]
- tags: type:heuristic subtopic:weather-adjustment level:general-population sport:running phase:race metric:pace
- [SRC: Hansons Half-Marathon Method | 2014 | Ch. 6, Ch. 10]
- [EVIDENCE: author-opinion]

**HEUR-009 — Midrace pacing correction**
If crowds forced you slow (e.g., 9:20 vs 9:00 goal) early, DON'T overcorrect to 8:45 — settle back to goal pace and gradually pick up over several miles. If adrenaline made you too fast, slow to goal pace, don't panic, find rhythm. Focus on your own race; assume you'll catch fast starters later.
- tags: type:heuristic subtopic:midrace-pacing level:general-population sport:running phase:race
- [SRC: Hansons Half-Marathon Method | 2014 | Ch. 10]
- [EVIDENCE: author-opinion]

**HEUR-010 — Negative/even split strategy**
Run first half conservative; second half slightly faster (negative split). Nearly every world record and most PRs set via negative or even splits; "fly and die" (positive split) fails. Going out slow rarely damages overall pace; going out fast does.
- tags: type:heuristic subtopic:split-strategy level:general-population sport:running phase:race
- [SRC: Hansons Half-Marathon Method | 2014 | Ch. 3, Ch. 10]
- [EVIDENCE: benchmark]

**HEUR-011 — Shoe wear-pattern read**
Medial (inside) tread grind → overpronating → need more support. Outer edge worn → supinating → need less support, more cushioning. Even wear → likely correct category. Not exact science — if 10 versions of a model kept you injury-free, stick with it regardless of wear pattern.
- tags: type:heuristic subtopic:shoe-selection level:general-population sport:running
- [SRC: Hansons Half-Marathon Method | 2014 | Ch. 9]
- [EVIDENCE: author-opinion]

**HEUR-012 — Race-day shoe/flat decision**
More competitive → more reason to consider flats. Use flats only if you already do SOS in lightweight/minimalist/flats. Trade-off: lighter weight vs cushioning/stability; fatigue harms mechanics late, so cushioning may prevent injury. Lightweight trainers = good transitional option. Race in shoes with 50–100 mi (2–3 weeks) — never brand-new on race day.
- tags: type:heuristic subtopic:race-shoes level:general-population sport:running phase:race
- [SRC: Hansons Half-Marathon Method | 2014 | Ch. 9]
- [EVIDENCE: author-opinion]

**HEUR-013 — Consistency > sporadic supplemental work**
Stretching/strength done only every few weeks yields no benefit and adds fatigue. Be consistent or don't bother. Start early in (or before) a training block, not mid-block.
- tags: type:heuristic subtopic:supplemental-consistency level:general-population sport:running
- [SRC: Hansons Half-Marathon Method | 2014 | Ch. 7]
- [EVIDENCE: author-opinion]

## 11. Applied Guidance

### Nutrition

**APP-001 — Three nutrition aspects**
Consume (1) enough calories, (2) the right calories, (3) at the right times.
- tags: type:nutrition subtopic:principles level:general-population sport:running
- [SRC: Hansons Half-Marathon Method | 2014 | Ch. 8]

**APP-002 — Caloric-need formulas (TIER B)**
1. Light–moderately active (45–60 min moderate activity most days): **body weight (lb) × 16–20 cal/lb = daily calories**
2. Very active (60–120 min moderate exercise most days): **body weight (lb) × 21–25 cal/lb = daily calories**
- Worked example: 150 lb × 16 = 2,400; ×20 = 3,000 → 2,400–3,000 cal/day. Use higher-calorie formula on long-run days.
- Sex adjustment: males → middle-to-high end; females → middle-to-low end.
- Age adjustment (population that becomes sedentary): men −10 cal per year past 20; women −7 cal per year. Minor if activity stays high.
- Caution: avoid large deficits (≥1,000-cal reductions / skipped meals) — overtraining/illness/injury (often by ~6 weeks). Slight deficits OK for weight loss; seek pro guidance.
- tags: type:formula subtopic:caloric-needs level:general-population sport:running nutrition
- [SRC: Hansons Half-Marathon Method | 2014 | Ch. 8]

**APP-004 — Carbohydrate roles**
Provide energy (esp. high intensity/anaerobic once VO2max reached); aid water absorption (help stomach empty faster); primary fuel for brain/CNS ("foggy feeling" = glycogen depletion); "fat burns in the flame of carbohydrates" (~20% of fat-metabolism process depends on carb presence). Body stores only ~2,000 carb calories (~1,500 in muscles, local-use only).
- tags: type:nutrition subtopic:carbohydrates level:general-population sport:running system:fatoxidation
- [SRC: Hansons Half-Marathon Method | 2014 | Ch. 8]
- [EVIDENCE: author-opinion]

**APP-005 — Fat & protein roles**
Fat: energy (spares carbs), better fuel as fitness improves, vitamin A/D/E/K absorption, cell/spinal tissue structure. Protein: repairs muscle, energy last-resort (from running muscles), makes enzymes/antibodies/hormones, transports vitamins/minerals/fat, regulates fluid balance (prevents cramping/electrolyte issues). Consume protein WITH carbs post-exercise to minimize muscle damage & speed recovery.

**APP-007 — During-workout fueling & hydration (TIER B)**
Practice on runs >1 hr. Guidance for those running 2+ hr especially:
- Start early: drink within first 10–20 min / first water stop.
- Drink **2–8 oz every 15–20 min** (1 gulp ≈ 1 oz; aim 4–6 gulps). Don't overdo.
- Carbohydrate: **30–60 g per hour** of exercise (60 g/hr if >4 hr). 8-oz sports drink ≈ 50–80 cal; a gel ≈ 25 g carb.
- Total calories: **200–300 per hour** (8 oz sports drink every 20 min ≈ 195 cal/hr — enough for most running <2 hr).
- If using gels, chase with water (not sports drink); one gel every 30–45 min.
- Physiological cost of dehydration: each 1% body-weight loss → HR up to +7 bpm and ~2% slower pace (at 8:00/mi, 2% ≈ 5 s/mi; 2–4% loss → ~8:20/mi ≈ 1:44 vs 1:49 finish).
- tags: type:nutrition subtopic:during-workout level:general-population sport:running
- [SRC: Hansons Half-Marathon Method | 2014 | Ch. 8]
- [EVIDENCE: study] (thirst-based drinking: Butler et al., Clin. J. Sports Med., 2006 — 68–82% fluid recovery; ~2.3% deficit before thirst)

**APP-008 — Postworkout fueling (TIER B)**
- Most important window: first **15–30 min**.
- Rehydrate: **2.5 cups water per pound of body weight lost.**
- Carbohydrate: **50–100 g** immediately, high-glycemic (bananas, sports drinks, oatmeal, cooked carrots, beans, orange juice, corn flakes, baked potato, bread, ice cream).
- Within next 2 hr: full meal with carbs + protein (oatmeal, PB + bagel, cereal; chocolate milk = good protein-rich option).
- Plan ahead: bring food to group workouts; start refueling ASAP.
- tags: type:nutrition subtopic:postworkout level:general-population sport:running recovery
- [SRC: Hansons Half-Marathon Method | 2014 | Ch. 8]
- [EVIDENCE: author-opinion]

**APP-009 — Race-week / day-before / morning fueling (TIER B)**
Final week: maintain normal diet (no big changes); 70% carbs (complex/starches); full glycogen restoration can increase endurance up to ~20%; weight gain (up to ~5 lb) normal & desirable (each gram stored carb holds ~3 g water); hydrate all week.
Day before: healthy beverage each snack/meal (mix in sports drinks); avoid gas/GI-disrupting foods, high-fiber, sugar substitutes; limit alcohol; healthy bedtime snack (unbuttered popcorn, bagel+PB, sports bar).
Race morning: top off stores; carbohydrate by timing —
- 1 hr prior: 50 g total
- 2 hr prior: 100 g total
- 3 hr prior: 150 g total
- 4 hr prior: 200 g total
Author's typical: bagel+PB, banana, coffee, maybe OJ ~3 hr before. Most runners in 2–3 hr window. Balance topping off vs bathroom lines. Overnight the liver burns ~half its glycogen → a midnight snack can cut morning need to ~100–200 cal (vs 300–500).

- [SRC: Hansons Half-Marathon Method | 2014 | Ch. 8, Ch. 10]

**APP-010 — Race caloric-expenditure & fueling calculation (TIER B)**
- Rule: **1 cal per kg body weight per km run (1 cal/kg/km).** Caloric expenditure tied to DISTANCE, not pace (a faster & slower runner of same weight burn ~same total).
- Convert weight: lb ÷ 2.2 = kg. Half-marathon = 21.0975 km.
- Example (150 lb): 150÷2.2 = 68.18 kg; 21.0975 × 68.18 = **1,438 cal** total.
- Carb:fat ratio by intensity (Table 8.3): HM pace ≈ 60% VO2max (beginners), 75% (advanced), 85% (elite). Per-mile: 1,438÷13.1 ≈ 110 cal/mi. Carb/mi = 110 × intensity fraction (60% → 66; 75% → 77; 85% → 93.5). Race carb total = 66–93.5 × 13.1 ≈ **864.6–1,224.85 cal** (~350 cal spread ≈ three 100-cal gels). → running a few s/mi too fast can create an unrecoverable carb deficit.
- Potential leg-glycogen storage: trained athlete stores ~80 cal/kg in muscle (local use). Leg mass ≈ 21% total mass (males), 20% (females). Storage = weight(kg) × 0.20–0.21 × 80 cal/kg. Example: 68.18 × 0.21 = 14.32 kg; ×80 = **1,146 cal** potential storage. (Tables 8.4/8.5 give values by weight/sex.)
- Interpretation: 68-kg example stores ~1,146 cal, expends 864–1,224; at 75–85% VO2max needs extra calories during race. Replacement gets you to finish, can't keep tank full — long-term aim is fat-burning efficiency. Err toward consuming calories if stomach tolerates.

### Hydration

**APP-011 — Dehydration effects & fluid-loss factors**
Even 2% hydration loss (~3 lb sweat for a 150-lb runner) impairs performance; 3% loss → 4–8% aerobic-capacity decrease. Sweat rates 1–2 lb/hr (cool/dry), 2–4 lb/hr average during exercise. Effects: ↑HR, ↓stroke volume, ↓cardiac output, impaired heat dissipation (heat illness risk), GI distress, electrolyte imbalance/cramps, accelerated glycogen burn, cognitive impairment.
Factors ↑ fluid loss: higher temperature; higher humidity (blocks evaporative cooling; worsened by nonwicking fabric); larger body surface area; better-conditioned athletes actually sweat more (but cool more efficiently); starting already dehydrated.
Absorption factors: carbs aid water absorption (short chains = dextrose fast; long chains = maltodextrin sustained); larger fluid volumes absorb faster but impractical mid-race; cooler fluids leave stomach faster during exercise; faster running diverts blood from gut → harder absorption; can't "catch up" once behind.
- tags: type:nutrition subtopic:hydration level:general-population sport:running
- [SRC: Hansons Half-Marathon Method | 2014 | Ch. 8]
- [EVIDENCE: study]

**APP-012 — Hyponatremia guidance**
Cause: heavy sweat loss + large plain-water intake → diluted blood sodium. Types: euvolemic (water up, sodium same), hypervolemic (both up, water far more), hypovolemic (both down, sodium faster). Serious (coma/death possible). Guidelines: if session >1 hr, use a sports drink; know your sweat rate and replace to that point (most replace 65–80% of losses; some over-drink); in recovery focus on electrolyte-containing fluids (low-carb options fine). Moderation is key.
- tags: type:caution subtopic:hyponatremia level:general-population sport:running nutrition
- [SRC: Hansons Half-Marathon Method | 2014 | Ch. 8]
- [EVIDENCE: study]

### Gear

**APP-013 — Foot strike & stride biomechanics**
Foot strike goal: brief ground contact (minimize braking) but not so brief that push-off force is lost. Hasegawa et al. (2007, J. Strength Cond. Res. 21[3]:888–893) — elite half-marathoners at 15 km: ~75% heel strike, 24% midfoot, 1% forefoot; but 60% of first 50 finishers were midfoot strikers. Author's view: WHERE the foot lands relative to center of gravity matters more than which part lands. Avoid overstriding (foot far ahead, heel-first, toes up → braking, more shock, longer contact, slower). Cues: lift with quads, pull lower leg under quad, land under center of gravity/midfoot; "run tall" (shoulders back, slight lean at waist, torso as one lever).
- tags: type:physiology subtopic:biomechanics level:general-population sport:running
- [SRC: Hansons Half-Marathon Method | 2014 | Ch. 9]
- [EVIDENCE: study]

**APP-014 — Foot types → issues**
- Flat arch (overpronator): most troublesome; too-flexible foot, rolls inward; rotational forces → tendinitis, plantar fasciitis, Achilles tendinitis.
- High arch (supinator/underpronator): inflexible, poor shock absorption; injuries + iliotibial band issues.
- Medium/neutral arch: least common; best mechanics but still needs correctly-matched shoe.
- tags: type:physiology subtopic:foot-types level:general-population sport:running caution
- [SRC: Hansons Half-Marathon Method | 2014 | Ch. 9]
- [EVIDENCE: author-opinion]

**APP-015 — Shoe construction & types**
Components: outsole (tread/traction), midsole (cushioning + support; where money goes; life ~300–500 mi; dual/tri-density adds support+weight), last (straight=overpronators, curved=supinators, semicurved=neutral), heel counter (ankle-motion control), upper (breathable mesh, wicking).
Shoe types: motion control (flattest feet, straight last, dual-density, roll bar, stiff heel counter); neutral (high arches, curved last, cushioning, minimal heel counter); stability (neutral/mild overpronators, semicurved, some dual-density, mild heel counter); lightweight (between trainer & flat; not for exclusive training; good for speed/strength SOS; racing flats life ~100–150 mi); minimalist (little cushioning; requires months-long gradual transition — injury ~2 weeks if rushed per Lieberman et al., Nature 463 [2010]:531–535).
Pricing tiers: entry-level (basics, heel-only cushioning), midgrade (most popular; full-length cushioning), high-end (premier/latest tech).

- [SRC: Hansons Half-Marathon Method | 2014 | Ch. 9]

**APP-016 — Shoe fitting & replacement**
Go to a running specialty store; bring old shoes; expect gait/wear analysis, questions (injuries, past shoes). Fit: snug heel (no slip), thumb-width toe box room, shop late-day (feet swell). Choose by function/comfort, not fashion. Replace at 300–500 mi (18-wk Beginner Program ≈ 640 mi → needs ≥2 pairs). Confirm model unchanged when rebuying. Race shoes: 50–100 mi of use (2–3 weeks) before race day.

**APP-017 — Apparel guidelines**
Avoid cotton (traps heat/sweat → chafing/blisters). Dress as if 20°F warmer than actual (40° → dress for 60°); layer + shed. Consider cost-per-wear (durable gear lasts seasons). Try on before race day; run in outfit first (esp. long run) to check chafing/fit. Compression socks/tights: claims (↑venous return, ↑lactate clearance, ↓DOMS) unproven; one view warns post-run compression risks DVT — jury out.

**REC-003 — Sleep & lifestyle for elites (context)**

**REC-004 — Post-race recovery timeline**
- Immediately: revel/be proud; expect soreness/exhaustion (muscle broken down microscopically).
- First 30 min: eat any calories available (glycogen depleted, blood glucose low, dehydrated, low electrolytes) — small optimal window.
- First 2 hr: find family, get cleaned up/comfortable shoes; high-carb lunch when stomach settles; steady calories; nutritious foods, water/juice/sports drinks.
- Rest of day: hydrate, replace calories, feet up; soreness expected (first HM = hardest recovery).
- Next 5–7 days: NO running; no new races on calendar; planned rest now avoids unplanned rest later; light non-weight-bearing aerobic OK if needed.
- After 1 week: resume easy running (see PLAN-005); fitness minimally lost; resistance training 2–3×/wk on non-future-SOS days.

- [SRC: Hansons Half-Marathon Method | 2014 | Ch. 11]

**REC-005 — Planned break after training segments**
Hansons-Brooks athletes take a planned break after every training segment (restores muscle, rejuvenates spirit, plans next move). Individual: comfort-zone newbie may need ~10 days off after a half; veteran ~5 days. (Full-marathon prescription is ~2 weeks off; half needs less.)

**ADAPT-003 — Half-marathon training by goal-pace physiology** (see PHIL-010)
Faster (<1:45): raise anaerobic threshold. Slower (3+ hr): aerobic pathways, fat utilization, glycogen saving; train like a marathoner (mostly slow-twitch, high fat use). Middle: equal blend. Faster runners' AT more important than fat-burning; slower runners' fat-burning more important (but AT ≈ their 10K performance).
- tags: type:methodology subtopic:goal-pace-adaptation level:general-population sport:running system:threshold
- [SRC: Hansons Half-Marathon Method | 2014 | Ch. 2]
- [EVIDENCE: author-opinion]

**ADAPT-004 — True beginners / crosstraining ease-in**
Absolute beginners may start ~15 min, 2–3 days/wk, filling other days with elliptical/bike/walk; gradually replace crosstraining with running over several months to reach 5–6 running days/wk.
- tags: type:methodology subtopic:beginner-easein level:beginner sport:running
- [SRC: Hansons Half-Marathon Method | 2014 | Ch. 7]
- [EVIDENCE: author-opinion]

**ADAPT-005 — Elite/postcollegiate context**
Postcollegiate runners often need years to adjust from 5K/10K college training to road racing; half serves as stepping-stone between 5K/10K and marathon. Development takes patience — most Hansons-Brooks recruits were only average-to-good in college.
- tags: type:methodology subtopic:elite-development level:elite sport:running phase:offseason
- [SRC: Hansons Half-Marathon Method | 2014 | Appendix A]
- [EVIDENCE: author-opinion]

**ADAPT-006 — Fiber ratio "ideal" varies by runner speed**
No one-size-fits-all fiber mix. Faster runners benefit from intermediate (IIa) fibers (need high force); slower runners do best training like marathoners (mostly slow-twitch, high fat use, lower intensity, ~2× time on feet).
- tags: type:physiology subtopic:fiber-by-speed level:general-population sport:running system:neuromuscular
- [SRC: Hansons Half-Marathon Method | 2014 | Ch. 2]
- [EVIDENCE: author-opinion]

**ADAPT-007 — Caloric/hydration needs by body size & sex**
Bigger runners: more heat dissipation capacity but more surface area to gain heat → hotter, sweat more. Sex: males higher end of calorie ranges, females lower; leg mass 21% (M) vs 20% (F) for glycogen-storage calc.
- tags: type:nutrition subtopic:size-sex-adaptation level:general-population sport:running
- [SRC: Hansons Half-Marathon Method | 2014 | Ch. 8]

## 14. Common Mistakes, Contraindications & Cautions

**CAUT-001 — Overtraining from too-fast easy runs**
Running easy days too hard (esp. easy days sandwiched between SOS, e.g., Friday after Thursday tempo) causes overtraining, forced days off, injury. "When we say easy, we mean easy." Newer runners run easy too hard (plan feels too easy early); experienced runners run too hard from zeal.
- tags: type:caution subtopic:easy-too-hard level:general-population sport:running
- [SRC: Hansons Half-Marathon Method | 2014 | Ch. 1, Ch. 3]

**CAUT-002 — Speed workouts run above 100% VO2max**
Running speed intervals at/above 100% VO2max breaks down structural muscle, forces anaerobic reliance, overstresses anaerobic system, and nullifies aerobic gains + risks injury. Illustrative error: 6×800 at 5K pace done too fast early (2:45s) then fading (3:10s) — averages the pace but accumulates zero time at target.
- tags: type:caution subtopic:speed-too-fast level:intermediate sport:running system:vo2max
- [SRC: Hansons Half-Marathon Method | 2014 | Ch. 1, Ch. 3]

**CAUT-003 — Too-slow easy running**
Excessively slow easy runs just break down tendon/bone without aerobic benefit — stay within the easy range, don't over-slow.
- tags: type:caution subtopic:too-slow level:general-population sport:running
- [SRC: Hansons Half-Marathon Method | 2014 | Ch. 3]

**CAUT-004 — Tinkering with the long run first**
Don't add mileage via the long run; keep it 25–30% of weekly mileage, <3 hr. Grueling long runs after low-mileage weeks (e.g., 16-mi long run ending a 3-day/wk plan) are demoralizing/injurious.
- tags: type:caution subtopic:long-run-error level:general-population sport:running phase:build
- [SRC: Hansons Half-Marathon Method | 2014 | Ch. 3, Ch. 4]

**CAUT-005 — Racing too often**
Racing every week/other week causes missed workouts and a compromised training cycle ("debacle"). Race sparingly; weigh pros/cons, especially near race day.
- tags: type:caution subtopic:over-racing level:general-population sport:running phase:race
- [SRC: Hansons Half-Marathon Method | 2014 | Ch. 4, Ch. 5]

**CAUT-006 — Abrupt/over-aggressive taper**
Cutting everything (mileage, workouts, intensity, days) too fast → sluggish, more fatigued. Taper gradually; keep the same number of running days; last SOS 10 days out.
- tags: type:caution subtopic:taper-error level:general-population sport:running phase:taper
- [SRC: Hansons Half-Marathon Method | 2014 | Ch. 3]

**CAUT-007 — Ballistic & pre-run static stretching**
Avoid ballistic (bouncy, beyond-range) stretching (injury). Static stretching before running/racing reduces force production and risks muscle tears — do static stretching post-run only.
- tags: type:caution subtopic:stretching-error level:general-population sport:running
- [SRC: Hansons Half-Marathon Method | 2014 | Ch. 7]

**CAUT-008 — New activities/gear/minimalist shoes during training**
Don't start new crosstraining, new shoes, or minimalist footwear mid-block or on race day. Minimalist transition needs months; rushing → injury (~2 weeks per Lieberman et al.). Non-adapted runner switching to barefoot/minimalist heel-strikes with ~7× the impact force of a shod heel strike.
- tags: type:caution subtopic:new-stimulus level:general-population sport:running
- [SRC: Hansons Half-Marathon Method | 2014 | Ch. 7, Ch. 9]

**CAUT-009 — Crosstraining that aggravates injury / masks overtraining**
Match crosstraining to the injury (e.g., biking aggravates a broken foot). Don't replace running with crosstraining to "handle" overtraining — take a day off instead. Rowing = poor running carryover (upper-body focus).
- tags: type:caution subtopic:crosstraining-misuse level:general-population sport:running
- [SRC: Hansons Half-Marathon Method | 2014 | Ch. 7]

**CAUT-010 — Diet/dehydration contraindications**
Avoid ≥1,000-cal deficits/skipped meals during training; don't start calorie-cutting diets during endurance training. Chronic carb depletion and chronic dehydration both hinder performance. Overdrinking plain water risks hyponatremia. Cognitive impairment from dehydration can prevent self-rescue.
- tags: type:caution subtopic:diet-hydration level:general-population sport:running nutrition
- [SRC: Hansons Half-Marathon Method | 2014 | Ch. 8]

**CAUT-011 — Overstriding**
Overstriding (reaching leg forward, heel-first, toes up) creates braking, more shock, longer ground contact, slower pace — underlying cause of many injuries.
- tags: type:caution subtopic:overstriding level:general-population sport:running
- [SRC: Hansons Half-Marathon Method | 2014 | Ch. 9]

**CAUT-012 — Medical consultation triggers**
Consult a runner-familiar physician for injuries before self-diagnosing/treating; seek physician/sports-nutritionist for individualized nutrition concerns. General health can be jeopardized by under-fueling.
- tags: type:caution subtopic:medical-consult level:general-population sport:running
- [SRC: Hansons Half-Marathon Method | 2014 | Ch. 5, Ch. 8]

## 15. Formulas & Calculations

**FORM-001 — VO2max (Balke test, Vigil equation):**
`VO2max = 0.178 × ([m ÷ 15] − 150) + 33.3`
- m = distance run in 15 min (meters); 33.3 = base; special case subtracts if speed <150 m/min. (See TEST-001 for full worked example → 54 ml/kg/min.)
- tags: type:formula subtopic:vo2max level:intermediate sport:running metric:vo2
- [SRC: Hansons Half-Marathon Method | 2014 | Ch. 2]

**FORM-002 — Daily calories (light–moderate):** `body weight (lb) × 16–20 cal/lb`
**FORM-003 — Daily calories (very active):** `body weight (lb) × 21–25 cal/lb`
- Age adjustment: men −10 cal/yr past 20; women −7 cal/yr.
- tags: type:formula subtopic:caloric-needs level:general-population sport:running nutrition
- [SRC: Hansons Half-Marathon Method | 2014 | Ch. 8]

**FORM-004 — Race caloric expenditure:** `calories = distance(km) × body weight(kg)`, from `1 cal/kg/km`
- kg = lb ÷ 2.2; half-marathon = 21.0975 km. Example: 21.0975 × 68.18 = 1,438 cal.
- tags: type:formula subtopic:race-expenditure level:advanced sport:running phase:race
- [SRC: Hansons Half-Marathon Method | 2014 | Ch. 8]

**FORM-005 — Carbohydrate expenditure per mile:** `(total cal ÷ 13.1) × intensity fraction`
- e.g., 110 cal/mi × 0.60/0.75/0.85 = 66/77/93.5 carb cal/mi.
- tags: type:formula subtopic:carb-expenditure level:advanced sport:running
- [SRC: Hansons Half-Marathon Method | 2014 | Ch. 8]

**FORM-006 — Potential leg-glycogen storage:** `body weight(kg) × leg-mass fraction(0.21 M / 0.20 F) × 80 cal/kg`
- Example: 68.18 × 0.21 × 80 = 1,146 cal.
- tags: type:formula subtopic:glycogen-storage level:advanced sport:running
- [SRC: Hansons Half-Marathon Method | 2014 | Ch. 8]

**FORM-007 — Post-run rehydration:** `2.5 cups water per pound of body weight lost`
- tags: type:formula subtopic:rehydration level:general-population sport:running nutrition recovery
- [SRC: Hansons Half-Marathon Method | 2014 | Ch. 8]

**FORM-008 — Dehydration performance impact:** per 1% body-weight loss → HR up to +7 bpm; pace ~2% slower.
- tags: type:formula subtopic:dehydration-impact level:general-population sport:running
- [SRC: Hansons Half-Marathon Method | 2014 | Ch. 8]

**FORM-009 — Missed-training goal adjustment:** 2 wk off → +3–5% to goal time; 3 wk off → +7–10%.
- Example: 2:00 (120 min) goal, 2 wk off → +3.6 to +6 min → 2:03–2:06.
- tags: type:formula subtopic:goal-adjustment level:general-population sport:running
- [SRC: Hansons Half-Marathon Method | 2014 | Ch. 5]

**FORM-010 — Weather pace adjustment:** ~+5 s/mi at 60°F, +10 s/mi at 70°F, +15 s/mi at 80°F (range 5–8 s/mi at 60s+).
- tags: type:formula subtopic:weather-pace level:general-population sport:running phase:race metric:pace
- [SRC: Hansons Half-Marathon Method | 2014 | Ch. 6, Ch. 10]

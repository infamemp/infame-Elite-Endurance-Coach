## 2. Philosophy & Core Principles

### PH-1 — Emulate principles, not numbers
Any runner regardless of talent, age, or experience can improve by borrowing from professionals — but borrow the *principles* by which pros train and live, not their exact paces or volumes.
- Example: "Training like a pro" ≠ running 100+ mi/wk; it means running *a lot relative to one's personal limit*. Sleeping like a pro ≠ 10 hrs/day; it means *consistently getting all the sleep you need*. Eating like a pro ≠ 5000 kcal/day; it means the *core eating habits* pros share.
- tags: type:philosophy level:general-population subtopic:pro-emulation
- [SRC: Run Like a Pro | 2022 | Ch.1]
- [EVIDENCE: author-opinion]

### PH-2 — Elite vs nonelite practice gap
Pros do most running at low intensity; nonelites at moderate. Pros do runner-specific functional strength; nonelites skip it or do CrossFit/yoga. Pros eat balanced/inclusive natural-food diets; nonelites favor elimination diets (keto/plant-based/Paleo). Most nonelites simply don't *know* what pros do.
- tags: type:philosophy level:general-population subtopic:pro-emulation
- [SRC: Run Like a Pro | 2022 | Ch.1]
- [EVIDENCE: author-opinion]

### PH-3 — Two kinds of talent; trainability is widespread
"Walking-around fitness" (innate distance ability, rooted in a small set of VO2max genes) vs "trainability" (capacity to gain fitness from training, from a different/broader gene set). Pros have both. To benefit from pro-style training you only need *some* trainability, which is far more common than walking-around fitness genes.
- tags: type:physiology level:general-population subtopic:trainability
- [SRC: Run Like a Pro | 2022 | Ch.1]
- [EVIDENCE: study] Bouchard, Pennington Biomedical, 2017 — trainability scoring: more subjects (52) had the highest possible score than the lowest (36).

### PH-4 — Race demands are universal; individualization is granular
No matter your genotype, training must prepare you for the specific demands of your target race, and those demands are the same for every participant. Individualization (as Coach Ben does at NAZ Elite) happens at a granular level — everything else is trained the same because all are human training for the same event types.
- tags: type:philosophy type:methodology level:general-population subtopic:specificity
- [SRC: Run Like a Pro | 2022 | Ch.1]
- [EVIDENCE: author-opinion]

### PH-5 — Rosario's coaching philosophy: blend of art and science
"Hodgepodge" borrowed from mentors (Linhares, Barney, Schneider, K. Hanson, McMillan). Science from reading/conferences/conversations; art from personal successes and mistakes. Central lessons: importance of form drills; power of long-term aerobic development; "train to race, not train to train"; mileage sweet spot; plyometrics to unlock speed.
- tags: type:philosophy level:elite subtopic:coaching-philosophy
- [SRC: Run Like a Pro | 2022 | Introduction]
- [EVIDENCE: author-opinion]

### PH-6 — "It's about being ready on the day"
Success comes from being mentally and physically ready on race day, not from cramming missed work. A segment can survive missed/modified workouts if the bulk of the intended work is completed.
- tags: type:philosophy type:heuristic level:general-population subtopic:race-readiness
- [SRC: Run Like a Pro | 2022 | Ch.2 Coach's Tip]
- [EVIDENCE: case-study] Scott Smith, Frankfurt 2017: 2:12:21 PB despite missed/modified work due to infection.

## 3. Underlying Mechanisms

### ME-1 — Dose-response of running volume; three benefits of high volume
A dose-response relationship exists between running volume and performance. Three mechanistic benefits of running a lot:
1. **Greater fitness** — higher VO2max and endurance (fatigue resistance).
2. **Better efficiency** — running economy improves with accumulated skill/practice.
3. **Heightened durability** — tissue adaptation reduces injury risk.
- tags: type:physiology level:general-population subtopic:training-volume metric:load system:aerobic
- [SRC: Run Like a Pro | 2022 | Ch.3]
- [EVIDENCE: study] Emig & Peltonen, *Nature*, 2020 (14,000 runners, 1.6M sessions): correlation between volume and performance both across and *within* individuals; volume→performance mediated primarily by running economy gains. Vickers & Vertosick, *BMC Sports Sci Med Rehabil*, 2016 (2,300+ runners): mileage-velocity association similar across race distances.

### ME-2 — Glycogen flux and aerobic capacity
Proposed mechanism for high-volume→VO2max: glycogen depletion activates genes that boost aerobic capacity; rest + carb intake replenishes stores. More glycogen flux over time = more stimulus. Requires eating plenty of carbs, resting adequately, AND running a lot.
- tags: type:physiology level:advanced subtopic:glycogen-flux system:aerobic
- [SRC: Run Like a Pro | 2022 | Ch.3]
- [EVIDENCE: derivation] "not yet fully understood... may have to do with"

### ME-3 — Running as skill; efficiency via unconscious self-optimization
Running is a skill. The brain continuously communicates with proprioceptive nerves, refining the motor program (blueprint) to reduce waste. Running economy improves with experience and does not decline with age the way VO2max/strength/power do. The organism optimizes *mental effort/perceived effort*, not strictly energy cost.
- tags: type:physiology level:intermediate subtopic:running-economy system:neuromuscular
- [SRC: Run Like a Pro | 2022 | Ch.3, Ch.6]
- [EVIDENCE: study] Hunter & Smith, *Eur J Appl Physiol*, 2007 (unconscious stride adjustments minimize efficiency loss with fatigue); Univ. New Hampshire 2011 (running economy preserved with age despite VO2max/strength decline); Dixon (Exeter) 2012 (8.4% economy improvement in 10 wks, no technique instruction).

### ME-4 — Running protects against the injuries it causes
Running stress inflicts microscopic tissue damage; if next stress comes too soon, damage accumulates into injury; if healing completes, tissues become stronger and more resistant. Injuries occur when tissues face more stress than they're ready for (testing limits).
- tags: type:physiology type:caution level:general-population subtopic:injury-prevention system:musculoskeletal
- [SRC: Run Like a Pro | 2022 | Ch.3]
- [EVIDENCE: study] Danish 2013 (662 marathoners): <30 km/wk injured >2× as often as 30–60 km/wk; >60 km/wk no more than 30–60. Rauh (SDSU), *JOSPT*, 2014 (421 HS runners): <8 wks summer running → far more injuries. UMass Amherst 2014 (biomechanical differences reduce knee injury in higher-mileage runners).

### ME-5 — Acute:Chronic training load ratio (injury spike threshold)
When acute training load (past ~1 week volume+intensity) exceeds chronic training load (past ~4 weeks) by more than ~10%, injury likelihood spikes in the following 2–3 weeks. Keep ATL in the range 101–110% of CTL.
- tags: type:physiology type:heuristic level:intermediate subtopic:training-load metric:load
- [SRC: Run Like a Pro | 2022 | Ch.3, Ch.7]
- [EVIDENCE: study] Dutch/German team, *Int J Sports Med*, 2020 (23 recreational runners, 2-yr diaries).

### ME-6 — Progressive overload
To improve, subject the body to challenges somewhat greater than accustomed. Training must trend toward increased training load (unless already overtrained) AND become more event-specific over a cycle.
- tags: type:methodology type:physiology level:general-population subtopic:progressive-overload metric:load
- [SRC: Run Like a Pro | 2022 | Ch.2]
- [EVIDENCE: study] Banister's TRIMP; Italian study, 8-wk progressive program: correlation coefficient 0.82 between TRIMP and 10,000 m TT performance (0.7 strong, 1.0 perfect).

### ME-7 — Fat is an inefficient fuel (why low-carb hurts endurance)
Fat requires more oxygen than carbohydrate to metabolize; VO2max caps oxygen consumption. Low-carb adaptation increases fat-burning but decreases carbohydrate-burning capacity, lowering the speed at which VO2max is reached — net negative on performance.
- tags: type:physiology type:nutrition level:advanced subtopic:carbohydrate system:fatoxidation
- [SRC: Run Like a Pro | 2022 | Ch.8]
- [EVIDENCE: clinical-trial] Burke et al., Australian Institute of Sport (race walkers): keto group peak fat oxidation rose 0.6→1.3 g/min but 10K racewalk declined avg 2.3% vs +4.8% for high-carb group (even with 17-day carb replenishment).

### ME-8 — Supercompensation / recovery as fitness driver
Most fitness adaptations are extensions of the recovery process (supercompensation). Recovery only happens when there is something to recover from; a "runner" who only rests is merely sedentary. Modulate load to create enough recovery demand to build fitness while supplying enough recovery to avoid injury/burnout.
- tags: type:physiology type:recovery level:general-population subtopic:supercompensation
- [SRC: Run Like a Pro | 2022 | Ch.7]
- [EVIDENCE: author-opinion / derivation]

### ME-9 — Legs as springs; free energy and leg stiffness
~Half the energy to maintain a given running speed is "free energy" returned from the ground. Stiffer "springs" (legs) capture/reuse more free energy; plyometrics increase leg stiffness, reduce ground contact time, improve economy.
- tags: type:physiology level:intermediate subtopic:leg-stiffness system:neuromuscular
- [SRC: Run Like a Pro | 2022 | Ch.6]
- [EVIDENCE: study] Paavolainen et al., Univ Jyväskylä, 1999 (9 wks plyo → improved economy, 5K times, reduced ground contact); Chilean 2017 (6 wks, 2×/wk, <30 min/session → significant benefits).

### ME-10 — Psychology limits performance; pacing is by feel
Except in rare health catastrophes, runners stop/slow because they *feel* they can't continue, not for a physiological cause. Pacing is learned by experience and executed by feel; supplying false performance data doesn't change TT performance.
- tags: type:physiology type:philosophy level:general-population subtopic:pacing metric:rpe
- [SRC: Run Like a Pro | 2022 | Ch.5]
- [EVIDENCE: study] Univ Exeter 2008 (cyclists, blinded distance TTs); studies on inaccurate performance feedback.

### ME-11 — First ventilatory threshold (VT1) and why moderate/high intensity is more stressful
Above VT1, the brain must recruit oxygen-hogging fast-twitch fibers to sustain output; the brain works harder, autonomic recovery slows, maximum tolerable dose drops. VT1 is now the accepted low/moderate boundary due to the abrupt jump in stressfulness.
- tags: type:physiology level:advanced subtopic:ventilatory-threshold system:threshold metric:hr
- [SRC: Run Like a Pro | 2022 | Ch.4]
- [EVIDENCE: study] Seiler et al., *Med Sci Sports Exerc*, 2007 (HRV-tracked recovery markedly slower above VT1).

### ME-12 — Maximally effective dose (pharmacology analogy)
For runners, the maximally effective dose of low-intensity training far exceeds that of moderate-to-high-intensity training — even though minute-for-minute high intensity is more effective. This underlies the 80/20 balance. (Minimum effective dose < maximally effective dose < maximum tolerable dose.)
- tags: type:physiology type:methodology level:advanced subtopic:intensity-distribution
- [SRC: Run Like a Pro | 2022 | Ch.4]
- [EVIDENCE: study] French 1999 (elite mid-distance): highest VO2max after 80/20 block; high-intensity block produced lowest scores + markers of nonfunctional overreaching (↑norepinephrine, suppressed HR).

### QR-2 — VT1 (low/moderate boundary) reference values
For most trained runners, VT1 falls at:
- 77–81% of maximum heart rate
- 60–65% of maximum aerobic speed (MAS)
- Rating of 4 on a 1–10 perceived effort scale
- Fitter runners are nearer the top of these ranges.
- Worked example: 45:00 flat 10K runner → MAS ≈ 9.4 mph (6:23/mi); 65% = 6.11 mph (9:49/mi) → VT1 pace 2:25/mi slower than 10K race pace.
- Slowest pace at which any runner "wouldn't be better off walking" ≈ 13:00/mi.
- tags: type:test type:formula level:general-population subtopic:ventilatory-threshold metric:hr metric:pace
- [SRC: Run Like a Pro | 2022 | Ch.4]
- [EVIDENCE: study]

### QR-3 — VT2 (moderate/high boundary) reference values
- ~84–87% of MAS
- ~91–93% of HRmax
- Aligns closely with critical velocity (CV); running to exhaustion at CV usually lasts 20–30 min.
- tags: type:test type:formula level:advanced subtopic:ventilatory-threshold metric:hr metric:pace
- [SRC: Run Like a Pro | 2022 | Ch.4]
- [EVIDENCE: study]

### QR-4 — 80/20 intensity balance (population optimum)
~80% of training time at low intensity (below VT1 / below ~82% HRmax) and ~20% at moderate+high combined. Applies on weekly/monthly/yearly timescales, NOT within a single day; not to be applied rigidly (round numbers not magical).
- Elite reference data: Norwegian track runners 76.4/79/80.8% low intensity across three seasonal periods; road racers 83.6/84.7/79.9%. Billat 2003: elite Kenyans ~85% below lactate threshold. 3 Canadian marathoners: 74% low intensity. Pro cyclists (Sanders 2017): 79.5% low intensity.
- tags: type:methodology type:test level:general-population subtopic:80-20 system:aerobic
- [SRC: Run Like a Pro | 2022 | Ch.4]
- [EVIDENCE: study] Norwegian universities study, *Int J Sports Sci Coaching*, 2010; supporting comparative studies across endurance sports.

### QR-5 — 80/20 performance evidence
- 30 club runners (~40-min 10K), 9 wks: 80/20 group +5% (best adherers +7%); 50/50 group +3.5%. The ~35 s difference on the clock (12 s more for best adherers).
- Spanish 2018 (ultrarunners, 12 wks): 80/20 +2.4% time-to-exhaustion; more-moderate group no improvement.
- Pyramidal vs polarized triathletes (Spanish, *J Sports Sci Med*, 2019, 20 wks): pyramidal ~78% low / 19% moderate / 3% high improved more than polarized ~85/4/11; both adhered to ~80/20 overall.
- tags: type:evidence level:general-population subtopic:80-20
- [SRC: Run Like a Pro | 2022 | Ch.4]
- [EVIDENCE: meta-analysis / multiple studies]

### QR-6 — Nutrition macro targets
- Baseline carbohydrate during race-focused training: ~60% of daily calories. Typical Western diet is slightly <50% carb.
- Habitual elite carb intake ranges: 60% (Netherlands) to 78% (Ethiopia) of daily calories; most runners perform equally well across this range if overall quality is high.
- Low-carb health warning: <40% of daily calories from carbs → died ~4 yrs younger on avg vs 40–70% (esp. when carbs replaced with animal fat/protein).
- tags: type:nutrition type:formula level:general-population subtopic:carbohydrate
- [SRC: Run Like a Pro | 2022 | Ch.8]
- [EVIDENCE: study] Seidelmann et al., ARIC study, *Lancet Public Health*, Aug 2018 (15,400+ adults, 25 yrs).

### QR-7 — Training-load recovery guardrails
Keep acute:chronic training load at 101–110%. Recovery/step cycle: increase load 2–3 weeks, then reduce ~30% for one week. Recovery weeks reduce load by ~30%.

## 6. Testing & Assessment Protocols

### T-1 — Six-Minute Test (VT1/MAS estimate) [TIER B]
1. Find flat, smooth, fast route; warm up thoroughly.
2. Run as far as possible in 6:00.
3. Convert average pace to speed; multiply by 0.65 (VT1) → convert back to pace = upper limit of low-intensity range.
4. If pacing botched, wait ~48 hrs and retry.
- Worked example: 1.09 mi in 6:00 = 5:30/mi = 10.9 mph (MAS). ×0.65 = 7.09 mph = 8:27/mi VT1 pace.
- VT2 pace: 84–87% of MAS. (Same test used to derive both.)
- tags: type:test level:general-population subtopic:vt1 metric:pace
- [SRC: Run Like a Pro | 2022 | Ch.4]

### T-2 — Maximum Heart Rate (HRmax) test [TIER B]
1. Strap on HR monitor; jog 1–2 miles.
2. Run 1 mile at moderate intensity (~6/10 effort).
3. Accelerate gradually over next 0.25 mile.
4. Run 0.25 mile as fast as possible; highest reading = HRmax.
- Then VT1 HR = 77–81% of HRmax (fitter → top end); VT2 HR = 91–93% of HRmax.
- Example: HRmax 185 → VT1 HR 142–150 bpm.
- Caution: torturous protocol.
- tags: type:test level:general-population subtopic:hrmax metric:hr
- [SRC: Run Like a Pro | 2022 | Ch.4]

### T-3 — Talk Test (VT1 estimate) [TIER B]
1. Pick device(s) to calibrate (GPS watch, HR monitor, power meter).
2. Flat, smooth route; warm up.
3. Increase speed in small increments each minute.
4. At each 1:00 mark recite ~50-syllable statement (e.g., Pledge of Allegiance); note whether you can speak *comfortably*.
5. If unsure whether comfortable → counts as uncomfortable.
6. VT1 = highest pace/HR/power at which you still "pass" (can speak comfortably).
- Smaller speed increments = more accurate; treadmills often poorly calibrated. Repeatable frequently (ends at low intensity).
- tags: type:test level:general-population subtopic:vt1 metric:hr metric:pace
- [SRC: Run Like a Pro | 2022 | Ch.4]
- [EVIDENCE: study] Foster et al., *J Sports Sci Med*, 2002.

### T-4 — Relaxed 10K Time Trial (used in plans) [TIER B]
Run 10K at 10KP + 5% (slower than all-out 10K), sandwiched by 1–3 mi easy warm-up/cool-down per level. Used as a fitness check within marathon/ultra plans.
- tags: type:test level:general-population subtopic:time-trial metric:pace
- [SRC: Run Like a Pro | 2022 | Marathon/Ultra plans]

### T-5 — Subjective recovery monitoring
Subjective factors (mood, motivation, perceived energy) predict acute/chronic training loads with superior sensitivity and consistency vs objective devices (HRV monitors, sleep monitors). Use "how you feel" as the final say; if you feel refreshed on waking and not sleepy until bedtime, sleep is adequate.
- tags: type:test type:recovery level:general-population subtopic:monitoring metric:hrv
- [SRC: Run Like a Pro | 2022 | Ch.7]
- [EVIDENCE: meta-analysis] Australian review, 2015.

## 7. Methodology & Progression Logic

### M-1 — The Five Rules of Pro-Style Planning
1. **Start Where You Are** — base plan selection on *training history*, not goal times or PBs. Next plan should administer a load slightly greater than the heaviest you handled successfully for a prior race of the same distance. (e.g., built to 45 mi/wk last marathon → build to 50 next.)
2. **Choose a Direction (Periodization)** — training must (a) trend toward increased load and (b) progress from general to event-specific. No single best periodization model.
3. **(Almost) Always Do Less than You Think You Could** — create the limit plan, discard it, make one 10–20% easier. Feel good most of the time; feeling lousy often signals training is *too* hard.
4. **Don't Race Too Often** — each race costs ~1 full week of normal training (taper + recovery). 5Ks: every ~2 wks for ~8 wks max. Marathons: possibly 2 in 6 wks if very fit, then ≥3 months to rebuild.
5. **Rest and Take Breaks** — rest enables more training. Prefer relative rest (recovery weeks, ~30% reduction every 3rd/4th wk) over absolute rest; take breaks between cycles.
- tags: type:methodology level:general-population subtopic:planning
- [SRC: Run Like a Pro | 2022 | Ch.2]
- [EVIDENCE: author-opinion + study] Univ Western Australia, *JSCR*, 2018 (linear vs reverse-linear periodization both beat non-periodized: +1:16, +1:52 vs +0:03 on 5K; difference between periodized groups statistically insignificant).

### M-2 — Cycle length / break frequency
- Max ~24 weeks of progressive training before burnout risk.
- Longer/less-fit-start (marathon) may need full 24 wks; shorter events / fit start may need only 10–12 wks.
- Ideal cycle culminates in 1–2 wk functional overreaching, then taper, then race, then break.
- tags: type:methodology type:heuristic level:general-population subtopic:periodization
- [SRC: Run Like a Pro | 2022 | Ch.2]
- [EVIDENCE: author-opinion]

### M-3 — Volume progression logic (Zero → Seven → beyond)
1. Absolute beginner: alternate-day running (48-hr recovery), walk/jog intervals building to 30:00 continuous over several weeks (see P-BEG).
2. Add consecutive days one at a time; add duration in small increments toward the Seven-Hour Standard.
3. Beyond 7 hrs: add frequency (doubles) before extending existing runs — running more frequently is gentler than equal added volume via longer runs. Pros spread 10–15 hrs across 12–13 runs.
4. Find mileage sweet spot by climbing weekly structures (Options A→B→C) one rung at a time, evaluating improvement vs setbacks.
- tags: type:methodology level:beginner level:intermediate subtopic:volume-progression metric:load
- [SRC: Run Like a Pro | 2022 | Ch.3]
- [EVIDENCE: author-opinion]

### M-4 — Measure by time, not distance
Stress is determined by time exposure, not distance covered (vVO2max example: all runners exhaust at ~6 min regardless of distance covered). Pro ~12 hrs/wk = 110–120 mi; well-trained nonelite matching *time* covers ~65–70 mi. Measuring by distance is fine, but "the body measures things in time."
- tags: type:methodology level:general-population subtopic:time-based-training metric:load
- [SRC: Run Like a Pro | 2022 | Ch.3]
- [EVIDENCE: derivation]

### M-5 — Event specialization: consider, but not too much
Pros train similarly across distances (draw from same workout formats, 80/20 balance, high volume). Nonelites over-specialize. Most nonelites don't run enough to maximize performance at *any* distance, so train roughly as much for short races as long. Race-time predictors overestimate marathon (nonelites under-prepared volume-wise vs shorter events).
- tags: type:methodology level:general-population subtopic:specificity
- [SRC: Run Like a Pro | 2022 | Ch.3, Training Plans Overview]
- [EVIDENCE: author-opinion]

### M-6 — Consistency, but not too consistent
Aim for consistent volume, but not chronically at physical ceiling (injury risk from testing limits — both increasing toward and hovering at ceiling too long). Hang out slightly below current ceiling most of the time; use gentle fluctuations + recovery weeks.
- Half-marathon example: if heaviest weeks ≤60 mi, median 45–50 mi/wk, most weeks in range with low points (short breaks) and brief peaks at 60.
- Kellyn Taylor example (not testing limits despite aggressive build): week off → 42 → 79 → 97 mi after peaking at 128 mi in prior Boston buildup.
- tags: type:methodology type:heuristic level:intermediate subtopic:consistency metric:load
- [SRC: Run Like a Pro | 2022 | Ch.3]
- [EVIDENCE: case-study]

### M-7 — Balancing moderate vs high intensity within the 20%
Not spending enough time at high intensity is the second-biggest nonelite mistake (spending too much at moderate is the biggest). Author's advice: copy pros — favor moderate intensity for longer events, high intensity for shorter events. Skeptical of strict polarized training for most distances (race pace often falls in moderate range).
- tags: type:methodology level:intermediate subtopic:intensity-distribution
- [SRC: Run Like a Pro | 2022 | Ch.4]
- [EVIDENCE: author-opinion]

### M-8 — Plan execution rules (Coach Ben)
1. Don't overreact to one bad workout (one bad workout ≠ bad segment; two in a row warrants attention).
2. Listen to your body (sluggish consecutive workouts, life stress, low desire → adjust; write plans in pencil).
3. It's (almost) never too late to turn things around (blood work → reduced intensity → rebuild momentum; be ready on the day).
- tags: type:heuristic type:methodology level:general-population subtopic:autoregulation
- [SRC: Run Like a Pro | 2022 | Ch.2 Coach's Tip]
- [EVIDENCE: case-study] Rosario Twin Cities 2005 (2nd, $20,500); Grandma's 2006 (16th, over-tapered stubbornness); Scott Smith Frankfurt 2017.

### M-9 — Plan architecture (all plans)
Three-week step cycles with a recovery week every third week. Level 1: 4 runs/wk + 2 optional easy/cross-training + 1 rest; recovery via overall reduction. Level 2: 7 runs/wk (6 in recovery weeks, begins with rest day); drills+strides after 1 easy run/wk. Level 3: 9 runs/wk (8 in recovery weeks); adds plyos; matches pro workload.
- Shading system: dark = highly challenging; light = moderately challenging; unshaded = easy.

## 9. Discrete Protocol Library

### P-1 — Coach Ben's Favorite 5K Workout
- Goal / target outcome: Race-specific readiness for 5K (fast running on fatigued legs)
- Domain / context: endurance-sport
- Structure:
    - Warm-up: 1 mile easy (or 15–20:00 easy per level) + Drills and Strides
    - Main: 7 × 3:00 @ CV +0:08–0:10 per mile / 1:00 rest (3:00 rest after final rep); then 1-mile all-out time trial
    - Cool-down: 1 mile easy (or 15–20:00 easy)
- Intensity/dose anchors: CV+8–10 s/mi (≈15K pace) for reps; all-out for TT
- When used: ~9–10 days before goal 5K race
- Execution cues: reps just fast enough to fatigue while keeping HR high; TT simulates middle→final mile of 5K; enjoyable = effective
- Result cited: two athletes ran 1K reps in 2:55, mile TT 4:16; 9 days later 13:29 (4:20/mi) and 13:36 (4:22/mi) for 5000 m
- tags: type:protocol sport:running system:threshold system:vo2max metric:pace subtopic:5k level:advanced
- [SRC: Run Like a Pro | 2022 | Ch.10 Coach's Tip]
- [EVIDENCE: case-study]

### P-2 — Coach Ben's Favorite 10K Workout
- Goal: 10K-specific challenge (sustained high HR, tired-leg finishing kick)
- Domain / context: endurance-sport
- Structure:
    - Warm-up: 15–20:00 easy + Drills and Strides
    - Main: 8 × 3:00 @ CV / 1:00 rest (3:00–5:00 rest after last rep); then 4 × 300 m @ VHI, cutdown (each faster than previous) / 1:00 rest
    - Cool-down: 15–20:00 easy
- Intensity/dose anchors: CV (30-min pace) for reps; 300s progress from ~mile pace down (example: 52, 51, 50, 49 s)
- Rationale: 3:00 at 30-min pace = akin to 1:00 at 10-min pace; multiple reps on short rest = exponential specificity. 300s train final-kick (in a 10K on the track, ~300 m to go is where you switch gears).
- Pro version: 10 × 1K (≈3:00 each) instead of 8 × 3:00; nonelites use time-based version.
- 300 pacing derivation: start from mile pace = target for rep #3; make #1 two seconds slower, cut down to end. Reps <600 m need no altitude conversion.
- Result cited: Kellyn Taylor (team 10,000 m record holder) 10×1K in 3:18 @7,300 ft (=3:07 sea level) + 4×300 (52/51/50/49); ~2.5 wks later 31:07.60 for 10,000 m.
- tags: type:protocol sport:running system:threshold metric:pace subtopic:10k level:advanced
- [SRC: Run Like a Pro | 2022 | Ch.11 Coach's Tip]
- [EVIDENCE: case-study]

### P-3 — Coach Ben's Favorite Half-Marathon Workout
- Goal: HM-specific — run hard on ~1 hr of accumulated running volume
- Domain / context: endurance-sport
- Structure:
    - 15:00 @ LTP + 10 s/mi (i.e., 10 s/mi *slower* than LT pace)
    - 8:00 jog recovery
    - 3 × 5:00 @ LTP − 10 s/mi (10 s/mi *faster* than LT pace) / 2:00 rest (5:00 after #3)
    - 15:00 @ LTP + 10 s/mi
- Intensity/dose anchors: LT pace ± 10 s/mi. Worked example (LT 6:00/mi): tempos at 6:10; five-min efforts at 5:50.
- Total ~90 min including warm-up/cool-down; final tempo done on ~1 hr of prior running (simulates late-race fatigue).
- Philosophy: HM best run off an *eclectic* block (accidentally be ready) — hills, track, roads, slower- and faster-than-race-pace work. (Rory Linkletter example: 1:01:44 HM off a wide-range block; example sessions listed in Section 11.)

- [SRC: Run Like a Pro | 2022 | Ch.12 Coach's Tip]

### P-4 — Coach Ben's Favorite Marathon Workout ("The 10/10")
- Goal: Simulate the final miles of a marathon (cement-legs feeling on tired legs)
- Domain / context: endurance-sport
- Structure: 2:00:00 continuous — first 1:00:00 @ SSP + 1:00/mile ("medium"); second 1:00:00 @ SSP ("hard"/marathon effort). Plans append +1:00 @ SSP.
    - Warm-up/cool-down in plans: 10–20:00 easy each side.
- Intensity/dose anchors: first half = marathon pace + 50 s/mi (for pros); second half = marathon/steady-state pace. For pros "marathon pace" ≈ SSP (2–2.5 hr).
- Rationale: marathon problem isn't pace, it's whether tissues tolerate 26.2 mi of pounding; needs many 10–16 mi at/near race pace on tired legs from high weekly volume.
- Recovery: requires an easy day after (even 2:09 marathoner Scott Fauble needs it — 4 mi easy next day).
- Origin: adapted from Terrence Mahon coaching Ryan Hall (Mammoth Lakes).
- Result cited: Tuliamuk, Bruce, Taylor did it 1 month out from 2020 Olympic Trials; their 22nd mile of the day (incl. 2-mi warm-up) at marathon effort → finished 1st, 6th, 8th.

- [SRC: Run Like a Pro | 2022 | Ch.13 Coach's Tip]

### P-5 — Coach Ben's Favorite Ultramarathon Workout (Back-to-Back Long Runs)

- Domain / context: endurance-sport (ultra)
- Structure: Day 1 = 3:00:00–4:00:00; Day 2 = 2:00:00–3:00:00 (combined 5–7 hrs over a weekend). In plans (L3 Week 17): Sat 3:30:00 easy (no calories before/during) + Sun 3:00:00 easy.

## 10. Professional Heuristics & Decision Rules

### H-1 — Discard-the-limit plan rule
Build the plan representing your body's limit, discard it, build one 10–20% easier.
- tags: type:heuristic level:general-population subtopic:planning
- [SRC: Run Like a Pro | 2022 | Ch.2]

### H-2 — Feeling-lousy signal
If you begin feeling lousy more often than good, reductions in performance/fitness are imminent — training is too hard, not "hard enough."
- tags: type:heuristic type:caution level:general-population subtopic:autoregulation
- [SRC: Run Like a Pro | 2022 | Ch.2]
- [EVIDENCE: study] (studies show performance/fitness decline follows)

### H-3 — Easy-run pacing by comfort
Maintain a *consistent comfort level* across easy runs even if pace is inconsistent. Feel very comfortable start to finish; on tired days run well below VT1; on good days up to VT1; allow fluctuation within a run. Never let perceived effort exceed 4/10 on easy runs. If you can't speak in complete sentences, you're too fast. (You basically can't run too slow on easy days.)
- tags: type:heuristic level:general-population subtopic:easy-runs metric:rpe metric:pace
- [SRC: Run Like a Pro | 2022 | Ch.5, Training Plans Overview]

### H-4 — Workout effort target (intensity-target sessions)
Aim to finish feeling you could have run reps ~10% faster or ~10% farther (i.e., ~9/10 effort overall) — "difficult but not grueling," not race-in-practice.
- Ray Treacy: "When I hear someone say 'I just had the best workout of my life,' I get scared. It probably means they just ran their race in practice."
- tags: type:heuristic level:general-population subtopic:effort-management metric:rpe
- [SRC: Run Like a Pro | 2022 | Ch.2, Ch.5]

### H-5 — Pace-target workout intent
It's the *effort to nail* the target pace that matters, not perfect success; don't stray more than a couple seconds/rep in either direction. Same physiological benefit whether splits are perfect or slightly variable.
- tags: type:heuristic level:general-population subtopic:pacing metric:pace
- [SRC: Run Like a Pro | 2022 | Ch.5]

### H-6 — Two-bad-workouts rule (Coach Ben)
One bad workout ≠ bad segment; two in a row = investigate (rest, easy days, blood work, reduce intensity).
- tags: type:heuristic type:caution level:general-population subtopic:autoregulation
- [SRC: Run Like a Pro | 2022 | Ch.2 Coach's Tip, Ch.9]

### H-7 — Don't be a slave to numbers
Prioritize doing the right amount each day; let weekly totals take care of themselves. Avoid chasing round numbers (100-mi weeks, etc.). Measure in time/Badger miles or don't track at all (many East Africans don't).
- tags: type:heuristic type:caution level:general-population subtopic:mileage
- [SRC: Run Like a Pro | 2022 | Ch.3]

### H-8 — Sweet-spot evaluation ladder
At current structure: if you see improvement and manage comfortably → advance a rung; if improvement with only small setbacks (normal aches) at a livable frequency → sweet spot; if no improvement or limit signs (incipient injuries, mounting fatigue, poor workouts) → drop back.
- tags: type:heuristic level:intermediate subtopic:mileage-sweet-spot
- [SRC: Run Like a Pro | 2022 | Ch.3]

### H-9 — Injury decision-making: reason over emotion ("inner Spock")
Take a mental step back at every injury-relevant decision; treat your athletic self as a separate person you're coaching. Cost-benefit calculated risks are fine (e.g., "I'd rather miss 2 miles today and train 100% next week" — Aaron Braun). Accept uncertainty; control the controllable.
- tags: type:heuristic level:general-population subtopic:injury-coping
- [SRC: Run Like a Pro | 2022 | Ch.9]
- [EVIDENCE: case-study]

### H-10 — Race discomfort mantras / metacognition
Use awareness of discomfort as a signal to engage a strategy (adjust pace, relax, chunk distance/time). Bracing ("This is going to hurt") + detachment ("I've been here before... this is nothing new"). Preselect a race emotion (anger, gratitude, joy). "Pain only hurts" (Scott Jurek).
- tags: type:heuristic level:general-population subtopic:mental-skills metric:rpe
- [SRC: Run Like a Pro | 2022 | Ch.9]
- [EVIDENCE: study] Brick et al., *Psychology of Sport and Exercise*, 2015.

### H-11 — Napkin-math training-load check
If total time is up with same fraction at higher intensities, OR same total time with more of it at higher intensities → load increased. Opposite → decreased.
- tags: type:heuristic type:formula level:general-population subtopic:training-load metric:load
- [SRC: Run Like a Pro | 2022 | Ch.7]

### H-12 — 30-day habit challenge
To overcome inertia when building sleep/other habits, commit to a 30-day, no-excuses challenge to build momentum.
- tags: type:heuristic level:general-population subtopic:habits
- [SRC: Run Like a Pro | 2022 | Ch.7]

## 11. Applied Guidance

### AG-1 — Warm-up like a pro
Pros warm up with a lengthy multimodal routine: activation exercises, jogging, drills, and strides (relaxed sprints). Nonelites typically just jog. Do drills + strides before every faster workout/race; do a longer drill session after an easy run 1–2×/wk.
- tags: type:methodology sport:running subtopic:warm-up level:general-population
- [SRC: Run Like a Pro | 2022 | Ch.1, Ch.6]

### AG-2 — Cross-training as fitness tool (not just for beginners)
Injury-prone runners can pad schedules with low/non-impact cardio (indoor/outdoor cycling, elliptical running, outdoor elliptical biking, uphill treadmill walking, XC skiing) to gain most run-fitness benefit with less injury risk. Best modalities involve independent leg movements.
- tags: type:methodology sport:running subtopic:cross-training level:general-population
- [SRC: Run Like a Pro | 2022 | Ch.3, Training Plans Overview]
- [EVIDENCE: study] Flynn et al., Purdue 1998 (extra runs vs bike, both +2.5% 5K); Meb Keflezighi outdoor elliptical biking → 2nd USATF Half 1:01:22 at 38.

### AG-3 — Five core eating habits ("rules")
1. **Eat everything** — well-rounded, inclusive; avoidance diets create deficiencies exposed by training stress.
2. **Eat quality** — highest quality: vegetables, fruits, nuts/seeds, whole grains, seafood; also unprocessed meat/dairy. Limit refined grains, processed meats, sweets, fried/added-fat foods.
3. **Eat carb-centered** — center meals/snacks on a high-quality, high-carb food; ~60% of calories baseline in training.
4. **Eat enough** — undereating is worse than overeating for runners; base diet on natural/unprocessed foods (more satiating); practice appetite awareness.
5. **Eat individually** — tailor within 6 factors: culture, preferences, trial-and-error, allergies/intolerances, lifestyle, values.
- tags: type:nutrition level:general-population subtopic:diet-principles
- [SRC: Run Like a Pro | 2022 | Ch.8]
- [EVIDENCE: multiple studies] Univ Bergamo 2019 (Mediterranean counseling → greater VO2max/body-comp gains); Saint Louis Univ (11 runners: 4 days Mediterranean → +6%/90 s faster 5K vs Western diet); *Cell Metabolism* 2019 (unprocessed diet → −508 kcal/day voluntarily, −2 lb; processed → +2 lb).

### AG-4 — Appetite awareness tips
- Don't start eating until physical hunger cues appear (except special occasions); don't let hunger persist long.
- Stop when comfortably satisfied, not stuffed.
- Avoid auto-finishing served food / eating just because food is there.
- Consider apps (Am I Hungry? Virtual Coach); if disordered-eating history, hire an RD.
- tags: type:nutrition type:heuristic level:general-population subtopic:appetite-regulation
- [SRC: Run Like a Pro | 2022 | Ch.8]

### AG-5 — Depletion run practice guidelines
- Simplest: longer low-intensity run on empty stomach first thing AM; water or water+electrolytes only (no carbs).
- Fancier: normally-fueled HIIT run PM → very-low-carb dinner → sleep → easy run empty stomach AM.
- Start conservatively (if long run is 15 mi, start with 10-mi carb-restricted). One depletion run every 1–2 weeks is plenty; boosts fat-burning AND VO2max (activates aerobic genes) without cutting carb-burning/economy.
- tags: type:nutrition type:protocol level:advanced subtopic:depletion-runs system:fatoxidation
- [SRC: Run Like a Pro | 2022 | Ch.8]

### AG-6 — Diet Quality Score (DQS)
Fitzgerald's simplified index for endurance athletes to monitor daily diet quality; details/portion sizes/scoring at racingweight.com; iPhone/Android app available.

## 12. Recovery, Monitoring & Lifestyle Factors

### R-1 — Recovery priorities (in order): rest, sleep, stress management, nutrition, corrective exercise
High-tech recovery methods (electrostimulation, compression/progression boots) are largely placebos; focus on the basics. Highest performers rely less on fancy methods.
- tags: type:recovery level:general-population subtopic:recovery-priorities
- [SRC: Run Like a Pro | 2022 | Ch.7]
- [EVIDENCE: study] *Frontiers in Physiology* review 2018 (minimal evidence for electrostimulation); Christie Aschwanden *Good to Go* 2019 (Sands: most recovery-center time → least likely to medal in Beijing 2008).

### R-2 — Rest: quality matters; relative > absolute
Not all rest is equal (hammock vs high-pressure sales presentation). Find ≥20 min/day of true relaxation (meditation, reading, journaling, bath, pleasant conversation, calming music). Prefer relative rest (recovery weeks) to lean too heavily on absolute rest.
- tags: type:recovery level:general-population subtopic:rest
- [SRC: Run Like a Pro | 2022 | Ch.7]
- [EVIDENCE: study] Brunel Univ (calming music post-cycling drastically reduced stress hormones).

### R-3 — Sleep
Increased training load raises sleep need (no universal formula; assess subjectively — refreshed on waking, not sleepy until bedtime = enough). Pros sleep a lot (Matt Llano 9–10 hrs night + 1–2 hr nap; napping onset for Fitzgerald ~85 mi/wk). Consequences of deprivation are severe.
- Sleep routine tips: consistent bed/wake times (incl. weekends); dark/quiet/comfortable room; relaxing pre-bed ritual; no screens in bedroom; avoid late exercise/alcohol/caffeine if they interfere; consider melatonin/GABA/valerian/CBD/kava/passionflower/glycine/chamomile/5-HTP/magnesium (research first).
- tags: type:recovery level:general-population subtopic:sleep
- [SRC: Run Like a Pro | 2022 | Ch.7]
- [EVIDENCE: study] Tunisian, *Physiology & Behavior*, 2020 (8→4 hrs one night → ↑perceived effort, ↓self-selected pace).

### R-4 — Stress management (allostatic load)
Burnout is caused by total stress, not just training. Manage via thinking style (CBT-like), run/life balance, sport psychology, meditation, pets, creativity, doing nice things for others, socializing/games. "A happy runner is a fit runner." Seek professional help when needed.
- tags: type:recovery level:general-population subtopic:stress-management
- [SRC: Run Like a Pro | 2022 | Ch.7]
- [EVIDENCE: clinical-trial] Perna et al., *Annals of Behavioral Medicine*, 2003 (CBT in college rowers → ~80% fewer illness/injury training days; ↓negative affect & cortisol).

### R-5 — Nutrition for recovery: real food + timing
Real food > supplements for recovery. Timing: don't go too long without food/drink, especially the first ~2 hrs post-run; nutrients (protein/carb/water/fats/antioxidants) drive recovery. Eat before exercise too (fasted first session blunts recovery for a later session). Normal breakfast/lunch/dinner + snacks when hungry generally suffices. Post-run meal composition need not be perfectly scientific (fast-food = formulated recovery products in one study), but consider long-term health.
- tags: type:nutrition type:recovery level:general-population subtopic:nutrient-timing
- [SRC: Run Like a Pro | 2022 | Ch.7]
- [EVIDENCE: study] *Biology of Sport* 2018 (fruit/veg → ↑antioxidant capacity); Univ Malaysia, *Eur J Sport Sci*, 2018 (intermittent fasting/skipping lunch impaired recovery); McCartney et al., *Sports Medicine*, 2017 (adequate carb+water post-workout → +4% later same-day performance); 2015 fast-food vs recovery-product study.

### R-6 — Corrective exercise
Reverses negative effects of running (tight spots), reduces injury risk, improves efficiency; also relaxing. Best individualized by a PT. Universal starter routine (~10 min):
- **Kneeling Hip Flexor Stretch** — kneel R knee, L foot flat 90°, strap around L knee, press hips forward, hold 1:00 each side.
- **IT Band Foam Rolling** — 30 s each side.
- **Isometric Hip Adduction with Foam Roller** — supine, knees bent, roller between knees, press 10 s, relax 5 s, ×10.
- **Single-Leg Balance** — barefoot, eyes closed, 30 s each foot (restart count if you lose balance).
- **Wall Ankle Mobilization** — tap wall with kneecap, heel down, slide back progressively; each leg.
- **Toe Yoga** — lift big toe (others down) 1 s, then reverse 1 s; several reps each foot.
- tags: type:recovery type:protocol level:general-population subtopic:corrective-exercise system:musculoskeletal
- [SRC: Run Like a Pro | 2022 | Ch.7]
- [EVIDENCE: study] Brazilian, *Am J Sports Medicine*, 2020 (foot corrective routine reduced injury rate 41% in 18 runners).

### R-7 — Run/life balance (Coach Ben)
Value recovery but balance with life; sleep first; stress management second; nutrition + corrective exercise close behind. More than any method, it's *respecting* recovery that separates pros.
- tags: type:recovery type:philosophy level:general-population subtopic:life-balance
- [SRC: Run Like a Pro | 2022 | Ch.7 Coach's Tip]

## 13. Adaptations by Population / Context

### AD-1 — Late-starting adult runners
More vulnerable to bad coaching/influences; best practices (80/20, tapering) are non-intuitive and must be learned. Solution: adopt pro principles now regardless of starting age.
- tags: type:methodology level:beginner subtopic:adult-onset
- [SRC: Run Like a Pro | 2022 | Ch.1]

### AD-2 — Beginners: rest and volume
Newer runners may need one+ days off per week to control fatigue; as fitness builds, shift from absolute rest to relative rest for more progress. Begin with alternate-day walk/jog (48-hr recovery).
- tags: type:methodology level:beginner subtopic:beginner-progression
- [SRC: Run Like a Pro | 2022 | Ch.2, Ch.3]

### AD-3 — Injury-prone runners (genetic)
Some are innately more susceptible (joint collagen characteristics). Use cross-training to gain most run-fitness benefit with less impact.
- tags: type:caution level:general-population subtopic:injury-proneness
- [SRC: Run Like a Pro | 2022 | Ch.3]

### AD-4 — Biomechanics-limited pros (mileage individualization)
Lauren Paquette (forefoot striker, high metatarsal pressure) → 65–70 mi/wk; Scott Fauble (efficient strike but big workouts suffer at high mileage) → 100–110 mi/wk; Scott Smith → 110–120 mi/wk. Individualize mileage by foot strike, efficiency, response.
- tags: type:heuristic level:elite subtopic:mileage-individualization
- [SRC: Run Like a Pro | 2022 | Ch.3 Coach's Tip]
- [EVIDENCE: case-study]

### AD-5 — Allergies/intolerances & clinical conditions
Steph Bruce: celiac + dairy/soy/egg allergies → eat diversely within constraints; see a physician for symptoms hurting running. Iron deficiency (low ferritin) → iron supplement + iron-rich foods (red meat). Don't just live with symptoms that could signal food allergy/intolerance.
- tags: type:nutrition type:caution level:elite subtopic:medical-nutrition
- [SRC: Run Like a Pro | 2022 | Ch.8, Ch.8 Coach's Tip]
- [EVIDENCE: case-study]

### AD-6 — Masters/aging runners
Running economy is preserved with age even as VO2max/strength/power decline; pros often PR in their 30s. Drills, plyos, and strength support longevity ("getting faster with age" — Bruce, Taylor at NAZ Elite).
- tags: type:physiology level:masters subtopic:aging-runner
- [SRC: Run Like a Pro | 2022 | Ch.3, Ch.6]
- [EVIDENCE: study] Univ New Hampshire 2011.

### AD-7 — Ultramarathon specifics
Ultras (50K–100mi) are increasingly competitive. Human body can't adapt to runs >~5 hrs; cap long runs at 5:00 even for 100-milers. Train on race-similar terrain; measure by vert; expect walking/hiking on steep climbs; use back-to-back long runs.
- tags: type:methodology level:advanced subtopic:ultramarathon
- [SRC: Run Like a Pro | 2022 | Ch.14]
- [EVIDENCE: author-opinion / case-study]

### AD-8 — Runners not chasing PRs
Chasing PRs isn't the only valid reason to race. Make a conscious choice: race sparingly & successfully (like pros) OR race often with less peak performance. Don't try to have it both ways.
- tags: type:philosophy level:general-population subtopic:race-goals
- [SRC: Run Like a Pro | 2022 | Ch.2]

## 14. Common Mistakes, Contraindications & Cautions

### C-1 — Over-racing
Common nonelite mistake; disrupts training flow (~1 lost week per race). Excuses ("need to race to know fitness," "I choke with too much focus," "I overtrain without racing") are just excuses.
- tags: type:caution level:general-population subtopic:racing-frequency
- [SRC: Run Like a Pro | 2022 | Ch.2]

### C-2 — Moderate-intensity rut
Doing far less low-intensity work than pros (easy runs above VT1); biggest nonelite training mistake. Second-biggest: not enough high intensity.
- tags: type:caution level:general-population subtopic:intensity-distribution
- [SRC: Run Like a Pro | 2022 | Ch.4]
- [EVIDENCE: study] Gilman 1993 (<half of weekly time at low intensity in female recreational runners); Tanda (Univ Genova, recreational marathoners avg 7:39/mi training, ~58 s/mi above low-intensity limit).

### C-3 — Increasing mileage too abruptly
Even high-mileage runners get injured when acute load exceeds chronic by >10%. Build gradually then hold.
- tags: type:caution level:general-population subtopic:injury-prevention metric:load
- [SRC: Run Like a Pro | 2022 | Ch.3]

### C-4 — Being a slave to round numbers
Chasing 100-mi weeks / arbitrary totals despite body warnings → injury. (Fitzgerald injured chasing first 100-mi week when sweet spot was high-80s/low-90s.)
- tags: type:caution level:general-population subtopic:mileage
- [SRC: Run Like a Pro | 2022 | Ch.3]

### C-5 — Low-carb / keto diets for endurance
Well established as harmful to endurance performance (reduces carb-burning capacity, VO2max at speed); highly restrictive; risks disordered eating; possible long-term health risk. Not recommended for runners.
- tags: type:caution type:nutrition level:general-population subtopic:low-carb
- [SRC: Run Like a Pro | 2022 | Ch.8]
- [EVIDENCE: clinical-trial] Burke et al. (AIS); Seidelmann ARIC study.

### C-6 — Undereating / RED-S
Undereating consequences (low energy, muscle loss, reduced performance/recovery, lower metabolic rate, attenuated gains, ↑injury/illness) are more severe for runners than overeating. Within-day energy deficits (≥2 hrs) suppress resting metabolic rate even with matched 24-hr intake; ↑cortisol, ↓testosterone. Fuel throughout the day.
- tags: type:caution type:nutrition level:general-population subtopic:red-s
- [SRC: Run Like a Pro | 2022 | Ch.8]
- [EVIDENCE: study] Univ Agder/Copenhagen 2018; case examples Molly Seidel (stress fractures), Elise Cranny (bone injuries, amenorrhea).

### C-7 — Technique instruction / consciously altering natural stride
Consciously altering natural stride in *any* way tends to worsen running (increased mental effort reduces economy). Thinking about movements while running reduces economy. Copying elite form does not make you faster. Self-consciousness is the enemy of efficiency.
- tags: type:caution level:general-population subtopic:running-form system:neuromuscular
- [SRC: Run Like a Pro | 2022 | Ch.6]
- [EVIDENCE: study] Dallam (CSU Pueblo) 2005 (12 wks POSE Method → reduced economy); Schücker (Münster) series; Cochrum (MTSU), *Eur J Sport Science*, 2020 (only 6% of 121 coaches could rank 3/5 runners by tested economy from form).

### C-8 — Fading in long runs
Training yourself to hit the wall; running after involuntary slowing offers no benefit, increases recovery needs and injury risk (form breakdown). ~80% of runners hit the wall in marathons, mostly from poor pacing. Fix with Fastest Mile Last Rule.
- tags: type:caution level:general-population subtopic:pacing metric:pace
- [SRC: Run Like a Pro | 2022 | Ch.5]

### C-9 — Strength-training don'ts for runners
Avoid bodybuilding-type upper-body bulk (e.g., bench press) and machine exercises with low functional carryover (e.g., machine leg curls). Cap at 2–3 full-body sessions/wk (more interferes with running).
- tags: type:caution type:strength-training level:general-population subtopic:strength-training system:neuromuscular
- [SRC: Run Like a Pro | 2022 | Ch.6]

### C-10 — Over-tapering vs under-tapering (learn the right lesson)
Rosario's Twin Cities success and Grandma's failure: don't fit findings to a self-affirming narrative. A too-mild taper (grinding a 90-mi week 2 wks out while already exhausted) can wreck a marathon. Listen to the body.
- tags: type:caution level:general-population subtopic:tapering
- [SRC: Run Like a Pro | 2022 | Ch.2 Coach's Tip]
- [EVIDENCE: case-study]

### C-11 — Recovery supplements/machines
Electrostimulation and similar high-tech recovery devices largely function as placebos; not where recovery focus should be.
- tags: type:caution type:recovery level:general-population subtopic:recovery-gadgets
- [SRC: Run Like a Pro | 2022 | Ch.7]

## 15. Formulas & Calculations

### F-1 — VT1 pace from Six-Minute Test
VT1 pace ≈ 65% of MAS. Steps: distance in 6:00 → avg pace → convert to speed → ×0.65 → convert back to pace. (Also 60–65% MAS range.)
- Variables: MAS = max aerobic speed (6-min max pace/speed).
- Example: MAS 10.9 mph → ×0.65 = 7.09 mph = 8:27/mi.
- tags: type:formula level:general-population subtopic:vt1 metric:pace
- [SRC: Run Like a Pro | 2022 | Ch.4]

### F-2 — VT1 / VT2 heart-rate ranges
VT1 HR = 77–81% × HRmax; VT2 HR = 91–93% × HRmax (fitter → top end). Example HRmax 185 → VT1 142–150 bpm.
- tags: type:formula level:general-population subtopic:ventilatory-threshold metric:hr
- [SRC: Run Like a Pro | 2022 | Ch.4]

### F-3 — VT2 pace from MAS
VT2 pace ≈ 84–87% of MAS.
- tags: type:formula level:advanced subtopic:vt2 metric:pace
- [SRC: Run Like a Pro | 2022 | Ch.4]

### F-4 — 80/20 percentage calc (example from Table 4.1)
% low intensity = (minutes at low intensity ÷ total minutes) × 100 = 310 ÷ 385 = 80.5%.
- tags: type:formula level:general-population subtopic:80-20 metric:load
- [SRC: Run Like a Pro | 2022 | Ch.4]

### F-5 — Badger mile
1 Badger mile = 7 minutes (time-based volume proxy; Jerry Schumacher / Univ Wisconsin).
- tags: type:formula level:general-population subtopic:time-based-training
- [SRC: Run Like a Pro | 2022 | Ch.3]

### F-6 — Acute:Chronic load target
Keep ATL (past ~1 wk volume+intensity) at 101–110% of CTL (past ~4 wk volume+intensity). Injury spike when ATL > CTL by >10%.
- tags: type:formula level:intermediate subtopic:training-load metric:load
- [SRC: Run Like a Pro | 2022 | Ch.3, Ch.7]

### F-7 — Pace-target rep example (5K sub-20 sharpener)
Goal 20:00 5K → 4:00/km. Workout: 5 × 1K @ goal pace (4:00/km). 10K sub-40 → 6:26/mi → 0.75-mi rep target 4:04.5.
- tags: type:formula level:general-population subtopic:race-pace metric:pace
- [SRC: Run Like a Pro | 2022 | Ch.5]

### F-8 — Relaxed 10K TT pace
Target = 10K race pace + 5% (used as a fitness check in marathon/ultra plans).
- tags: type:formula type:test level:general-population subtopic:time-trial metric:pace
- [SRC: Run Like a Pro | 2022 | Marathon/Ultra plans]

### F-9 — 300 m cutdown pacing (Coach Ben)
Start from mile pace = target for rep #3; rep #1 two seconds slower; cut down each rep. Reps <600 m need no altitude conversion. Example (mile ~4:30 → 50 s/300): 52, 51, 50, 49.
- tags: type:formula level:advanced subtopic:speed-work metric:pace
- [SRC: Run Like a Pro | 2022 | Ch.11 Coach's Tip]

## 2. Philosophy & Training Principles (TIER A)

**P-1 — Purpose of training**
Training exists to improve performance (run faster, farther, longer). When improvement is no longer possible (age, exhausted potential), the goal shifts to maintaining current level / minimizing decline.
- tags: type:philosophy general
- [SRC: Ultramarathon Training | 2012 | Ch.6.1]

**P-2 — Ultra training is not radically different from marathon training**
Moving up to ultra distances requires the same *kinds* of adjustments as moving between shorter distances. The core departure: as distance increases, workouts become longer and slower; and (uniquely vs. traditional distance running) basic speed is developed in the *preparation* period, before very long distances are introduced.
- tags: type:philosophy type:methodology general
- [SRC: Ultramarathon Training | 2012 | Ch.8, Ch.6.3]

**P-3 — Deliberate scope limit (no elite prescriptions)**
Author intentionally excludes elite-level training plans. Belief: at elite level, universal advice is meaningless; a serious coach–athlete dialogue is required. Direct collaboration with an experienced ultra coach is recommended. Assumes readers already have years of running experience and basic training theory.
- tags: type:philosophy level:elite type:caution general
- [SRC: Ultramarathon Training | 2012 | Introduction, Ch.17.1]

**P-4 — Mental strength scales with distance (signature model)**
Marathon ≈ 80% legs / 20% head. 24-hour race ≈ 50/50. Beyond 24 h, mental strength exceeds 50%. First-class physical prep is still non-negotiable ("even the strongest mind cannot achieve much without a fit body").
- tags: type:philosophy phase:race general
- [SRC: Ultramarathon Training | 2012 | Ch.15 / cover]

**P-5 — "We control circumstances; they don't control us" (Goethe)**
Core mental stance: the length of ultra racing exposes the runner to far more uncertainty than a marathon (weather swings, GI issues, fueling, low points). The athlete must pre-plan a coping solution for every foreseeable problem rather than react in the moment.
- tags: type:philosophy type:heuristic phase:race general
- [SRC: Ultramarathon Training | 2012 | Ch.15]

**P-6 — Establish current fitness before planning**
"It only makes sense to draw up a training plan once you have established your current state of fitness." Analyze prior training and race performances; ideally get a neutral experienced coach/sports scientist — especially if performance has plateaued.
- tags: type:methodology type:heuristic general
- [SRC: Ultramarathon Training | 2012 | Ch.6.1]

**P-7 — Heart rate is the author's preferred management tool**
"Heart rate monitor training is the simplest and most effective way for runners to plan structured and targeted training… the heart rate doesn't lie!" Preferred over pace because daily conditions (terrain, temperature, weather) vary.
- tags: type:philosophy metric:hr type:methodology general
- [SRC: Ultramarathon Training | 2012 | Ch.7, Ch.7.2.2]

**P-8 — Individualization increases with distance**
Beyond 24 h there is no universal plan. "Blanket recommendations are useless — it is up to the individual to find out what works for him." Two philosophical "camps" of 24-h training coexist (see M-6).
- tags: type:philosophy phase:race level:advanced general
- [SRC: Ultramarathon Training | 2012 | Ch.8, Ch.17.12]

## 3. Physiology Foundations (TIER A)

**PH-1 — Fat metabolism is central to ultra**
Slow, long, low-intensity ultra sessions (often 4–6 h, sometimes 8+) draw up to ~70% of energy from fat. Trained endurance athletes oxidize more fat at a given intensity than untrained. Long recovery jogs specifically train fat metabolism and are the race pace for 24-h/multi-day races.
- tags: system:fatoxidation system:aerobic type:physiology general
- [SRC: Ultramarathon Training | 2012 | Ch.6.2.1, Ch.12.1]

**PH-2 — Lactate threshold concept**
Lactic acid forms during activity; above ~4 mmol/l it can no longer be broken down, causing a sudden performance drop. This concentration varies individually and must be measured per runner (see tests, Section 6).
- tags: system:lactate system:threshold type:physiology metric:hr
- [SRC: Ultramarathon Training | 2012 | Ch.7.1.1]

**PH-3 — Supercompensation model**
Performance improvement comes from correctly timed/intensity training stimuli. Correct spacing → linear improvement. Stimuli too close/too intense → overtraining (performance drop). Stimuli too far apart → no improvement/decline.
- tags: type:physiology type:methodology load
- [SRC: Ultramarathon Training | 2012 | Ch.6.4]

**PH-4 — Training frequency floor (from supercompensation)**
Stimuli should be no more than 4 days apart → train at least 2×/week to maintain a minimum level; performance only *improves* with ≥3×/week. Novices/detrained improve briefly on 2×/week; trained endurance runners need ≥3×/week depending on fitness.
- tags: type:physiology type:heuristic load
- [SRC: Ultramarathon Training | 2012 | Ch.6.4]

**PH-5 — Downhill running is the true limiter in mountain ultra**
Running downhill is the performance-limiting factor in mountain ultrarunning; shock load on muscles/joints/tendons is huge (bodyweight multiplied). Muscles must be prepared beforehand; keep the flight phase short so bodyweight is cushioned. Untrained downhill muscles can force premature retirement.
- tags: sport:running phase:race system:neuromuscular type:physiology type:caution
- [SRC: Ultramarathon Training | 2012 | Ch.18]

**PH-6 — Upright posture protects breathing on climbs**
Bending/leaning forward when climbing reduces diaphragm and lung function → less oxygen intake → worse performance, especially at altitude. Keep upper body ~90° to ground where slope allows.
- tags: sport:running type:physiology type:heuristic
- [SRC: Ultramarathon Training | 2012 | Ch.18]

**PH-7 — Altitude acclimatization**
If race altitude is unfamiliar (~6,000 ft), acclimatize beforehand. Full acclimatization takes 2–3 weeks; if unrealistic, allow ≥1 week. If time-at-altitude in the race is short (e.g., UTMB drops below 6,000 ft quickly), it can be managed by appropriate pacing on those sections.
- tags: sport:running phase:prep type:physiology type:heuristic
- [SRC: Ultramarathon Training | 2012 | Ch.18]

## 4. Glossary — Author's Definitions (TIER A)

**G-1 — Ultramarathon:** "Any race that exceeds the length of a marathon (26.2 miles)." Author personally judges by *course length* (constant, measurable), irrespective of rest breaks taken within race rules.
- tags: type:glossary sport:running
- [SRC: Ultramarathon Training | 2012 | Ch.1]

**G-2 — Long recovery jog / long jog:** endurance run at <70% HRmax (stated elsewhere 65–70% HRmax). Trains fat metabolism; = race pace for 24-h and multi-day races.
- tags: type:glossary system:fatoxidation metric:hr
- [SRC: Ultramarathon Training | 2012 | Ch.6.2.1, Ch.17.2]

**G-3 — Extensive endurance run:** 75–80% HRmax (text also says "75–80% of maximal effort"). Used for medium–long workouts; = race pace for 100 km.
- tags: type:glossary system:aerobic metric:hr
- [SRC: Ultramarathon Training | 2012 | Ch.6.2.2, Ch.17.2]

**G-4 — Intensive endurance run:** 80–85% HRmax. Increases basic speed; usually once/week in ultra training.
- tags: type:glossary system:threshold metric:hr
- [SRC: Ultramarathon Training | 2012 | Ch.6.2.3, Ch.17.2]

**G-5 — Tempo endurance run:** 85–90% HRmax (defined in Ch.17.2 training-zone table).
- tags: type:glossary system:threshold metric:hr
- [SRC: Ultramarathon Training | 2012 | Ch.17.2]

**G-6 — Fartlek ("speed play," Swedish):** varying running tempo, ideally hilly (uphill = higher effort, downhill = recovery), pace kept as even as possible. Effort range 65–90% HRmax.
- tags: type:glossary system:aerobic metric:hr
- [SRC: Ultramarathon Training | 2012 | Ch.6.2.4, Ch.17.2]

**G-7 — Interval training:** intervals of varying lengths with jog breaks; very intensive, usually above the anaerobic threshold (≥90% of max effort). Interval lengths 200 m–5 km; jog recovery 50–100% of interval distance. Example (elite 100 km): 20 × 1 km with 200 m jog, splits ≤3:30 min/km.
- tags: type:glossary system:vo2max system:anaerobic metric:hr
- [SRC: Ultramarathon Training | 2012 | Ch.6.2.5]

**G-8 — Hill running:** running uphill over the training distance as fast as possible; short, high pulse; ideal course entirely uphill (uphill sections ideally ≥500 m at max speed).
- tags: type:glossary system:vo2max system:neuromuscular
- [SRC: Ultramarathon Training | 2012 | Ch.6.2.6]

**G-9 — Pyramid running:** jog start, then e.g. 3 min fast / 2 min jog / 5 min fast / 2 min jog / 7 min fast / 2 min jog, then back down the pyramid.
- tags: type:glossary system:threshold
- [SRC: Ultramarathon Training | 2012 | Ch.6.2.7]

**G-10 — Crescendo running:** progressively increasing tempo of longer sections; tempo raised every 10–15 min until 10 km race pace in final phase. Start ≈ long recovery run pace. Length depends on condition. Jog ≥2 km after final fast phase.
- tags: type:glossary system:threshold
- [SRC: Ultramarathon Training | 2012 | Ch.6.2.8]

**G-11 — "Ultra foot slap":** (author's term) a gait fault avoided by retaining some interval/tempo work in 24-h training.
- tags: type:glossary sport:running
- [SRC: Ultramarathon Training | 2012 | Ch.8]

**G-12 — "Double-decker":** author's shorthand for the ability to run 2 marathons/ultras on a weekend — a prerequisite fitness level for multi-day race training.
- tags: type:glossary level:advanced phase:transition
- [SRC: Ultramarathon Training | 2012 | Ch.17.13]

**G-13 — "Bonk":** running out of fuel due to inadequate food intake during a race.
- tags: type:glossary type:nutrition phase:race
- [SRC: Ultramarathon Training | 2012 | Ch.15]

## 6. Testing & Assessment Protocols (TIER B)

**T-1 — Lactic acid test / field test**
- Purpose: determine endurance ability and aerobic/anaerobic threshold to set training zones.
- Setup: treadmill preferred (accurate speed); on-track version = "field test."
- Protocol: start at a slow speed per ability; for ultrarunners increase pace by **0.5 m/s every 5 minutes**. After each stage take a blood drop (usually earlobe), test for lactate. End when athlete cannot complete a stage (ideal = stage test to exhaustion). Tester may stop early if threshold judged reached, but exhaustion is ideal.
- Cautions: sweat contamination and wrong blood volume distort results. Cross-check with a Conconi test; if significant discrepancy, repeat.
- tags: sport:running system:lactate metric:hr type:test
- [SRC: Ultramarathon Training | 2012 | Ch.7.1.1]

**T-2 — Spiro-ergometric (VO2max) test**
- Gradual load increase, sport-specific (running/treadmill; mobile kit allows field testing).
- Measures HR, lactate, and analyzes exhaled breath via mask → VO2max, metabolism, respiratory/cardiovascular data; used to calculate individual training zones.
- Stage duration ~5 min for best measurements; a short, sharply increasing test needed to establish absolute VO2max.
- Less error-prone than blood-based lactate test. Elite: performed at start of training block and repeated at intervals to monitor progress and adapt zones.
- tags: sport:running system:vo2max metric:hr type:test level:elite
- [SRC: Ultramarathon Training | 2012 | Ch.7.1.2]

**T-3 — Conconi test (author's cost-effective favorite for regular monitoring)**
- Establishes aerobic/anaerobic threshold without blood sampling (developed by Francesco Conconi).
- Principle: HR rises linearly in aerobic zone; curve flattens (deflection point) at threshold. For accuracy take **8 measurements in the aerobic zone and 3 in the anaerobic zone**.
- Protocol: on a 400 m track with a second person (ideally coach). After a gentle warm-up jog, **increase tempo by 0.5 km/h every 200 m** (log in tempo table). Measure HR after each 200 m stage (click HR monitor). Continue until athlete cannot hold the set pace over 200 m.
- Analysis: plot in Excel; the bend in the curve = aerobic/anaerobic threshold (worked example: deflection at **169 bpm**). A higher threshold speed on retest indicates progress.
- Frequency guidance: use regularly to monitor; a spiro-ergometry test once/year suffices for comparison for ambitious ultrarunners.
- tags: sport:running system:threshold metric:hr type:test
- [SRC: Ultramarathon Training | 2012 | Ch.7.1.3]

**T-4 — Establishing maximal heart rate (HRmax)**
- Do-it-yourself, on track, with ≥1 other person. Prereqs: good basic fitness, running experience, perfect health.
- Protocol: jog ~2 km warm-up (better 3 km to avoid soreness); run **3000 m at fastest even pace you can, at least faster than 5 km race pace**; then increase speed to exhaustion (final spurt where speed can no longer be increased). HR reading at that moment = HRmax.
- Use: set HRmax as 100% loading; derive % increments for plans (more reliable than pace given daily condition variance).
- tags: sport:running metric:hr type:test type:formula
- [SRC: Ultramarathon Training | 2012 | Ch.7.2.2]

**T-5 — HRV / "Own Zone" (POLAR)**
- Alternative near-accurate zone method: measure heart rate variability. POLAR's "Own Zone" lets a runner determine individual HR limits in ~2.5 minutes and structure training on them.
- tags: metric:hrv metric:hr type:test type:methodology
- [SRC: Ultramarathon Training | 2012 | Ch.7.2.1]

**T-6 — Resting heart rate as fitness benchmark**
- Measure pulse in the morning before rising. Should drop as fitness improves. Combined with a training diary (lower HR at same speed/conditions over time), it indicates efficient training and helps set training/race pace to avoid overtraining.
- tags: metric:hr type:test type:heuristic
- [SRC: Ultramarathon Training | 2012 | Ch.17.3]

**T-7 — Monitoring races (for tempo-oriented / non-HR athletes)**
- If training purely by time, monitor progress with shorter races: half-marathon and 10 km for frequent checks; for 100 km+ goals use marathon or 50 km monitoring races (allow adequate recovery afterward).
- Pre-race medical: before starting any plan, get a medical check including at minimum a **stress ECG and an echocardiogram**.
- tags: type:test type:caution phase:build
- [SRC: Ultramarathon Training | 2012 | Ch.7.3, Ch.17.3, Ch.17.13]

## 7. Methodology & Periodization (TIER A logic, TIER B numbers)

**M-1 — Periodization necessity & peak counts**
Ultra runners cannot peak year-round → periodize. Elite ultra runners typically plan **2–3 peaks/year** regardless of distance (100 km, 24 h, longer).
- tags: type:methodology phase:base phase:peak
- [SRC: Ultramarathon Training | 2012 | Ch.6.3]

**M-2 — Three phases per cycle**
1. **Preparation period** — focus basic endurance; foundations for fitness. Starts very slow, slow continual increase in content; later stages add basic-speed work. Length: ~4 weeks in a 3-peak year; up to 3 months in a 2-peak year.
2. **Competition period** — includes special (race-specific) prep, fundamentally different from prep-phase training; ultra special training runs **6–8 weeks**. Culminates in the race.
3. **Recovery/transition period** — 2–4 weeks depending on athlete. Active recovery in *other* sports (cycling, swimming, easy jogging); no activity above 70% HRmax.
- tags: type:methodology phase:prep phase:build phase:peak phase:transition metric:hr
- [SRC: Ultramarathon Training | 2012 | Ch.6.3]

**M-3 — Signature reversal: basic speed built in preparation**
Opposite to traditional distance plans (which do little speed work early): ultra prep does tempo work over shorter distances (10 km, half-marathon) to raise basic speed *before* embarking on very long training distances.
- tags: type:methodology phase:prep system:threshold
- [SRC: Ultramarathon Training | 2012 | Ch.6.3, Ch.16]

**M-4 — Test/preparation races within the cycle**
Ultra prep races can be longer than for marathon: a 100 km runner races a marathon; a 24-h runner races a 100 km or 12-h; a multi-day racer races a 24-h or a stage race. Unlike marathon test races (run as fast as possible), ultra test races are run slower — the longer the target race, the slower the test.
- tags: type:methodology phase:build type:test
- [SRC: Ultramarathon Training | 2012 | Ch.6.3]

**M-5 — Basic-training structure between plans (base building)**
Between the "final" race-specific plans, do basic training that already points toward the future race. For fun→ambitious ultrarunners: **increase volume/intensity for 3 weeks, then a recovery week (4th week ≈ half the previous week's volume).** Author prefers an 8-week race-specific plan rhythm. Basic-training weekly volume ≈ **75–80% of the special-preparation phase volume.** When ~80% of the special plan is reached, begin special preparation; if reached much sooner, raise goals or keep improving short-distance times. Do **not** race-train the short distances — do fast runs to build basic speed; avoid overtraining/injury.
- tags: type:methodology phase:base load
- [SRC: Ultramarathon Training | 2012 | Ch.16]

**M-6 — 24-hour training: two camps (methodological choice)**
No universal 24-h plan. Camp A: train as for 100 km but extend long runs (to 80 or even 100 km). Camp B: high mileage on 3–4 consecutive days. Neither drops tempo entirely (replace intervals with a longer run). Long weekend runs at target race pace. Common thread across all levels: high weekend mileage (e.g., 100–150 miles across a weekend in slow daily stages). Multi-day (5-day) races can be folded into this. Retain some tempo (long fartleks especially) to avoid "ultra foot slap." Example elite variants: Michael Irrgang — 40 × 200 m w/ 200 m jog (à la Zátopek); others — 3 × 10 km intervals on track.
- tags: type:methodology phase:build sport:running level:advanced level:elite
- [SRC: Ultramarathon Training | 2012 | Ch.8, Ch.17.12]

**M-7 — How workouts change moving up in distance (rules)**
- Marathon → 50 km: essentially a marathon plan; extend long workouts to 40–42 km; other workouts unchanged (per target). First 50 km attempt ≈ **10–15 s/km slower than marathon pace**.
- Marathon → 100 km: greater changes. Tempo workouts become longer & slower. Intervals lengthen (marathon uses 400 m & 1 km → 100 km uses up to **4 × 5 km**). Tempo endurance run over 30 km+ (≈ former marathon race tempo). Long weekend runs **35–70 km**.
- Elite examples: 20 × 1 km w/ 200 m jog, faster than 10 km best time.
- tags: type:methodology sport:running phase:build system:threshold
- [SRC: Ultramarathon Training | 2012 | Ch.8]

**M-8 — Workout structure (3 parts)**
Introduction (warm-up: slow jog, may include running drills, ball/team games) → Main section (specific workout) → Conclusion (warm-down). Per current sports science, **stretch in the conclusion, not the warm-up** — stretching before load has no proven benefit.
- tags: type:methodology sport:running
- [SRC: Ultramarathon Training | 2012 | Ch.6.5]

**M-9 — Tempo-oriented (time-based) training**
Plan purely from required workout times derived from target race pace. Risk: trying to hit preset times can cause overtraining → monitoring essential (use monitoring races, T-7).
- tags: type:methodology metric:pace type:caution
- [SRC: Ultramarathon Training | 2012 | Ch.7.3]

**M-10 — Trail/mountain-specific preparation logic**
Only needed if course is uneven AND hilly. Orient training to race conditions: study the elevation profile, footing, weather by altitude/time; contact a *finisher* for course intel; read DNF reports to pre-empt failure causes (link to mental prep). If you don't live in mountains, substitute one weekly session of hill reps (5–15% grade, ~500 m long, run up & down) or **step running** in a high-rise (≥5 floors). Replace the interval session with the mountain/step session. Practice both uphill (upright posture, PH-6) and downhill (short flight phase, PH-5). Run on race-similar surface ≥1×/week (park verges, sand, tractor tracks, bridle paths).
- tags: sport:running phase:build type:methodology system:neuromuscular
- [SRC: Ultramarathon Training | 2012 | Ch.18]

## 8. Training Plans (TIER B)

> Common conventions across plans: all use %HRmax anchors (see Z-1). "Cross training" = cycling, ergometer, swimming at recovery pace. Strengthening ~20–30 min (Ch.10) covering all muscle groups; running drills ≥6 (Ch.11); flexibility/stretching after extensive/recovery runs (Ch.9). Medical check (stress ECG + echocardiogram) required before starting. Prereq: several years of marathon training and basic conditioning completed.

### Plan Index & Prerequisites

**PL-1 — 50 km entry level**
- Prereq: current marathon ~5:00 h. 3–4 days/wk, 4–9 h/wk. Weekly mileage 40–87 km. Total incl. race 490 km. 8 weeks (3 build weeks + recovery week pattern, taper wks 7–8).
- Paces: extensive endurance @7:10–7:15 min/km (75–80% HRmax); intervals @6:30 min/km (up to 90% HRmax).
- Signature workouts: interval progression 4×1 km → 2×2 km → 2×3 km → 3×2 km → 3×3 km (2 km warm-up/down; jog recovery 600 m–2 km); long runs building 25→30→35→37→42 km.
- Race day (Wk8 Sun): 50 km @7:20 min/km = 6:06:40 h. Author's note: at 7:10 min/km → 5:58:20.
- tags: sport:running phase:build type:plan level:beginner metric:hr
- [SRC: Ultramarathon Training | 2012 | Ch.17.5]

**PL-2 — 50 km sub-5:00 (6:00 min/km)**
- Prereq: marathon ~4:00 h. 3–5 days/wk, 5–10 h/wk. Mileage 49–89 km. Total incl. race 540 km. 8 wks.
- Paces: intensive @5:35 (80–85%); tempo @5:20 (85–90%); extensive @5:50 (75–80%); intervals @5:10 (>90%).
- Race day: 50 km @6:00 min/km = 5:00:00. Author would run 5:50 splits → 4:51:40.
- tags: sport:running phase:build type:plan level:beginner metric:hr
- [SRC: Ultramarathon Training | 2012 | Ch.17.6]

**PL-3 — 50 km sub-4:30 (5:24 min/km)**
- Prereq: marathon 3:30–3:40 h. 4–5 days/wk, 6–10 h/wk. Mileage 71–108 km. Total 694.4 km. Stretch 3×/wk.
- Paces: intensive @5:05 (80–85%); tempo @4:55 (85–90%); extensive @5:20 (75–80%); intervals @4:45 (>90%).
- Race day: 50 km @5:24 = 4:30:00. Author would run 5:20 splits → 4:26:40.
- tags: sport:running phase:build type:plan level:intermediate metric:hr
- [SRC: Ultramarathon Training | 2012 | Ch.17.7]

**PL-4 — 50 km sub-4:00 (4:48 min/km) — threshold-raced**
- IMPORTANT distinction: unlike PL-1–3, the race is NOT run at extensive pace — it is run at the aerobic/anaerobic threshold, like a marathon. Requires accurate self-assessment, HR watch, good sleep, low stress.
- Prereq: marathon 3:15 h. 5 days/wk, 7–11 h/wk. Mileage 75–124 km. Total 759.6 km. Strength ≥30 min; stretch 3×/wk.
- Paces: intensive @4:45 (80–85%); tempo @4:35 (85–90%); extensive @4:55 (75–80%); intervals @4:15 (>90%); recovery @5:30 (up to 70%).
- Signature: intervals up to 3×5 km; long runs to 42 km with 5–15 km final acceleration at/≤5 s/km faster than race pace.
- Race day: 50 km @4:48 min/km @80% HRmax.
- tags: sport:running phase:build type:plan level:advanced system:threshold metric:hr
- [SRC: Ultramarathon Training | 2012 | Ch.17.8]

**PL-5 — 100 km sub-11 h (6:36 min/km)**
- Prereq: marathon 3:44 h. 4–5 days/wk, 7–15 h/wk. Mileage 68–131 km. Total 754 km.
- Paces: intensive @5:55 (80–85%); tempo @5:42 (85–90%); extensive @6:30 (75–80%); intervals @5:30 (>90%).
- Signature: intervals to 3×3 km; long runs 30→50 km; option "training ultramarathon over 50 km at extensive pace."
- Race day: 100 km @6:35 min/km = 10:58:20. Author: 6:30 splits → 10:50.
- tags: sport:running phase:build type:plan level:intermediate metric:hr
- [SRC: Ultramarathon Training | 2012 | Ch.17.9]

**PL-6 — 100 km sub-10 h (6:00 min/km)**

## 9. Individual Workouts Library (TIER B)

**W-1 — Long recovery jog (long jog)**
- Goal: train fat metabolism; = 24-h / multi-day race pace.
- Structure: continuous run @ <70% HRmax (65–70% per Ch.6). Example: 3 miles @70% HRmax.
- tags: sport:running system:fatoxidation metric:hr type:workout phase:base
- [SRC: Ultramarathon Training | 2012 | Ch.6.2.1]

**W-2 — Extensive endurance run**
- Goal: medium–long aerobic development; = 100 km race pace.
- Structure: continuous @75–80% HRmax. Example: 15 km, slightly undulating terrain.
- tags: sport:running system:aerobic metric:hr type:workout
- [SRC: Ultramarathon Training | 2012 | Ch.6.2.2]

**W-3 — Intensive endurance run**
- Goal: increase basic speed.
- Structure: continuous @80–85% HRmax; ~1×/week. Example: 10 km tempo run.
- tags: sport:running system:threshold metric:hr type:workout
- [SRC: Ultramarathon Training | 2012 | Ch.6.2.3]

**W-4 — Tempo endurance run**
- Goal: threshold development.
- Structure: continuous @85–90% HRmax.
- tags: sport:running system:threshold metric:hr type:workout
- [SRC: Ultramarathon Training | 2012 | Ch.17.2]

**W-5 — Fartlek**
- Goal: varied load; trail-friendly.
- Structure: vary tempo, ideally hilly; effort 65–90% HRmax (uphill harder, downhill recovery). In plans, "≥3 different tempos."
- tags: sport:running system:aerobic system:threshold metric:hr type:workout
- [SRC: Ultramarathon Training | 2012 | Ch.6.2.4]

**W-6 — Interval training**
- Goal: high-intensity tempo, above anaerobic threshold.
- Structure: intervals 200 m–5 km @ ≥90% max; jog recovery 50–100% of interval distance; jog warm-up/warm-down. Elite 100 km example: 20 × 1 km w/ 200 m jog, ≤3:30 min/km.
- tags: sport:running system:vo2max system:anaerobic metric:hr type:workout
- [SRC: Ultramarathon Training | 2012 | Ch.6.2.5]

**W-7 — Hill running**
- Goal: strength/power; high pulse.
- Structure: uphill as fast as possible; ideally fully uphill course; uphill sections ≥500 m at max speed.
- tags: sport:running system:vo2max system:neuromuscular type:workout
- [SRC: Ultramarathon Training | 2012 | Ch.6.2.6]

**W-8 — Uphill reps (trail plans)**
- Structure: 2 km warm-up jog; 6–25 × 200 m uphill runs (up to 90% HRmax), jog back 200 m; 2 km warm-down jog.
- Progression: PL-11 6→18; PL-12 14→25.
- tags: sport:running system:neuromuscular metric:hr type:workout phase:build
- [SRC: Ultramarathon Training | 2012 | Ch.18]

**W-9 — Step running (high-rise stairs) — author's UTMB substitute for intervals**
- Goal: prepare muscles for mountain up/down, esp. downhill loading.
- Structure: 2 km warm-up jog; 30–120 min step running (HR significantly >90% HRmax); ≥2 km warm-down jog. Tackle each step individually going up AND down.
- Progression: PL-11 20→80 min; PL-12 60→120 min.
- tags: sport:running system:neuromuscular metric:hr type:workout phase:build
- [SRC: Ultramarathon Training | 2012 | Ch.18, Ch.17.11.x formatting]

**W-10 — Pyramid run**
- Structure: jog → 3 min fast / 2 min jog / 5 min fast / 2 min jog / 7 min fast / 2 min jog → back down.
- tags: sport:running system:threshold type:workout
- [SRC: Ultramarathon Training | 2012 | Ch.6.2.7]

**W-11 — Crescendo run**
- Structure: start ≈ long-recovery pace; raise tempo every 10–15 min until 10 km race pace in final phase; jog ≥2 km after.
- tags: sport:running system:threshold type:workout
- [SRC: Ultramarathon Training | 2012 | Ch.6.2.8]

**W-12 — 24-h tempo variants (elite examples)**
- Michael Irrgang: 40 × 200 m w/ 200 m jog recovery (Zátopek-style).
- Alt: 3 × 10 km intervals on track.
- Long fartlek runs recommended to break monotony; keep some intervals to avoid "ultra foot slap."
- tags: sport:running phase:build level:elite type:workout
- [SRC: Ultramarathon Training | 2012 | Ch.8]

**W-13 — Running drills protocol**
- When: ≥1×/week (base: ideally 2×/week), before an easy jog; preceded by ≥10 min warm-up jog. 6 reps of each drill sufficient. Stop when fatigue degrades form.
- Drills: high knee skips; high knee running; sideways running; sideways running with hip twist; heel flicks; running backwards; heel running; toe running. Goal: economy of running style (more important than powerful push-off/high knee at ultra pace).
- tags: sport:running system:neuromuscular type:workout phase:base
- [SRC: Ultramarathon Training | 2012 | Ch.11]

**W-14 — Strength circuit (9 stations)**
- Setup: gym; ≥6 stations, ideally 10; 30 s per station for novices → up to 1 min. Stations: (1) pop-ups (squat jump), (2) front plank alternating arm/leg raises, (3) jumping rope, (4) leg-change jumps on bench, (5) jumps over bench, (6) push-ups (normal or knee), (7) lower back (partner-held bench extensions — do not over-extend past horizontal), (8) lower abs (wall-bar hang knee/leg raises), (9) crunches on sloping bench.

## 10. Coaching Heuristics & Decision Rules (TIER A)

**H-1 — Plateau → change the analyst**
If you've worked with a coach and performance stalls/plateaus, have your analysis done by someone not previously involved — ideally a coach experienced in coaching ultra runners.
- tags: type:heuristic type:methodology
- [SRC: Ultramarathon Training | 2012 | Ch.6.1]

**H-2 — Overtraining vs. under-stimulus (from supercompensation)**
Drop instead of gain → stimuli too close together or too intense (overtraining). No improvement → stimuli too far apart. Keep stimuli ≤4 days apart; ≥3 sessions/week to improve.
- tags: type:heuristic load system:aerobic
- [SRC: Ultramarathon Training | 2012 | Ch.6.4]

**H-3 — Set three goals**
Set a minimum goal, an ambitious goal, and a dream goal — all realistic (even the dream goal achievable under ideal conditions). Memorize solutions and goals; go to the start line and "KEEP IT SIMPLE."
- tags: type:heuristic phase:race
- [SRC: Ultramarathon Training | 2012 | Ch.15]

**H-4 — Pre-plan solutions to every foreseeable problem**
Expect: pain will come (accept it); uncertainties (self- or externally caused) will arise; but pain will stop, and circumstances are controllable. Prepare coping strategies in advance (goal-setting, targeted plan, confronting weather/body uncertainties, visualization, positive thinking). Frequent DNF-by-"head" → read sport psychology (author cites Sigurd Baumann, *Psychology in Sport*).
- tags: type:heuristic phase:race
- [SRC: Ultramarathon Training | 2012 | Ch.15]

**H-5 — Adapt the plan to life stress (don't be its slave)**
"The slave of the training plan is also at risk." If professional/social situation becomes more stressful, adapt the plan. Avoid "pot hunting" (racing too many races too close without recovery) — a cause of fatigue fractures. Not every race must be flat out; some are for meeting friends and enjoyment.
- tags: type:heuristic type:caution load
- [SRC: Ultramarathon Training | 2012 | Ch.14.4, Ch.14.3.3]

**H-6 — Test everything in training (race day = no surprises)**
Test food types/amounts, carbo-loading (may feel stiff/sluggish), shoe changes, and pacing in training before racing. For remote/untested aid stations, carry basic supplies.
- tags: type:heuristic type:nutrition phase:race
- [SRC: Ultramarathon Training | 2012 | Ch.12.5, Ch.12.6]

**H-7 — Fueling begins early**
"Start eating early on; don't wait until you feel hungry." Longer race → more necessary to fully replace calories burned.
- tags: type:heuristic type:nutrition phase:race
- [SRC: Ultramarathon Training | 2012 | Ch.12.6]

**H-8 — Drinking strategy: neither too little nor too much**
Develop a drinking strategy for very long events; perfect fluid balance is unrealistic/unnecessary except in races >24 h (lower intensity makes it possible). "Salty sweater" crusting → increase sodium.
- tags: type:heuristic type:nutrition phase:race metric:load
- [SRC: Ultramarathon Training | 2012 | Ch.12.3]

**H-9 — Shoe-store salesperson vetting**
Choose a shop with a treadmill and knowledgeable staff. Vet the assistant: ask what/how far they run. A marathon+ runner has the right experience to advise an ultrarunner; a middle-distance/spike specialist may not. A good assistant asks about your old shoes (sole wear reveals gait). Bring old shoes.
- tags: type:heuristic sport:running
- [SRC: Ultramarathon Training | 2012 | Ch.19]

**H-10 — Treadmill gait analysis before buying shoes**
Run barefoot (in socks) on treadmill at usual pace 3–4 min; analyze video; get accurate gait description and shoe recommendation. Beware: a 30-min store treadmill assessment by an inexperienced person can give a wrong result a coach could spot in 30 seconds.
- tags: type:heuristic sport:running
- [SRC: Ultramarathon Training | 2012 | Ch.19, Ch.14.9]

**H-11 — Never race in your oldest shoes**
Old shoes offer no support after a few hours → causes pain in upper tibio-fibular joint (often misdiagnosed as lateral meniscus tear). Race distance is usually longer than longest training distance → suitable footwear essential.
- tags: type:heuristic type:caution sport:running
- [SRC: Ultramarathon Training | 2012 | Ch.14.3.1]

**H-12 — Rest shoes 24–48 h between runs**
Material needs 24 (up to 48) hours to "recover"; daily runners should not wear the same shoes two days running.
- tags: type:heuristic sport:running
- [SRC: Ultramarathon Training | 2012 | Ch.19]

**H-13 — Aid-station discipline (author's Spartathlon success method)**
Treat major control points like ordinary aid stations — don't over-rest. In his successful Spartathlon, the author ran past aid stations early, then (last 3 laps of a timed race) walked past them to ensure adequate drinking. Prepare drop bags every 10 km with fixed contents (drink, salt, snack; periodic bag balm / nipple plasters / magnesium). Be self-sufficient except water.
- tags: type:heuristic phase:race type:nutrition
- [SRC: Ultramarathon Training | 2012 | Ch.20.1]

**H-14 — Timed-race aid table minimalism (to maximize distance)**
Make your own aid station "as Spartan as possible and as good as necessary": no comfortable chair/lie-down; a stool (for shoe changes) and a table are enough. Applies up to and including 24-h (no planned rest phases). 48-h+ requires different planning (planned rest).
- tags: type:heuristic phase:race sport:running
- [SRC: Ultramarathon Training | 2012 | Ch.17.11]

**H-15 — 12/6-h night races as stepping stones**
Ideally hold stepping-stone races at night to practice "antisocial hours" and biorhythm struggle, and to experiment with nutrition, breaks, pacing, equipment.
- tags: type:heuristic phase:build sport:running
- [SRC: Ultramarathon Training | 2012 | Ch.17.11]

**H-16 — Compression garments — experiment individually**
CEP socks helped the author with calf issues mid multi-day race; fellow runners wear them post-race for recovery. "Each person should experiment to see if they benefit or not."
- tags: type:heuristic type:recovery
- [SRC: Ultramarathon Training | 2012 | Ch.19]

**H-17 — Change direction & vary stimulus to fight monotony (24-h/timed)**
Race organizers reverse running direction periodically (e.g., every 12 h in one race described) for variety; in training, anything that relieves monotony is valuable.
- tags: type:heuristic phase:race
- [SRC: Ultramarathon Training | 2012 | Ch.8, Ch.20.8]

## 11. Nutrition & Fueling Guidance (Dr. Olaf Hülsmann; numbers TIER B)

**N-1 — Overriding principle**
Goal of performance-oriented nutrition: lasting, balanced energy supply covering each nutrient requirement. Everyday ultra nutrition = logistical challenge of adequate intake alongside high volume + life. Racing = maximal possible intake of energy and liquids. "Don't discount your own experience of what works for you — all bodies react differently."
- tags: type:nutrition type:philosophy general
- [SRC: Ultramarathon Training | 2012 | Ch.12 intro, 12.1]

**N-2 — Daily energy requirement by bodyweight (TIER B)**

| Activity level | kcal/kg/day |
| --- | --- |
| Low activity (sedentary, no training) | 30–35 |
| Average (1–2 h training) | 40–50 |
| High (several hours training) | 50–60 |
| Ultra race (100 km – 24 h) | 80–120 |

- Notes: metabolism slows with age (from ~50, use lower; under 30, higher possible). Multi-day races with reduced sleep can exceed these.
- Reference peaks: Kouros, Sydney–Melbourne 960 km (1985): 55,079 kcal over 5 days 5 h ≈ **163 kcal/kg per 24 h**. Tour de France mountain stages: 130–140 kcal/kg/day.
- tags: type:nutrition type:formula metric:load phase:race
- [SRC: Ultramarathon Training | 2012 | Ch.12.1]

**N-3 — Energy cost of running (TIER B)**
- Studies: **~0.89 kcal per kg per km** (assumed stable up to ~marathon distance; independent of speed, but varies with terrain/technique/condition).
- Rule of thumb: **~1 kcal per km per kg bodyweight** for estimating training demand. Exact individual cost requires lab testing.
- tags: type:nutrition type:formula metric:load
- [SRC: Ultramarathon Training | 2012 | Ch.12.1]

**N-5 — Fat-metabolism & carbohydrate strategy notes**
- Body adapts to a wide range of diets within a few days. Trained athletes oxidize more fat at a given intensity.
- Deliberate fat-metabolism training ("train low"/empty-stomach) is probably worthwhile preparing for 24-h and multi-stage races (energy deficit near-inevitable). Not conclusively shown to improve short-distance performance if carbs are available.
- HIGH-intensity work (intervals, tempo) should be done with full glycogen; inadequate provision → reduce load or stop; injury risk rises with falling blood sugar.
- In basic training, higher fat keeps daily food volume manageable; use nutritious fats (nuts, olives, fatty fish, vegetable oils). Avoid fatty meat/cold cuts even in high-demand phases (arachidonic acid → inflammatory).
- tags: type:nutrition system:fatoxidation phase:base type:heuristic
- [SRC: Ultramarathon Training | 2012 | Ch.12.1]

**N-6 — Protein timing**
Endurance requirement ~1.4 g/kg/day (up to 1.8 in normal training with balanced energy). Higher-quality protein needed if energy is significantly reduced (poultry, fish, milk, soya). Timing more important than total daily amount (see recovery, N-11).
- tags: type:nutrition type:formula
- [SRC: Ultramarathon Training | 2012 | Ch.12.1]

**N-7 — Basic snacks (list)**
Dried fruit; nuts; trail mix; muesli/fruit bars; rice cakes; yoghurt/curd cheese/soya desserts; rice pudding; fruit juice/tomato juice.
- tags: type:nutrition phase:base
- [SRC: Ultramarathon Training | 2012 | Ch.12.2]

**N-8 — Water balance & sodium (TIER B)**
- Sweat loss: typically **0.5–1 L/hour** at moderate temp/intensity; up to **2.5 L/hour** at race pace + high ambient temperature.
- Performance drops at ~1–2% body-fluid reduction; trained athletes tolerate greater losses.
- Sweat sodium ~**1 g/L**. Excess pure water → **hyponatremia** (dilution): headaches → coordination loss → coma → (extreme) death; more common in less-trained athletes told to "drink before you feel thirsty."
- Commercial sports drinks: **500–600 mg sodium/L**. In heat/high losses raise to **1 g/L** (add ~1 g cooking salt — 40% sodium — per L to a 600 mg/L drink). In training, plain water + **1–2 g cooking salt per L** (add lemon/lime for taste). High-sweat runners: carry salt capsules to ensure **500–1000 mg sodium/hour**.
- tags: type:nutrition metric:load phase:race type:caution
- [SRC: Ultramarathon Training | 2012 | Ch.12.3]

**N-9 — Micronutrients (TIER B)**
- Athletes on average do NOT eat better than sedentary people; higher intake helps but may not meet increased needs. Aim 6–8 portions fruit/veg/day (official "5-a-day" often unmet).
- Population deficits cited: 80% below folic acid recommendation; 58% of women below iron; 32% of men below zinc; iodine only ~43% of recommended.
- Increased needs: B-vitamins (energy metabolism) and trace elements lost in sweat (zinc, iron, iodine, copper). Only iron is reliably measurable in routine tests → long-distance runners should test iron regularly.
- Supplement guidance: **100–300% of recommended intake per vitamin**, plus trace elements per Table 2 below. Use only purity-tested products (contamination/doping risk).

Table 2 — Recommended trace-element dose for basic supply:

| | Zinc (mg) | Iron (mg) | Copper (mg) | Iodine (µg) | Selenium (µg) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| RDA | 10 | 14 | 1 | 150 | 55 |
| Advisable supplement/day | 5–10 | 0–15 | 0–1 | 100–200 | 30–100 |

- Supplements = long-term measure (shorten recovery, minimize illness), not instant performance boost; benefit only where correcting a deficiency.
- tags: type:nutrition type:formula type:recovery
- [SRC: Ultramarathon Training | 2012 | Ch.12.4, Table 2]

**N-10 — Carbohydrate loading (TIER B)**
- Value increases for shorter ultras (e.g., 50 km); less decisive ≥100 km (runners eat regularly). Still ensure enough for the first phase and unexpected supply breaks. Well-trained athletes use more carbs at a given intensity.
- Method: increase daily carb intake to **8–10 g/kg** for the final **3–4 days** before the race (recommendations up to 12 g/kg often cause intolerance).
- Depletion phase before loading appears unnecessary: in trained athletes, 3 days at 75% carb energy gives identical loading regardless of whether preceding 3 days were 50% or 15% carb.
- ~**3 g water stored per g glycogen** → +300 g carb ≈ +1 kg at start; weight quickly lost as glycogen is used and water liberated. MUST test in training (muscles may feel stiff/sluggish).
- tags: type:nutrition type:formula phase:taper
- [SRC: Ultramarathon Training | 2012 | Ch.12.5]

**N-11 — Race nutrition (TIER B)**
- 50 km ≈ marathon needs (water + moderate carb via gels/cola). 24-h/multi-day/multi-stage need advanced planning/testing.
- General rules: practice with different foods/amounts; prefer low-fiber, low-protein foods (easier to digest); in long races (from 100 km) match intake to calories burned — guide **0.9 kcal per km per kg bodyweight**, carb intake up to **1 g per kg per hour**; start eating early; test all foods under race conditions; carry basics for remote races.
- Fluid: realistic intake ~**0.5–1 L/hour**; commercial sports drinks can supply up to **70 g carb**.
- Readily available race foods: sports drinks 4–7% carb (maltodextrin/glucose + fructose/sucrose); carb gel (maltodextrin main → more digestible than glucose); sports bars (test at race temperature); chocolate/bars; cookies/cakes; currant buns/fruit loaf with butter/margarine; salt sticks/pretzels/crackers; baby drinks; high-energy tube-feed formulae.
- Fruit at aid stations: welcome for taste variety but low energy — average banana/apple = **15–20 g carb**; watch potassium (antagonist to sodium; sodium losses higher via sweat). Fruit's high fiber/fructose makes it less ideal as primary race food; test beforehand.
- tags: type:nutrition type:formula phase:race
- [SRC: Ultramarathon Training | 2012 | Ch.12.6]

**N-12 — Recovery nutrition (TIER B)**
- Muscle cells absorb high nutrient amounts post-exercise; carb + a small amount of protein/amino acids enhances glycogen storage. Consume **within 30 minutes** of exercise; use liquid/semi-solid (yoghurt, whey/soya drinks, buttermilk, purée/liquid baby food, or cocoa on glucose/maltodextrin + simple protein powder — dry-mix in advance, add water later).
- Amounts: protein **10–20 g** per portion first recovery phase; for max glycogen storage target extra **0.8–1 g carb per kg bodyweight**. Repeat ~2 h later or have a full meal.
- Cited outcome: 10 g protein + 8 g carb post-exercise during an intensive phase → better recovery and reduced infection vulnerability vs. placebo or pure carb.
- Weight-loss phases: delay post-exercise eating (endurance liberates fat; insulin from ingested carbs halts fatty-acid mobilization); max glycogen synthesis unnecessary at lower intensities.
- tags: type:nutrition type:recovery type:formula phase:transition
- [SRC: Ultramarathon Training | 2012 | Ch.12.7]

## 12. Recovery, Sleep & Monitoring

**R-1 — Recovery/transition phase**
2–4 weeks per annual peak; active recovery in other sports (cycling, swimming, easy jogging); nothing above 70% HRmax. (See M-2.)
- tags: type:recovery phase:transition metric:hr
- [SRC: Ultramarathon Training | 2012 | Ch.6.3, Ch.16]

**R-2 — Stretching timing & dosing**
Stretch AFTER training (conclusion), not before (pre-load stretching counterproductive as muscles/tendons contract under load; stretching opposes this). Avoid extensive stretching immediately after intensive workouts (worsens microtears). Sessions ≤15 min (10 usually enough); focus leg muscles (≥3 leg + 3 other-body exercises); hold ≥15 s per extremity (ideally 20–30 s); no jerky/bouncing movement; second stretch may be held longer but never painful. Supplementary flexibility/gymnastics ≥1×/week (ideally 2×). If prior stretching has been positive/neutral, continue gently — but never combine warm-up + extensive stretching + racing.
- tags: type:recovery sport:running type:heuristic
- [SRC: Ultramarathon Training | 2012 | Ch.9]

**R-3 — Strength/stability as injury prevention**
Regular strength training important with very high mileage to avoid one-sided-loading injuries and postural misalignment. ≥1 weekly full-body strength session for runners who previously only ran. "20–30 minutes per week are all it takes." Wobble board recommended for ankle proprioception (use during daily routine, e.g., brushing teeth). Search "proprioception" / "strength training for runners" for more.
- tags: type:recovery sport:strength system:neuromuscular type:heuristic
- [SRC: Ultramarathon Training | 2012 | Ch.10]

**R-4 — Sleep in high-load blocks**
During high-mileage weeks (multi-day prep) get enough sleep — author reports being "flat" the following week after inserting a high-mileage week into normal life. Multi-day plans schedule Sunday rest.
- tags: type:recovery load phase:build type:heuristic
- [SRC: Ultramarathon Training | 2012 | Ch.17.13]

**R-5 — Monitoring toolset**
Training diary/log (now computerized); resting HR (should fall with fitness); HR at fixed speed/conditions over time; regular threshold retests (Conconi regularly; spiro ~yearly). Recommended device features for ultrarunners: readable display in rain/dark; long battery/measuring time; ECG-accurate data; adjustable HR zones; usable with gloves; coded transmitter; PC analysis; distance measurement + GPS; (elite) training planning/management. Note: smartphone apps often unsuitable for ultra due to battery life. Software cited: SportTracks (free v2; ~€35 v3).
- tags: metric:hr metric:hrv metric:load type:recovery type:methodology
- [SRC: Ultramarathon Training | 2012 | Ch.17.3, Ch.7, Ch.19]

## 13. Adaptations by Athlete Type / Level / Sport

**AD-1 — Level-based plan progression (50 km ladder)**
Same distance, four ability tiers with faster paces and higher volume as marathon fitness rises: entry (marathon ~5:00, race at extensive pace) → sub-5:00 (4:00) → sub-4:30 (3:30–3:40) → sub-4:00 (3:15, race at THRESHOLD like a marathon). The sub-4:00 plan is the qualitative break: the race itself is run at aerobic/anaerobic threshold, not extensive pace.
- tags: sport:running level:beginner level:intermediate level:advanced type:plan
- [SRC: Ultramarathon Training | 2012 | Ch.17.5–17.8]

**AD-2 — Beginner vs. experienced projections (marathon → 100 km)**
- Complete beginner: marathon PR × 3.
- Experienced/elite first 100 km: marathon PR × 2.7–2.8.
- Experienced elite: marathon best × 2.5–2.7.
- tags: sport:running level:beginner level:advanced level:elite type:formula
- [SRC: Ultramarathon Training | 2012 | Ch.7.3]

**AD-3 — Masters / senior runners**
Timed races (6/12/24-h) let senior runners continue careers without fear of sweeper buses/cut-offs; every runner is classified regardless of distance. Good demographic fit for aging Western populations.
- tags: level:masters phase:race sport:running
- [SRC: Ultramarathon Training | 2012 | Ch.17.11]

**AD-4 — Women — orthopedic tendencies (from Dr. Göbel)**
Women commonly knock-kneed → more prone to knee-cap lateralization and patellofemoral/arthritis pain (behind kneecap); vastus medialis relatively underweight. Emphasize medial quadriceps (vastus medialis) strengthening. Men's slight bandy-legged tendency partially compensates. Side-stitch reported more in women (also during menstruation → possible hormonal cause). GI disorders more frequent in women and younger athletes.
- tags: level:masters sport:running type:physiology general
- [SRC: Ultramarathon Training | 2012 | Ch.14.3.1, 14.3.3, 14.7, Ch.13.2]

**AD-5 — Trail vs. road/track**
Trail/mountain requires specific prep only if uneven AND hilly (see M-10). Downhill is the limiter (PH-5). Otherwise flat-course training principles apply. Trail shoes are a recommended 4th shoe (see EQ-4).
- tags: sport:running type:methodology
- [SRC: Ultramarathon Training | 2012 | Ch.18, Ch.19]

## 14. Common Mistakes, Contraindications & Cautions

**C-1 — Overtraining / "pot hunting"** — racing too many races too close without recovery → fatigue fractures (foot, knee). Don't be a "slave of the training plan." (See H-5.)
- tags: type:caution load
- [SRC: Ultramarathon Training | 2012 | Ch.14.4]

**C-2 — Racing on injury / ignoring early symptoms** — ignoring first fatigue-fracture pain (creeping pain during exercise) to fulfill the plan makes consequences longer-lasting. Fatigue fractures require long breaks (rest → cycling/aqua jogging).
- tags: type:caution sport:running
- [SRC: Ultramarathon Training | 2012 | Ch.14.4]

**C-3 — Pre-load stretching + racing** — avoid warm-up + extensive stretching + race combination at all costs (see R-2).
- tags: type:caution sport:running
- [SRC: Ultramarathon Training | 2012 | Ch.9]

**C-4 — Hyponatremia from over-drinking plain water** — potentially fatal; prevent with appropriate drinking + sodium (see N-8).
- tags: type:caution type:nutrition phase:race
- [SRC: Ultramarathon Training | 2012 | Ch.12.3]

**C-5 — GI disorders** — up to 80% of long-distance athletes affected; up to 50% exercise-induced diarrhea; worse with dehydration (80% of runners losing >4% bodyweight developed GI symptoms). Triggers: exercise intensity >70% VO2max; oxygen deficiency (blood flow reduced >70%); mechanical trauma; incorrect diet; physical/mental stress; medication (aspirin, NSAIDs, antibiotics, doping). Prevention: avoid high-calorie/fatty foods ≥3 h before training/racing; avoid rapid increases in intensity/duration; avoid excessive anaerobic training; personalized plans; manage stress. GI bleeding usually harmless/needs no management; proton-pump inhibitor prophylaxis can reduce it (Spartathlon study: less occult blood with pantoprazole, 10% vs 71%). Anemia/iron deficiency/occult blood loss common in long-distance running — investigate fatigue/performance decline for anemia.
- tags: type:caution type:physiology phase:race general
- [SRC: Ultramarathon Training | 2012 | Ch.13 (Dr. Hinze)]

**C-6 — Exercise with GI disease (Dr. Hinze rules)** — Gastric/duodenal ulcers: no racing in active stage; exercise OK in inactive stage. Intestinal inflammation / ulcerative colitis / Crohn's: no racing in active phases; moderate exercise (recovery/basic endurance) in inactive phases. Intestinal ischemia (rare, usually self-resolving): avoid physical exercise ~4 weeks in acute phase; ensure adequate fluid intake and monitor intensity/length.
- tags: type:caution type:physiology
- [SRC: Ultramarathon Training | 2012 | Ch.13.9, 13.8]

**C-7 — Cortisone injections into tendon** — for Achilles tendinopathy, cortisone should only be given as a tablet, never as an injection (injection can further damage the tendon). (Dr. Göbel.)
- tags: type:caution sport:running
- [SRC: Ultramarathon Training | 2012 | Ch.14.5]

**C-8 — Barefoot running dosing** — good for proprioceptive training in SMALL doses (beach, grass infield). Ultrarunners must remember what happens to a tired body after 8–12+ h on a long course; Dr. Göbel personally runs supported shoes at week's end (after long standing at work), unsupported on Mondays.
- tags: type:caution sport:running
- [SRC: Ultramarathon Training | 2012 | Ch.14.9]

**C-9 — Shoe selection cautions** — overpronation/supination correction shoes only for very slight malposition; for more severe, see orthopedic specialist/podiatrist (who should ask about old shoes, observe gait on treadmill, prescribe individually adapted orthotics — get a diagnosis, not just a prescription). Bandy-legged runners tend to over-pronate → an inexperienced 30-min store gait analysis may be completely wrong.
- tags: type:caution sport:running
- [SRC: Ultramarathon Training | 2012 | Ch.19, Ch.14.9]

**C-10 — Clothing/kit mismatch** — a top-quality breathable running top under a non-breathable jacket traps sweat. Functional (wind/water-resistant + breathable) clothing essential for workouts >3 h; use the onion (layering) principle. Don't lace shoes too tightly / avoid narrow shoes with smooth synthetic (compression) socks → rubbing → shin-splint-type issues.
- tags: type:caution sport:running
- [SRC: Ultramarathon Training | 2012 | Ch.19, Ch.14.6]

**C-11 — "Run them in" myth** — Ignore advice that new shoes need breaking in ("it's rubbish!"). Running shoes should feel comfortable straight away, no pressure points.
- tags: type:caution sport:running
- [SRC: Ultramarathon Training | 2012 | Ch.19]

**C-12 — Orthopedic injury summary (Dr. Göbel, frequency order)**
30–50% of runners injured ~once/year; distribution ~62% legs, ~18% lower back, ~20% other. Clinic order of frequency: (1) shoulder (rotator cuff — often from shortened chest muscles, cold-weather sleeveless runs, unaccustomed hand-bottle carrying); (2) lower back/hip (hollow back at L5–sacrum; hip-flexor/quad shortening; weak abs/glutes/hip stabilizers; inadequately cushioned shoes); (3) knee (ligament overload, meniscus overload/tears, arthritis — medial meniscus vulnerable to feet-turned-out gait; strengthen vastus medialis); (4) fatigue fractures; (5) Achilles tendon; (6) periostitis/shin splints (unsuitable shoe hardness/support, sudden mileage increase, steep downhill; treat early to avoid chronic compartment syndrome); (7) dorsal heel spur; (8) plantar heel spur / plantar fasciitis / foot-muscle overload (overloaded longitudinal arch; both over-pronators AND high-arched runners vulnerable; also runners who train fatigued). Prevention theme: eliminate muscular imbalance via targeted physiotherapy; correct footwear; adequate recovery.
- tags: type:caution type:physiology sport:running general
- [SRC: Ultramarathon Training | 2012 | Ch.14 (Dr. Göbel)]

## 15. Formulas & Calculations (TIER B)

**F-1 — Marathon → 50 km target**
50 km time ≈ marathon time + 10 km best time.
- tags: type:formula sport:running
- [SRC: Ultramarathon Training | 2012 | Ch.7.3]

**F-2 — Marathon → 100 km target**
- Complete beginner: marathon PR × 3.
- Experienced/elite, first 100 km: marathon PR × 2.7–2.8.
- Experienced elite: marathon best × 2.5–2.7.
- tags: type:formula sport:running level:beginner level:elite
- [SRC: Ultramarathon Training | 2012 | Ch.7.3]

**F-3 — 100 km PR → projected 24-h distance (treat with great care)**
- 100 km sub-8:00 → over 230 km
- 100 km sub-9:00 → over 210 km
- 100 km sub-10:00 → over 180 km
- 100 km sub-12:00 → over 150 km
- Caveat: mental side grows with distance; a fast marathoner may not transfer basic speed to long distances.
- tags: type:formula sport:running phase:race type:caution
- [SRC: Ultramarathon Training | 2012 | Ch.7.3]

**F-4 — HRmax → training percentages**
Set measured HRmax (T-4) = 100% loading; derive % increments for plans. Preferred over pace (accounts for daily terrain/temp/weather variance).
- tags: type:formula metric:hr
- [SRC: Ultramarathon Training | 2012 | Ch.7.2.2]

**F-5 — Energy cost of running**
Study value ≈ 0.89 kcal/kg/km; rule of thumb ≈ 1 kcal/kg/km. Race-fueling guide 0.9 kcal/km/kg; carb up to 1 g/kg/hour. (Cross-ref N-3, N-11.)
- tags: type:formula type:nutrition metric:load
- [SRC: Ultramarathon Training | 2012 | Ch.12.1, 12.6]

**F-6 — Glycogen–water storage**
~3 g water stored per 1 g glycogen (→ ~+1 kg per +300 g stored carb). Carbo-load: 8–10 g/kg/day for final 3–4 days.
- tags: type:formula type:nutrition phase:taper
- [SRC: Ultramarathon Training | 2012 | Ch.12.5]

**F-7 — Sodium replacement math**
Sweat sodium ~1 g/L. To raise a 600 mg/L sports drink to 1 g/L, add ~1 g cooking salt/L (salt is 40% sodium). Training water: 1–2 g cooking salt/L. High-sweat target: 500–1000 mg sodium/hour (salt capsules).
- tags: type:formula type:nutrition
- [SRC: Ultramarathon Training | 2012 | Ch.12.3]

**F-8 — Conconi deflection identifies threshold**
Plot HR vs speed; increase 0.5 km/h per 200 m; the deflection (curve bend) = aerobic/anaerobic threshold (worked example 169 bpm). Rising deflection speed on retest = progress.
- tags: type:formula system:threshold metric:hr
- [SRC: Ultramarathon Training | 2012 | Ch.7.1.3]

**F-9 — Lactate test ramp**
Increase pace 0.5 m/s every 5 min; 4 mmol/l ≈ threshold reference (individual variation; measure per runner).
- tags: type:formula system:lactate
- [SRC: Ultramarathon Training | 2012 | Ch.7.1.1]
